What Makes an Infographic Great
Before we start looking at examples, let's discuss what exactly separates a strong infographic from a forgettable one. There are six aspects that make the difference.
Data Clarity
Infographics that visualize data must offer full clarity for the viewer. The numbers should be easy to read at a glance, with one idea per chart, labels next to the data they describe and no mental math for the reader. Name the one question your chart answers before you build it. If a chart needs a paragraph to explain it, split it into two charts or drop the secondary variable.
Alejandra Mariscalez, Design Production Manager at Visme, adds: "One of the most common issues I see in infographics is the inclusion of too much text."
Visual Hierarchy
The hierarchy starts at the header which is the main focus. Everything below it must adhere to a clear flow. Use size, color and spacing to organize the information: set your headline figure larger than anything around it, give supporting detail a visibly smaller size, and use white space to group related points so the eye moves in blocks rather than wandering. When everything is bold, nothing is.
Monfa Cabrera, Creative Director at Visme: "Design should actively guide the user through the information, rather than leaving them to decipher a maze of data."
Narrative Structure
A strong infographic moves in a clear narrative order: open with a question, work through the answer and land a point. For example, consider customer churn. The header asks why a quarter of new customers leave inside 90 days, the middle walks through the three drop-off points in the order they happen, and the footer gives the one fix that addresses all three. Same data with no order, and you have six unrelated statistics on a page.
Design Originality
The best examples look unique even when created with a template or AI. A distinct layout, palette or concept is what gets an infographic saved and passed along. Visual metaphors help: annotate an everyday object like a technical diagram, or explain a dry business model through an unlikely comparison. Both borrow a format built for objective information and aim it somewhere unexpected.
Accessibility
Following accessibility guidelines doesn't only serve readers with limitations; it makes your infographic better for everyone. Check your contrast ratios and never carry meaning in color alone, since WCAG 2.2 AA requires 4.5:1 for body text and 3:1 for large type.
Write alt text that states the finding rather than the chart type. "Support dropped 12 points between 2023 and 2026" tells a screen reader user something. "Bar chart of survey results" doesn't. Low-contrast text remains the most common accessibility failure on the web, appearing on 83.9% of the top 1 million home pages.
Daniela Verduga, Creative Graphic Designer at Visme: "The alt text should provide meaningful context instead of just listing the elements in the design."
Shareability
A great infographic is repurposeable, and its shape shouldn't limit how it gets distributed. A long vertical infographic can be split into smaller chunks for social media or newsletters. Design in self-contained sections from the start so each block holds a complete idea and reads on its own once separated. Inside the editor, use Visme's AI Resize Tool to convert the design into a Pinterest pin, a LinkedIn carousel or a newsletter image.
Chelse Hensley, Social Media Strategist at Visme: "You can break up pieces of the infographic and restructure them into five or more swipeable placements on Instagram or LinkedIn."
Don’t forget that timing matters as much as format. Check the best times to post on Instagram and other social channels.

Infographic Design Trends for 2026-2027
Six shifts are shaping how infographics get made and shared right now.
1. Motion, scroll and interactive formats. Static infographics are increasingly sharing space with scrollytelling, animated charts and click-to-explore graphics. Newsroom data teams at the New York Times, South China Morning Post, Reuters and The Guardian publish this way constantly.
2. AI-assisted design. You've surely noticed the sheer number of AI-made infographics out there. That trend isn't going away. 88% now use AI design tools in some capacity, yet only 18% say those tools reduced their need for designers. What they hand over is repeatable work: image editing (45%), social graphics (36%) and ad variations (35%).
Jack Oddy, Managing Director of Soap Media, told Clutch: "It's not that design work is resistant to automation; what's resistant is the judgment."
Shlomi Ron, founder of the Visual Storytelling Institute, adds a warning: "Always be validating the data the AI gives you."
3. Accessibility as a legal requirement. The European Accessibility Act became enforceable on 28 June 2025, covering any business selling to EU consumers wherever it's based.
This hits infographics because of the trend above. A scrollytelling piece or embedded interactive chart is a web page, not an image, so it carries the same requirements as the rest of the site: keyboard navigation, screen reader labels, contrast ratios and accessible fonts.
4. The shift from SEO to AEO. Infographics used to earn their keep through links and image search rankings. Now the real test is whether an answer engine can read the graphic at all. Otterly.ai tested this in April 2026 and found that the clearest way to help AI search "see" an infographic is to write the key numbers out as body copy around the graphic, then add alt text that states the finding directly.
One caveat: this is current crawler behavior, not a permanent limit. Agentic browsing already renders pages and reads images, so writing the text out costs nothing either way.
5. Hand-drawn and illustrated styles. Custom illustration and textured, handmade looks are returning as a way to stand apart from work that reads as machine-made, a shift Creative Bloq calls anti-AI crafting.
Adriana Danaila, professional infographic illustrator: "Hand-drawn infographics work best when a brand wants to connect on a human level, not just push information."
Category #1: Marketing & Advertising Infographics
Marketing is where most infographics end up, which means the bar is higher here. These examples work as ads, lead magnets, LinkedIn posts and trend roundups, not just as illustrations dropped into a blog post.
Before the examples, here's a quick primer on building infographics for marketing.
1. The Marketing “Lag Effect”
The marketing lag effect is the delay between doing the work and seeing results, and Pierre Herubel maps it across three phases: ideation and production, early traction and real impact. Then he speaks to founders and early-stage marketers who expect returns within a week of launching.
2. 12 Ways to Get More Blog Subscribers
HubSpot packs a dozen subscriber-growth tactics into a branded infographic built for bloggers and content marketers. The numbered layout helps with the visual hierarchy and skimming effect. They used an anatomy-style visual in the center to show exactly where the tactics make a difference.
The payoff of more subscribers is real: Did you know that email still returns $36 for every $1 spent? To use these tactics, pick two or three and test them one at a time instead of rolling out all twelve.
3. A Legacy of First Steps
This infographic is actually a print advertisement for the film The Fantastic Four. The concept of the infographic was to frame the 2025 release against the franchise's legacy that traces back to the team's 1961 comic debut. The visual quality is reminiscent of comic books in the 60’s with a more modern feel. This print infographic ran in the Straits Times, reaching film fans and casual readers at the same time.
4. Head Straightener
Nigerian agency SO&U turned an ordinary Dunlop slipper into a mock technical diagram for a Mother's Day ad, tagging its "landing area," "wings," and "handle" alongside a deadpan usage-and-warning spec sheet.
The infographic format usually promises objective information, so wrapping it around a nostalgic household object that moms also used to threaten kids to obey plays the layout against the punchline. The funniest part about this ad is that it would apply not only in Nigeria but also in all of South America.
5. 10 Must-Know Facts About Out-Of-Home
This infographic by Vistar Media is a classic blog related design that collects the information published in the text. The information is ten stats about out-of-home advertising, including future projections about the industry. For example, OOH returns $5.97 for every $1 spent, and 82% of adults remember the ads. So, if your infographic is mostly statistics, one number per block keeps it readable.
