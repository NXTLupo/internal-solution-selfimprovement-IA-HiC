Employee Training: The Introductory Guide for Modern Teams
The skills employees need are constantly changing.
New technologies, evolving roles and shifting business priorities mean organizations need to keep developing their people.
Companies are investing heavily in that effort. Research from Josh Bersin shows that corporate learning spending grew by double digits over two years, reaching more than $1,400 per employee annually on average.
But spending more doesn't automatically produce better training. HR and L&D teams still need to identify the right skills gaps, choose suitable training methods, create useful learning content and measure whether employees are actually improving.
This guide covers the entire process. We’ll look at how to identify training needs, create an employee training plan, choose the right methods and delivery formats, develop training materials and measure effectiveness. You’ll also find examples, templates and practical resources you can use to build or improve your own employee training program.

What Is Training and Development?
Training and development is the process of building the knowledge, skills and capabilities employees need for their current job and future growth. It sits within the broader Learning and Development (L&D) function, which connects employee learning to company goals.
L&D teams strategize, plan, create, deliver and track training programs. They also support career progression and manage learning technology. L&D contributes to company culture, but leadership, HR and managers shape it together.
What Is the Difference Between Training and Development?
Training and development are split into two related but distinct functions, each with its own type of program.
- Training addresses the knowledge or skills employees need to perform their current tasks or roles.
- Development takes a longer-term view, helping employees build capabilities for broader responsibilities, career progression and future roles.

Why Is Training and Development Important?
Training and development affect not only the individuals in an organization but also the organization as a whole. Here are five data-backed reasons why training and development are worth your time and budget.
- Improved job performance: Training gives employees the skills they need to do their jobs well and adapt to the situation at hand. Lighthouse Research & Advisory's 2024 study states that workers with proper training are 86% more likely to describe themselves as adaptable to change.
- Increased job satisfaction: Employees want to see a path forward, and they'll leave if they don't. According to Randstad's 2025 Workmonitor, 41% of workers would quit a job that didn't offer them development opportunities to future-proof their career, up from 29% the year before.
- Improved quality of work: Training can give employees better techniques and methods for their job, and many HR professionals see that reflected in performance. According to HR.com's Future of Upskilling and Employee Learning 2025 report, 68% agree that upskilling leads to a substantial improvement in job performance.
- Competitive advantage: Skill gaps slow a business down, whether it's slower product launches, more errors or work that has to be redone. Training closes those gaps. According to TalentLMS's 2026 L&D Benchmark Report, 42% of HR managers say their organization is grappling with a skills gap.
- Employee growth: Training and development programs give employees room to grow, both in their current role and toward what comes next. HR.com's Future of Upskilling and Employee Learning 2025 report finds that 54% of HR professionals say upskilling has improved employees' career mobility.

Types of Employee Training Programs
Training programs are grouped into categories that align with a particular purpose, like onboarding, compliance or skills development.
Below, we break down seven common types of employee training and what each should cover.
We also suggest methods, materials and delivery formats that tend to fit best for each type. Take these suggestions as a starting point; you can then adjust the details to your team's reality.
Onboarding Training for New Hires
Onboarding training covers all the knowledge a new employee needs to do their job. This includes foundational knowledge about their role, information about company policies and the specific skills they’ll need on a day-to-day basis.
This type of training needs to cover a lot of ground, including topics like:
- Company and role orientation: Mission, values, org structure, who's on their team and who to go to for what.
- Policies and procedures: The employee handbook, how PTO works, how performance gets reviewed, how to raise a concern.
- Job-specific skills and tools: The systems, software and workflows they'll use every day, ideally walked through hands-on rather than just handed a manual.
- Products and services: What the company actually sells and how their role fits into that, especially important for customer-facing hires.
- Team introductions and early relationships: Meeting the people they'll actually work with, not just seeing a name on an org chart.
How to Deliver It
Compliance Training
Compliance training covers what employees need to know to meet legal, regulatory, policy and risk requirements. The specific topics depend on the nature of the organization, where it's located, what the work entails and any internal policies or contractual obligations tied to the role.
According to SHRM, no single federal law covers every employer. That’s why specific rules kick in based on your industry, location and workforce.
These are some common compliance training topics that are sometimes complete programs or part of others, like onboarding or sensitivity training.
- Anti-harassment and anti-discrimination training: Covers what counts as harassment or discrimination under the law, how to spot it and how to report it.
- Data privacy and protection training: Teaches employees how to handle personal and company data in compliance with privacy regulations.
- Cybersecurity awareness training: Covers common threats like phishing and malware and what to do when there’s an imminent risk.
- Workplace health and safety training: Required under OSHA and similar regulations for employees exposed to job-site hazards, covering the specific protocols for their industry, including manual handling training for physical roles.
- Diversity, equity and inclusion training: Focuses on building an inclusive workplace and meeting anti-discrimination requirements.
- Ethics and code of conduct training: Covers company values, conflicts of interest and how to make the right call in gray-area situations.
- Workplace violence prevention training: Teaches employees how to spot warning signs and what steps to take if an incident happens.
- Intellectual property training: Explains how to handle patents, trademarks, copyrights and trade secrets without exposing the company to risk.
- HIPAA training: Required for healthcare organizations, covering how to protect patient information and stay compliant with federal law.
How to Deliver It
Skills Training
Skills training refers to any program designed to teach a specific skill. Most skills fall into one of three categories:
- Technical (hard) skills training: Teaches the specific tools, processes or physical tasks someone needs to do their job, like running a machine, navigating a CRM or following proper lifting technique.
- Soft skills training: Covers communication, collaboration, critical thinking and other interpersonal skills that shape how well someone works with a team or a customer. See our guide to soft skills training for a full breakdown.
- AI skills training: Teaches employees how to use AI tools effectively and responsibly in their role, from prompting to knowing when not to rely on them. That creates a clear training gap for many organizations.s. According to a July 2026 report from The Conference Board, 55% of workers regularly use AI at work, but only 1 in 3 have received employer-provided AI training in the past six months.
How to Deliver It
Product Knowledge Training
Product knowledge training teaches employees the ins and outs of your company's own product: what it does, how it works and how to talk about it. This is internal-facing and built for your team.
It’s different from training on the tools employees use to do their jobs, such as an internal wiki or project management software. That falls under technical skills training.
Most of the product knowledge training is directed at customer-facing teams, but it isn't limited to them:
- Sales teams need it to explain value, handle objections and close deals with confidence.
- Support and customer success teams need the technical depth to resolve issues without escalating everything.
- Marketing needs it to write accurate, compelling content for blogs and social media.
