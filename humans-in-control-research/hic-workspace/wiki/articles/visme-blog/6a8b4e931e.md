# Training & Development: Methods and Delivery Formats

Source: [visme_blog](https://visme.co/blog/training-and-development/)

Training & Development: Methods and Delivery Formats
There’s no one-size-fits-all approach to employee training.
The right method depends on what employees need to learn, where the training takes place and how they’ll apply the information to their work.
A warehouse team learning safety procedures may need hands-on practice, while a remote sales team might benefit from self-paced modules combined with live coaching.
Hybrid teams may need a mix of formats to provide an effective learning experience for everyone.
That’s why organizations often invest in different approaches. In CIPD’s Learning at Work 2023 survey, UK organizations reported funding in-house development programs (43%), mentoring (42%) and external conferences and workshops (41%).
In this article, we’ll break down nine common training methods and delivery formats, where each works best and the trade-offs to consider. You’ll also see real-world examples to help you choose the right approach for your training program.

Why is it Important to Choose the Right Training Method and Format?
- Keeps employees engaged: A training format that fits the subject matter and employees’ work environment makes it easier to participate. Interactive sessions may suit topics that require discussion and practice, while shorter self-paced modules can work better when employees need to learn around their schedules.
- Improves retention and application: How employees learn can affect how well they remember and use the information. Hands-on training gives employees a chance to practice complex tasks, while microlearning and job aids can reinforce knowledge when they need it later.
- Makes training easier to access: Your workforce may include employees in different locations, time zones and work environments. Virtual, hybrid and self-paced formats can make training available without requiring everyone to be in the same place at the same time.
- Makes better use of your training budget: According to ATD’s 2025 State of the Industry report, the average cost per learning hour reached $165 in 2024, up 34% from $123 in 2023. Choosing the right format helps you spend those hours where they add the most value rather than defaulting to costly or time-intensive delivery methods.
- Connects training to the outcome you want: The method should ultimately reflect what employees need to do after training. If the objective is knowledge recall, self-paced learning may be enough. If employees need to demonstrate a skill, make decisions or handle real-world situations, practice, coaching or simulations may be more appropriate.
Employee Training Methods and Delivery Formats at a Glance
What is a training method, and how does it differ from the delivery format?
- The training method is the instructional approach used to teach the content: instructor-led, blended, mentorship or microlearning.
- Delivery Format is the setting in which the method takes place: live in person, live virtual, or self-paced online.
Do these directly correlate with the training type your program aligns with? Not necessarily. For example, an onboarding training program for a warehouse job will need in-person training methods, while a remote worker will need virtual sessions.
So what do training methods and delivery formats align with? That would be your training goals, learning objectives and your employee’s day-to-day reality.

Effective Methods and Delivery Formats For Employee Training and Development Programs
Now we’ll discuss the options in detail so you can get a better idea of what’s possible.
Many programs combine two or more training methods and delivery formats in order to reach trainees at different points and in different situations. Create the combination that suits your employees’ realities while filling the skill gaps you’ve identified.
Blended Learning
Blended learning is one of the most widely used training styles.
According to ATD’s research report, Blended Learning: Leverage Flexibility to Maximize Results, 71% of respondents say that at least half of their training is blended.
As the name implies, “blended” refers to the fact that this training method combines several styles of learning. Diana L. Howles, CEO and co-founder of Howles Associates, describes blended learning as combining "synchronous (live online) training with asynchronous (on-demand components) into a comprehensive learning program."
This type of training works well when you need employees to learn many things or when the topic is complex. The combination will depend on what we’ve discussed before: the content, the goals and the situation.
For example, helicopter pilots transitioning to new aircraft are trained through blended programs that combine individual and instructor-led technical learning with simulator time to practice their skills and real-life flying with a pilot who teaches them skills as they are needed on the job.
Best delivery format
Blended learning doesn't have one best format. The combination depends entirely on the skill, the audience and how much practice they need before they're on their own.
For technical skills, a self-paced module can lay the foundation, followed by a live practice or simulator session to apply them. For skills that require judgment, a live discussion session can come first, followed by self-paced material that employees can return to on their own.
The mix can take almost any shape. For instance, a customer service team might pair a live role-play session with short microlearning refreshers sent by email afterward. A new software rollout might pair a self-paced walkthrough video with a live Q&A session to cover questions the video couldn't anticipate.
How to measure success
Track completion and any assessment scores for the self-paced methods separately from the live ones. If the self-paced score is strong but performance in the live session is weak, the material contains content that the trainee can't yet apply. If it's the other way around, the self-paced content didn't hold up without a facilitator explaining it.
Don't average the two into one pass-or-fail score. Each one is telling you something different about where the program is succeeding or failing.

Hybrid Learning
Blended and hybrid are similar terms and can seem interchangeable, but actually aren’t.
Jennifer Lindsay-Finan, of InSync Training, shares a clear definition that will help you remember the difference: "Blended learning is about how the content is delivered. Hybrid learning is about where the participants are learning."
Hybrid learning combines online and in-person training in the same session. It works well when some employees need to join in person and others can't, but the session still needs to happen live for everyone.
Best delivery format
Trainers run these sessions with some people in the room and others joining through a virtual meeting. The setup only works if remote participants have equal footing.
You can achieve that by designing content, materials and delivery formats that work for both audiences simultaneously. For example, an interactive presentation that can be shared via a link and presented in the room will work well.
Karen Vieth, President and CEO of InSync Training, recommends going a step further:
"Have every individual log on to a personal device, no matter how they are participating." That way, in-person attendees use the same polls, chat and breakout tools as the remote group.
How to measure success
Track attendance and participation for the in-person and remote groups in two separate lists. That way, you can see how effective each format is and how to align it better next time. For knowledge assessments, a quick pulse check at the end, using the same two or three questions for both groups, can also help catch an engagement gap.

Microlearning
Microlearning breaks training into short pieces, each covering a single topic. Instead of a 45-minute course, an employee might get a 2-minute video, a single email, or a short walkthrough tied to the exact moment they need it. This method works because it aligns with the amount of time employees actually have available for learning. Busy schedules can’t always fit in a two-hour training session in the middle of the day.
