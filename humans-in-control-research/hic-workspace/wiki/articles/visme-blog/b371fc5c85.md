# Employee Onboarding Training: A Complete Guide for HR and L&D Teams

Source: [visme_blog](https://visme.co/blog/employee-onboarding-training/)

Employee Onboarding Training: A Complete Guide for HR and L&D Teams
Hiring someone gets them through the door. Onboarding training gets them ready to do the job.
A new employee has a lot to learn: how their role works, which systems and tools to use, what processes to follow, who to work with and what good performance looks like. And they need to learn it without being overwhelmed with everything in their first few days.
That’s why solid employee onboarding training is critical. Unlike the administrative side of onboarding, such as completing paperwork, setting up payroll and enrolling in benefits, onboarding training focuses on the knowledge and skills employees need to become confident and productive in their roles.
In this guide, you’ll learn what employee onboarding training should include, the five stages of the process and how to build a program that takes new hires from their first day to working independently.

What is Employee Onboarding Training?
Employee onboarding training is the structured instruction a new hire receives to learn how to do their job effectively. It covers the tools, systems, processes, skills and workplace expectations they need to get started and gradually work more independently.
It sits inside the larger onboarding process, alongside administrative activities such as HR paperwork, benefits enrollment, compliance, information and IT setup.
New hire training usually covers a mix of:
- Role-specific training: The skills, workflows and responsibilities employees need to perform their jobs.
- Systems and tools training: How to use the software, platforms and processes required for day-to-day work.
- Safety training: Workplace safety procedures relevant to the role, such as equipment operation, manual handling or chemical safety.
- Culture and expectations training: How the organization and team work, including expected behaviors, communication practices and performance standards.
Why Employee Onboarding Training Is Important
Structured onboarding training gives new hires a clearer path from learning the basics to performing their role independently.
Without that structure, employees are more likely to rely on coworkers for information, misunderstand priorities or spend longer figuring out processes that could have been taught upfront.
When training is lacking substance, managers absorb the gap. They have to field more repeat questions, correct more errors and spend more time coaching than a documented program would require. That’s time that should go toward the new hire's actual work.
Aside from the work-related benefits of good onboarding, it also affects how employees feel about their new role. According to a2026 BambooHR and TalentLMS report, 39% of employees have second thoughts about their new job during onboarding, and a third walk away disappointed.
Amy Hirsh Robinson, Principal at Interchange Consulting Group, frames onboarding as a retention moment more than a paperwork step. In an SHRM interview, she said:
"Onboarding is a magic moment when new employees decide to stay engaged or become disengaged."
Training is one part of creating an onboarding experience that helps new hires feel prepared and supported.
The Stages of Employee Onboarding Training
Employee onboarding training happens in five stages, starting before a new hire's first day and continuing well past their first week. We like using the following terms to remember and always touch on every stage when creating a new onboarding training program: Prepare, Orient, Teach, Practice and Reinforce.
- Prepare: Pre-boarding training. At this stage, send the new hire self-paced material, such as a welcome video or a first look at the tools and systems they'll use, so they already know what to expect when they walk in on day one.
- Orient: Orientation training. This stage covers day one and the first week on the job. The content and materials will include compliance and policy training, as well as an introduction to company culture, team mechanics and expectations.
- Teach: Role-specific training. This is the core skill-building phase for the role the employee was hired for. It covers exactly how their job is done and what tools and processes they need to know.
- Practice: Applied, on-the-job training. This is where new hires perform the job, with supervision and feedback. As the employee works on real tasks, a manager or mentor coaches and corrects them along the way. The scope of this type of training depends on the nature of the work.
- Reinforce: Ongoing training. As formal onboarding training winds down, employees will need regular refreshers and skill-building sessions throughout the first few months.
So when does onboarding training end? Many onboarding programs use the first 90 days as a structured ramp-up period, but the right timeline depends on the role's complexity and how long employees typically need to reach proficiency.
Talya N. Bauer, PhD, Cameron Professor of Management at Portland State University and author of the SHRM Foundation's onboarding guide, argues that these stages are highly beneficial; therefore, your content, materials and methods should aim for this goal.
"The faster new hires feel welcome and prepared for their jobs, the faster they will be able to successfully contribute to the firm's mission."
How to Build an Employee Onboarding Training Program
The first installment of the complete guide to employee training covers the process of creating a training program. But each type of training program has its own unique characteristics, even if the mechanics are essentially the same.
An onboarding program, for example, must align with the stages we mentioned earlier. The content and materials have to map to a new hire's first days, weeks and months, as well as the needs of their specific role.
Let’s get going.
Step 1: Lay Out Your Onboarding Objectives
Every onboarding training program has the same main objective: to welcome, train and set up a new employee for success in their role. Then, each stage in the process also has a specific objective:
- To prepare the new employee for their first day.
- To orient the employee to the facilities, their role and their team.
- To teach them how to do their job and what tools they need.
- To have them practice and apply what they learned while actually doing the job.
- To reinforce a new hire’s skills and knowledge of their role on an ongoing basis.
Looking one layer further in, your objectives must align with the specific role you’ll be training for. Let’s say you’re building a training program for frontline workers. Your objectives would look like this:
- Prepare the new hire with their shift schedule, dress code and what to bring on day one.
- Orient the employee to the physical space: where to clock in, where supplies are kept and who their shift lead is.
- Teach them the point-of-sale system, safety procedures and customer service scripts specific to their role.
- Have them practice while working a shift under a trainer's supervision, handling real customers or tasks with real-time feedback.
- Reinforce it all with quick refreshers on policy updates, new products or seasonal procedures.
And if your onboarding program is for remote call center workers, those five objectives would look more like this:
- Prepare the new rep by ensuring their headset, softphone login and CRM access are tested and working before their first shift.
- Orient them to the team over video and walk them through where scripts, escalation paths and shift schedules live.
- Teach the new employee the call scripts, CRM system and de-escalation procedures they'll use on every call.
- Have them practice by shadowing live calls, then take supervised calls with a trainer listening in and coaching in real time.
- Reinforce the training with call reviews and refreshers whenever scripts or products change.
Step 2. Build Your Onboarding Timeline
There isn’t a set rule for building an onboarding timeline. Especially since you can create them either for internal use or for the employee to follow. Likewise, you can have a comprehensive timeline that shows everything, or several timelines that concentrate on specific objectives.
For the sake of this discussion, let’s say you’re creating an internal overview timeline for the remote call center workers we mentioned above.
Using the 5 stages as the base, build your onboarding timeline with the expected time frame each one should ideally span. Add the 30-60-90 framework to plan assessments in advance. You can build the timelines however you want, but a Gantt chart is a great option because you can see how different actions overlap and also how long each one is.
