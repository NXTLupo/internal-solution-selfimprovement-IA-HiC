How to Create an Employee Training Plan that Employees Will Love [+ Templates]
Good employee training starts with a well-structured plan.
Without one, it’s easy to invest in courses and workshops without being clear on what employees need to learn, how training should be delivered, or whether it actually worked.
An employee training plan connects those pieces. It defines the training needs, learning objectives, participants, delivery methods, timeline and measures of success.
And getting those pieces right can have a measurable business impact. Gallup’s 2025 research estimates that doubling the share of employees who feel they have opportunities to learn and grow could lead to 18% higher profit and 14% higher productivity.
In this guide, we’ll show you how to create an employee training plan step by step, with free templates you can customize for your team.

What Is an Employee Training Plan?
An employee training plan is a document that lays out a detailed training program tailored to a specific role, team or company. It explains your training objectives, methods, and tactics, as well as how you will execute and meet training expectations. This document is a working project that should be updated regularly to meet industry standards or company changes.
Employee training plan vs. employee development plan
An employee training plan covers the skills someone needs for their current role. An employee development plan outlines how that person will grow over the next one to several years, often across multiple roles.
The two work together. A training plan closes the gap for the role someone currently holds. A development plan points to the role after that.

Why You Need an Employee Training Plan
Let’s discuss four things an employee training plan can do that ad hoc training can’t.
1. Turns Skill Gaps Into Priorities
Training and development teams regularly conduct skills gap analyses and set up training matrices to identify the skills employees lack to do their jobs at their best. That action gives you information but doesn't tell you what to do about it. That’s why a training plan is so important: to rank those gaps and prioritize what needs to be done to fill them.
Say a diagnosis turns up several issues: outdated software knowledge, inconsistent customer scripts, a safety certification that lapses next quarter, and nine smaller gaps scattered across roles. Training all twelve at once dilutes the effort and could delay the one that carries risk.
You need to make that call, and then build the plan around that choice. AIHR's training needs analysis guide frames it as a short set of factors: urgency, business impact, risk and available resources.
Use the ranking to lay out the priorities, which in turn support the learning objectives and expected outcomes. They also outline the plan’s timeline and the types of content to be created.
2. Helps Deliver Consistent Training Across the Board
A well-structured plan means employees in the same role get the same core learning objectives, standards and required content, even when delivery varies by location, language or accessibility needs.
That scope is harder to pull off at scale, which is exactly why larger companies rely on structure in the first place. The 2024 Employer Skills Survey found that structured and online training is far more common among large employers: 92% among those with 100 or more staff, versus 62% among businesses with 2 to 4 staff. A written plan is what makes that possible without losing the standard along the way.
For example, in a three-person shop, training might look like the owner walking a new hire through the register and the morning checklist. For a chain with several locations, that informal system no longer works. A solid plan will explain how different trainers can run that same walkthrough at each site, maybe in a different language, maybe over video instead of in person.
3. Define Training Success to Know What Needs Reinforcing
Effective training plans don’t stop at a task list of what to cover and when. They also define a measurement strategy aligned with the objectives and skill-gap priorities. When your plan lays it all out, the training program starts tracking performance from the start, providing you with valuable data to help you make decisions later.
Brian Blecke and Kelly Smith of Actio Learning go so far as to suggest a strategy before a plan: "A learning strategy is much more than a plan; it is a comprehensive roadmap that outlines an intended future state and measures its own success along the way."
Therefore, if your plan outlines a clear future and a way to measure it, it will be easier to achieve the training program's learning goals. Here's how that looks in context, for a company rolling out a new CRM across its sales team:
- Skill gap: Reps are used to tracking deals in spreadsheets and don't trust the new system yet.
- Objective: Every rep logs deal stages directly in the CRM, with no spreadsheet backup.
- Training: Role-specific sessions for SDRs and account managers, plus a quick-reference guide for the fields people use day-to-day.
- Measure: CRM login and deal-logging rates by team, tracked weekly through the first quarter.
Three months in, the program’s results show exactly which teams adopted the system and which are still leaning on spreadsheets. Since the objective hasn’t been reached yet, the training needs reinforcing.
4. Helps Get Your Training Budget Approved
Before a training program starts or goes into production, someone has to sign off on the payment. A scoped request gives decision-makers more information to evaluate the investment.
A plan provides the specifics for a budget conversation to run on: which gaps the program will close, what employees it’s for, and how you'll know it worked.
Here's how a short summary of your plan could look when you’re pitching it:
- Ask: $18,000 for a certification program for newly promoted managers.
- Tied to: Reducing turnover in the first year after a promotion into management.
- Who it reaches: 12 managers promoted in the last two quarters, plus upcoming promotions this year.
- How it's tracked: 12-month retention for this group, compared to last year's cohort.
Types of Employee Training Plans
Below is an overview of the most commonly used employee training plans that address a variety of business needs.
Onboarding Employee Training Plan
Also known as a new employee training plan, employee onboarding is the training new hires receive when they start work at your company. It ensures they settle in quickly and become a valuable addition to your team. Aside from the content they will learn, it’s also the best opportunity to make a first impression.
Typically, organizations have a general onboarding program and then additional role-specific training. Will your plan cover both or only one? All of that needs to go into the training plan that will standardize the onboarding program for every new hire.
For instance, let’s say that aside from providing company knowledge, you want the onboarding program to foster company talent and ensure employee retention. Your plan will need to lay out how it will achieve that through company culture, knowledge and inspirational material.

Product Knowledge Employee Training Plan
A product knowledge employee training plan is for any company where employees need to know a product inside and out, whether they're selling it, supporting it or using it daily to do their own job.
The scope depends on which of those it is.
- A sales team needs to know features, pricing and how the product compares to competitors.
- A support team needs to know common failure points and how to walk a customer through a fix.
- An internal team using a heavy tech stack needs enough depth to work efficiently without constantly escalating questions.
Your plan should name which of these the program will cover and build out the strategy accordingly.

Role-Specific Employee Training Plan
Role-specific employee training lays out the skills, tools and processes tied to a single job, not an entire department or company. It's what you'd give to a warehouse supervisor, a sales manager, or anyone in a role where general onboarding doesn't cover enough of what the day-to-day actually requires.
To build the first plan for this type of training program, talk to people already doing the job. Ask which tools they use daily, which parts of the job description are outdated, and where new hires in that role tend to get stuck. That input becomes the basis for the plan's objectives.
