# How Estée Lauder turned a training problem into a 62% engagement rate

Source: [retail_cx](https://www.retailcustomerexperience.com/blogs/how-estee-lauder-turned-a-training-problem-into-a-62-engagement-rate/)

A prestige beauty brand rebuilt how field teams accessed seasonal training. What they didn’t expect was that the same format would become one of their most effective consumer tools.
August 13, 2026 by Janina Moza — Chief Marketing Officer, Flipsnack
The best content strategies I've seen rarely start as content strategies. They start as someone fixing a very specific, very practical problem. The Estée Lauder case is one of my favorite examples.
Their U.S. education team wasn't thinking about customer engagement when they redesigned how field consultants accessed seasonal training.
They were solving a delivery problem: beauty consultants needed product knowledge at the counter, and the internal systems they were used to weren't built for someone standing between customers. So they fixed the format. And in doing so, they accidentally built the foundation for one of their most effective consumer-facing tools.
I talk to retail marketing leaders regularly who tell me their field teams have everything they need. Technically, they're usually right. The training materials exist. Someone produced them, approved them, uploaded them somewhere.
But "somewhere" is doing a lot of heavy lifting.
Research backs this up. A joint study by Schoox and Lighthouse Research & Advisory found that only 24% of frontline workers feel confident they have the right training, while 80% say their biggest challenge is finding time to access it during demanding shifts. The content isn't the problem. Reaching it in the moment that matters is.
Estée Lauder's team faced exactly this. Each season, they produced thorough training material covering product knowledge, selling techniques, and seasonal priorities. But it lived inside systems that weren't designed for quick access on the floor. The fix was structural: they consolidated everything into a single interactive flipbook, organized by chapter, accessible through one bookmarked link on any device. The content didn't change. How it was packaged did. That was the difference between content that sat on a server and content that actually got used.
Here's what makes this case interesting. The jump from internal enablement to consumer-facing content was much shorter than anyone expected. The design problems are almost identical: reach someone with limited time, give them what they need without making them search for it, and make the experience simple enough that they actually use it.
The team made this connection during the holiday season, the highest-stakes selling period for any prestige beauty brand. Gift purchasers who don't regularly shop the category often arrive at the counter with no clear idea of what they want. So the team applied the same navigable flipbook structure they'd built for training to a digital gift guide, available to shoppers through a QR code at the counter.
The results: a 62% engagement rate and an average dwell time of 17 minutes. That's not someone glancing at a screen. That's active decision-making. Shoppers used it in-store and continued engaging after they left. What made it work wasn't the technology. It was that the team already knew how to think in this format. The internal flipbook had taught them how to structure content for navigation, for mobile, for people with a task and limited time. The consumer version was the second use of something they'd already figured out.
Consumer expectations and employee expectations for digital content have arrived at the same place. Both groups want content that loads fast, navigates easily, and is organized around the task at hand, not around how the company filed it internally. Brands that build internal content to that standard are creating transferable infrastructure, whether they planned to or not.
This becomes especially clear during product launches and peak selling seasons, when you're preparing field teams and engaging consumers on the same timeline. The decisions you make for one audience, like navigation structure, use-case organization, and mobile design, apply directly to the other. Refine it once instead of building it twice. That's not a shortcut. That's good content operations.
Estée Lauder didn't set out to build a strategy that served two audiences. They set out to make training easier to find. The consumer application came later, and it worked because the internal work had already proven what the format could do.
That's the part most brands miss. The biggest content problem in retail isn't a lack of good content. It's good content trapped in formats nobody uses. Buried in shared drives, locked inside PDFs, filed away in systems that made sense for someone in an office but not for someone on the floor. The investment was already made. The knowledge is already there. What's missing is a format that actually puts it to work.
