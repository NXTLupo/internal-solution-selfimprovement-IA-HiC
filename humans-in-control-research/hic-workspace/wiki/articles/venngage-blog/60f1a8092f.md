# How Arkansas Scaled Digital Accessibility for 436,000 Students

Source: [venngage_blog](https://venngage.com/blog/digital-accessibility-in-schools-arkansas/)

Most states approach accessibility the same way: start with a pilot program, test it in a handful of schools, wait for results, then maybe scale up. Arkansas skipped all of that.
In 2022, the Arkansas Department of Education decided that digital accessibility wouldn’t be an opt-in program or a pilot. It would be the default, for every one of the state’s 436,000 public school students, starting immediately.
Students wouldn’t need a diagnosis or special approval to access support, and schools wouldn’t have to wait for a complaint or lawsuit before making accessibility a priority.
Here’s what Arkansas actually did, why it worked and how other districts can apply the same approach, regardless of their size or resources.
Why Arkansas skipped a pilot for digital accessibility
The decision wasn’t made in a vacuum. Federal scrutiny around digital accessibility in education had been increasing, and accessibility-related lawsuits had been climbing nationwide for years. Arkansas’s Department of Education looked at that trajectory and chose to act early.
The intent was simple: accessibility had to be built into the default student experience across public schools, rather than added later for students who specifically requested support.
In many schools, assistive tools are tied to an IEP, diagnosis or formal accommodation request. Arkansas made them available more broadly from the start, so students could use the support without first having to qualify for it.
That approach feels even more relevant in 2026. ADA Title II now requires covered websites and mobile apps from public schools and other state and local government entities to meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA, with compliance deadlines beginning in 2027.
Takeaway for other districts: Arkansas offers a useful lesson. Building accessibility into the learning environment early gives schools more time to implement it well, instead of making changes only when compliance deadlines or accessibility issues force them to. Arkansas treated inclusion as a starting point, not a retrofit.
What “Access for All” actually looked like
A mandate is only as good as its execution, and this is where Arkansas’s approach gets specific enough to actually replicate.
The state rolled out a set of assistive tools to every student, regardless of IEP status or documented need:
- Text-to-speech (TTS) tools from TextHelp, deployed statewide
- AI sign language avatars to translate content in real time
- Smart glasses, piloted to help students navigate their physical environment
These tools were available by default, without requiring students to identify themselves as needing support, complete additional paperwork or wait for approval.
That removes two common barriers to assistive technology in schools: friction and stigma. When accessibility tools are part of the standard learning environment, students can use the support they need without having to make a separate request for it.
How Arkansas got schools to adopt accessibility tools
Making assistive technology available statewide was only part of the rollout. Arkansas also had to make sure educators knew how to use the tools and that students were actually using them.
The state implemented accessibility adoption in several ways:
- Statewide onboarding programs trained every educator on the tools, not just special education staff
- Accessibility officers were assigned at the district level, giving every district a named person responsible for follow-through
- Dashboards tracked actual usage, not just how many licenses were distributed
- High-performing districts shared best practices with districts that were struggling to adopt
The state ran weekly dashboard reviews. Districts falling behind on usage weren’t just flagged, rather they got additional support to fix it. This turned accessibility from a one-time IT rollout into an ongoing, measured program.
For school districts planning their own digital accessibility rollout, this is an important part of the process. Providing assistive technology is only useful if teachers understand how to integrate it into the classroom and schools have a way to track adoption over time.
Arkansas’s digital accessibility rollout improved student outcomes
Rollout numbers and adoption dashboards are only meaningful if they translate into outcomes — and Arkansas tracked that too.
- Reading achievement gaps narrowed across multiple districts after the rollout
- Text-to-speech usage was linked to significant improvement in standardized test scores for students using the tools
- Some districts used the resulting data to justify further edtech investment, turning a compliance mandate into an ongoing budget priority
In one district, reading scores for students with disabilities increased by 12 points over two years of consistent text-to-speech use. Other districts used accessibility and student performance data to support further edtech investment.
That helped shift accessibility from a compliance requirement to something districts could measure as part of the learning experience. Schools could look at how assistive technology affected participation, reading support and academic performance over time.
The broader lesson from Arkansas is that accessibility outcomes should be measured alongside adoption. Tracking both shows whether students have access to the tools and whether those tools are actually supporting learning.
How school districts can implement digital accessibility at scale
Arkansas had the scale and authority of a state education department, but the underlying approach can work at the district level too. The key is to treat digital accessibility as an ongoing system rather than a one-time technology rollout.
- Start with universal access. Make assistive technology available as broadly as possible so students do not have to go through unnecessary eligibility or approval steps before they can use it.
- Track how accessibility tools are being used. Usage dashboards can show whether students and teachers are actually adopting the tools, where engagement is low and where additional support may be needed.
- Assign accessibility ownership at the school level. Give each school or department someone responsible for accessibility implementation, training and follow-through.
- Train staff on digital accessibility. Teachers and administrators need to understand how to use assistive technology and how to create accessible digital content as part of their everyday work.
- Share what works across schools. Use adoption data, teacher feedback and student outcomes to identify practices that are working and make them easier for other schools to replicate.
The same approach should extend to the digital content a district creates.
