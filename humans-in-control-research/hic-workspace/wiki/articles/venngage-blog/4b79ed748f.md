# 15 Graphic Design AI Prompts for Infographics, Social Media Posts and Presentations

Source: [venngage_blog](https://venngage.com/blog/graphic-design-ai-prompts/)

TL;DR
- Good AI design starts with specific prompts, not vague instructions. The more clearly you define what the design needs to accomplish, the less AI has to guess.
- Effective prompts define the purpose, format, style, brand cues, and constraints. These details give AI clearer direction and help avoid generic-looking results.
- Different design jobs call for different approaches. Data-rich infographics, social graphics, and presentation decks each need different instructions for structure, hierarchy, and visual storytelling.
- AI-generated designs still need human direction. Review the output, check facts and data, and refine the design rather than treating the first result as final.
- The Generate → Refine → Remix approach helps improve AI designs. Start with a strong first prompt, adjust one variable at a time, then reuse what works for future designs.
If your AI design process looks like prompt, generate, dislike, regenerate, the problem may not be the tool. You may just need to give it better direction.
A vague prompt can produce a decent first draft, but it also leaves the AI to make decisions about the layout, visual style, hierarchy and overall look. That’s when you end up regenerating the same idea in slightly different versions instead of getting closer to what you actually want.
In this article, I’ll share 15 graphic design AI prompts for social posts, infographics and presentations, plus a simple framework you can use to write better prompts for your own designs.
What is a graphic design AI prompt?
A graphic design AI prompt is a written instruction that tells an AI design tool what visual asset to create, how it should look, what it should include and what it should avoid. You can specify the format, audience, layout, colors, typography, content and brand style in the same prompt.
But a graphic design prompt differs from an AI art prompt. While an art prompt mainly describes an image, such as a subject, setting, style or composition, a graphic design prompt describes a functional asset, such as an infographic, social post or presentation, with instructions for text, hierarchy, layout and brand context.
The 5 components of a strong graphic design AI prompt
Graphic design AI follows the classic GIGO rule: garbage in, garbage out. Give it a vague prompt and you’ll likely get a generic design. Give it specific direction and you’ll give it a much better starting point.
Here are the five things to include if you want a better starting point.
1. Purpose and audience
Always start with the job your design needs to do and the person it’s aimed at.
For instance, “Create an infographic about remote work” leaves a lot to the imagination. But “Create a one-page infographic for HR leaders that summarizes five remote-work trends for an internal leadership meeting” gives the AI a clear purpose and audience.
Defining your audience also affects the visual choices. Instagram graphics aimed at college goers need a wildly different approach from sales decks designed for CFOs.
2. Format and dimensions
Tell the AI exactly what you’re making and the channel or medium people will see it. Specify the type of asset, orientation, dimensions and platform when relevant.
For example: “Create a 1080 x 1350 px LinkedIn post” or “Create a 16:9 presentation for a 30-minute sales pitch.” This prevents the AI from making a design that needs a complete redesign before you can use it.
3. Style direction
AI doesn’t know what you mean by “modern,” or it can misinterpret it broadly. Give it specifics like cream backgrounds, serif headings, bold numbers and generous white space and you’ve given it something it can actually design from.
4. Brand cues
Your prompt should tell the AI what makes the design feel like your brand. Include your brand colors, fonts, logo treatment, imagery style, tone and recurring visual elements. You can even mention what to avoid.
Strong brand cues also help your designs feel distinctive instead of blending into the wider wave of AI slop in design.
5. What to avoid
“Constraints breed creativity” applies to AI design prompts, too. Think of it as giving the AI guardrails, not just directions. Along with what you want, mention what to leave out, whether that’s stock-photo clichés, gradients, decorative icons, tiny text or too many colors.
You can also define content rules, like avoiding jargon or statistics. If something would make the design feel wrong for your brand, say so in the prompt.
Here’s a simple template prompt that brings all five elements together. Try it the next time you ask an AI to design something:
[Asset type] for [audience] about [topic]. Format: [dimensions]. Style: [style]. Brand: [brand cues]. Avoid: [avoid list].
What this looks like in practice?
I tested this with Venngage’s AI Infographic Generator using two versions of the same idea: one vague prompt, another one much more specific. Here’s how differently they turned out:
The first prompt was simple:
Create an infographic about burnout at work.
The result was good, but broad. Venngage AI created a general explainer with a definition of burnout and its three core dimensions.
Then I gave it a more specific prompt:
Create a one-page infographic on Burnout at Work: 5 Warning Signs to Watch For, aimed at employees, managers and HR teams. The purpose is to help people recognize burnout early and understand what they can do before it gets worse. Cover constant exhaustion, loss of motivation, irritability and detachment, declining focus and performance, and physical stress signals. Use a clean professional layout with strong numbering, a central workplace illustration, soft blue and peach shapes and clear visual separation between each warning sign. Keep the copy concise and easy to scan.
This time, the design followed a much clearer structure. It focused on the five warning signs I wanted, used strong numbering to guide the reader and created a more intentional visual hierarchy around the content.

5 AI prompts for infographics
Like we always say at Venngage, a good infographic does more than just look nice. So when prompting AI, tell it how to handle the data density, visual hierarchy, icons and chart types, not just what the infographic should be about.
Here are a few examples:
1. Data storytelling infographic
Prompt:
Create a [vertical/landscape] data storytelling infographic for [audience] based on the following [survey/report/research]: [paste source content or key findings].Purpose: Help [audience] understand the three to five most important findings and the main takeaway.
Structure: A strong headline that summarizes the central finding A short introduction explaining the context Three to five key statistics, with the most important statistic receiving the strongest visual emphasis A supporting chart for each major data point where appropriate A short takeaway or conclusion
Use [bar charts/line charts/donut charts/pictograms] based on the type of data being presented. Use simple icons to support the content without distracting from the numbers.
Format: [dimensions].
Style: [editorial/corporate/minimalist/bold].
Brand: [brand colors, fonts, logo treatment].
Use clear visual hierarchy, short text blocks, generous spacing and high-contrast text.
Avoid: decorative charts, unnecessary statistics, tiny text, 3D charts, visual clutter and invented data.
Best for: Turning survey results, annual reports, research or campaign data into a visual summary.
What you’ll get: A structured infographic that leads with the key finding and supports it with relevant stats. AI may suggest chart types, but review them to ensure they accurately show the data.
Tweak it: You can also tell AI which finding matters most so it gives that statistic the strongest visual emphasis.
2. Myth vs reality infographic
Prompt:
Create a myth vs reality infographic about [topic] for [audience].Create [5] concise myth-and-reality pairs using only the verified information below: [insert claims, statistics or source content].
Structure: MYTH: [short claim] REALITY: [clear correction] EVIDENCE: [one supporting fact or statistic]
Make MYTH and REALITY visually distinct within one consistent design system. Lead with the strongest myth and use a simple vertical flow for quick scanning.
Format: [dimensions].
Style: [modern/editorial/bold/minimal].
Brand: [colors, fonts, logo treatment].
Use large headings, short copy, simple supporting visuals, strong contrast and generous spacing. Keep the infographic concise and image-led rather than text-heavy.
Avoid: exaggerated claims, invented statistics, fear-based imagery, decorative visuals, dense paragraphs and unsupported conclusions.
Best for: Debunking misconceptions about your industry, product category, process or research topic.
What you’ll get: A repeatable visual pattern for every myth and its correction. That makes the infographic easy to scan and gives each claim enough space to make its point.
