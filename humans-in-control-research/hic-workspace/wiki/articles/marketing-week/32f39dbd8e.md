# Marketing needs to solve its middle management problem

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketing-solve-middle-management-problem/)

Marketing needs to solve its middle management problem
Too many middle managers are channel experts and lack the ability to think strategically and put the customer first. AI is only exacerbating this problem.
There’s a particular kind of moment in marketing – tiny, forgettable, almost sweet – when you realise the system you lovingly built is quietly betraying you.
Mine arrived on a Tuesday, the sort of day that already feels like a shrug. We were testing a new agile content operating model, and I watched a junior marketer stride in with the confidence of someone who had just asked AI to “make me a brief”. She placed a neat, tidy, beautifully formatted rectangle on the table. It looked like a brief. It behaved like a brief. It just wasn’t burdened by anything as inconvenient as thinking. In that instant, I felt the quiet, unmistakable signal that something in the system was about to reveal one of its fault lines.
The experiment itself was simple: marketers brief content producers directly, without the usual choreography of long agency briefings, set‑piece creative reviews and bureaucratic process. Nothing dramatic. Just a small test of whether we could operate without the theatre, and whether we had sufficient strategic and creative thinking in the team to operate in this way.
The junior marketer brought her short content brief and a handful of creative start‑points to her manager, a senior marketer, for feedback. The brief was immaculate – but completely devoid of critical thinking. No identifiable customer segment. No customer insight. No sense of the distinctive role our brand could play. No choices made. No judgement exercised. No curiosity or creativity applied. Just a competent rectangle of emptiness.
Her manager tried to help, but their feedback hovered at the surface. It wasn’t just weak feedback; it was at the wrong level entirely. Rather than address the vacuous brief, they sought instead to address the creative start‑points directly: a headline tweak, an image swap, a formatting suggestion, a reminder about “how we usually do it for digital or social content”. Nothing about the need for precision on who the customer is. Nothing about the barrier to overcome or why only our brand could solve this. No distinctiveness.
And in that moment, I realised the problem wasn’t the junior, their inexperience is expected, that’s why they are here to learn the craft. It wasn’t even the brief. It was my middle management layer. The manager’s inability to lift the conversation was the real warning sign. This cohort of experienced executives were not just under‑developed; they had been structurally mis‑developed.
They had the misfortune to be shaped by years of channel‑first marketing rather than customer‑first critical thinking – rewarded for fluency over framing, optimisation over discernment. Many of the most experienced managers in our organisations have built entire careers on narrow channel specialisms, rewarded for tactical fluency over developing core strategic capabilities.
The senior manager wasn’t avoiding being selective and strategic; they were avoiding exposure. Execution was the only terrain they felt comfortable in. The junior’s weak brief was a symptom, not the disease. Their job was to learn, to attempt, to bring something imperfect into the room so that better judgement could be modelled around it. The manager’s job was to lift the conversation from execution into strategy: to ask who exactly we were trying to move, what tension we were trying to resolve, what role the brand had permission to play.
AI isn’t collapsing junior roles. It is exposing a truth we’ve been avoiding for too long: for many practitioners, marketing stopped being strategic long before AI arrived.
Instead, they focused on execution because competence in delivery was the only thing they had ever been rewarded for. Judgement, the ability to frame, choose and discern at a strategic level, had never been taught let alone valued. Excellence in execution has become the currency of modern marketing. The critical thinking, curiosity and imagination – desperately required in their role in a changing world of large language models – exposes the limits of a career built on channel tactics.
They micromanage their junior’s work because they don’t know how to coach. And some are so entrenched in their specialism that they don’t recognise the need to evolve – even as AI accelerates the automation of the very activities their careers were built on.
They aren’t bad marketers. They are mis-developed ones, shaped by a system that confused narrow channel specialism with the broader strategic thinking required to drive commercial outcomes. And the AI the junior had used to knock together the brief at speed hadn’t removed the learning experience; it had removed the hiding places for their ill‑equipped line manager to QA and help them improve their work.
This remains an uncomfortable truth. AI isn’t collapsing junior roles. It is exposing a truth we’ve been avoiding for too long: for many practitioners, marketing stopped being strategic long before AI arrived. Now it is exposing the hollow middle created when we, as an industry, confused making things with shaping thinking.
When we allowed channel expertise to masquerade as strategic competence. When we in‑housed content and mistook proximity for capability in changing customer perceptions, attitudes and behaviours. When we rewarded output over breakthrough and commercial outcome. When we let process replace judgement and quietly allowed the rungs of the marketing ladder to rot away.
Because the old way of learning – the one that taught marketers how to think, not just how to deliver relied on something the modern system no longer provides: time. Time to sit beside someone who knew what good looked like. Time to watch them frame a problem. Time to see how they chose what not to include. Time to learn the difference between a tactic and a strategy. Time to absorb judgement through proximity, osmosis and practice, not just theory learned in a marketing degree.
Today, these opportunities are largely gone. The middle has thinned to the point of fragility, hollowed out by years of execution‑first marketing, channel specialism, and the belief that optimisation was the same as strategy and that knowing how to operate a platform was the same as knowing how to build a brand.
Now, our junior marketers are operating very fast – aided by AI – they can produce content in an instant. Whether it is any good, or if they recognise that, is far less certain. And here is a 21st‑century marketing dilemma: AI is hoisting up the bottom rungs of the ladder – but the truth is the ladder had already begun to rot long before AI arrived. It is taking away the low‑stakes tasks where judgement, critical thinking, curiosity and imagination used to grow. It is collapsing execution and the ability to discern. It can accelerate competence but not wisdom. And it is revealing, with uncomfortable clarity, who has been building sound marketing judgement as their core capability, and who has been only building channel expertise.
It is forcing every marketing leader to confront a truth we’ve all been avoiding: learning how to be a great marketer isn’t vanishing because of AI. It is vanishing because, over the last 20 years, we quietly stopped tending it. And this is where the modern CMO must step forward, not as a functional leader, but as a steward of marketing judgement. Because judgement cannot be outsourced. It cannot be delegated. It cannot be automated. It can only be grown: deliberately, patiently and repeatedly.
In the middle – especially in the middle – at the edges, and across the whole marketing ecosystem. The modern CMO’s job is no longer to run a department. It is to rebuild a craft. To create marketers who can frame strategic arguments and enable breakthrough creativity to solve intractable business problems – not just assemble decks or execute campaigns. To develop people who can see the whole system, not just the task at hand. To grow strategists who can think through any marketing problem with confidence.
That means reinstating the conditions where judgement can grow. It means putting judgement, thinking, curiosity and imagination back into the marketing operating system: reinstating live brief reviews where people are taught to sharpen the problem, not just polish the asset; making managers accountable for coaching strategic choices, not simply approving outputs; creating standards for what a good brief, a good insight and a good creative argument actually look like; protecting time for learning and practice; and refusing to let channel fluency pass for marketing capability.
To restore core applied marketing techniques to solve problems, not nostalgically but intentionally, because the future will not wait for it to regenerate on its own.
The CMO role is changing and what is emerging is raw, exposed, and more consequential than ever. At its heart it is revealing something simple and unavoidable: the only thing that ever mattered in marketing was our ability to use critical thinking and imagination to solve problems. AI didn’t hollow out the middle. It simply revealed that we had. The ladder is broken, but the craft is not.
The future belongs to the marketing leaders brave enough to rebuild judgement itself: in their teams, in their agencies and in themselves.
