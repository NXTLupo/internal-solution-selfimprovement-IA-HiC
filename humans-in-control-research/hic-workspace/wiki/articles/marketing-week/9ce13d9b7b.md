# GEO is not SEO and neither of them is marketing

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/geo-seo-marketing/)

GEO is not SEO and neither of them is marketing
Being found by AI is not the same as being chosen by it and those brands that learn that distinction will win in the AI search era.
Open ChatGPT, Claude and Google’s AI overviews, and ask each one the same question a customer might ask. For example, “what’s the best food for my ageing dog?” You’ll notice the same pattern every time. Three or four brands make the shortlist. Only one or two of them get recommended. The rest of the category is invisible.
I opened ChatGPT and asked it for the best mouthwash. No surprises, Listerine came back first. Founded in 1879, it is the number one mouthwash brand in the UK and one of the billion-dollar brands I’ve managed and loved in my career.
Then I asked it the type of question a real person would ask. “My gums are bleeding, what should I use?” Listerine still makes the shortlist most of the time, Corsodyl made the top three exactly as often, but when the answer named a single winner, it was Corsodyl 16 times and Listerine not once.
Listerine is found. Corsodyl is chosen. Why?
If you are lucky enough for your brand to be on that list, I’m willing to bet that you had almost nothing to do with putting it there. A machine decided how your brand appears in its answer, based on everything it could read about you from across the internet.
A third buyer has entered the room
Classically trained marketers always had two audiences, the trade buyer at Boots, Tesco, Asda etc. and the end consumer of our products. The trade buyer secured the listing, the facings and category-weighted distribution, whilst our consumer campaigns drove initial demand and repeat purchase.
A third buyer, the LLM, has now joined the party, one which I call the ‘Invisible Buyer’. It stands between you and your consumer in the same way a trade buyer did in traditional retail. Unlike the Tesco category buyer, though, you have no relationship with it. You can’t drop by its office, you can’t take it out for lunch, and you can’t negotiate on trade margin or listing fees.
As agentic commerce moves into FMCG, it may well do your checkout too.
So where do SEO and GEO fit in?
There is a gold rush to win over this Invisible Buyer. Just as digital marketers rebranded themselves as SEO experts, now SEO experts are rebranding themselves as GEO gurus (most of them are not). SEO may help a human find you, and GEO may help a machine read you, but neither is a miracle pill that will get you chosen by an LLM. Your consumer marketing decides whether you get chosen.
We have spent 20 years perfecting how to be found through SEO. In my August audit of 220 sports nutrition answers across four AI platforms, the two most-named brands were almost equal: Optimum Nutrition appeared in 65 answers, Myprotein in 59.
Out of the 220 answers, the machine only chose a clear winner 39 times. Optimum Nutrition took 16 of those. Myprotein took one. Bulk, named in 31 answers, took none. GEO will make your brand present and retrieved. It will not make it the brand the machine recommends.
Why the machine chooses what it chooses
I have spent the past few months running an AI Visibility Audit (AIVS) across ten CPG categories, logging every source these models read when someone asks them a shopping question. It came to 5,772 citations.
The single most cited source is the NHS website. Reddit turns up in all ten categories, the only source that does. Instagram, Facebook and TikTok managed 53 citations between them, under one per cent, for three platforms we have spent a decade learning and chasing (or teaching, in my case, having worked at Instagram and Facebook).
Just over half of everything cited came from an independent authority: health bodies, established press, consumer testing. Under a quarter came from brand-owned pages and retailer listings.
Two things move an AI recommendation: salience and proof. A brand that comes to mind, and independent evidence that it is any good. Both are marketing outputs. You cannot optimise your way past a weak brand, because the model is a fast, literal reader of the reputation you have spent years building. It is reading your reviews, your ratings and your Reddit threads back at you, like some kind of weird ‘This Is Your Life’.
What has actually changed
For a century, building brand equity was part art, part science. KitKat has run ‘Have a break’ for 68 years. L’Oréal Paris has said ‘Because you’re worth it’ for 55. Red Bull’s been giving you wings for 39. Not every FMCG brand bothered with that kind of long-term investment though, a weak brand could still buy its way onto the shelf through distribution and trade deals. Own label did it without any brand building at all.
The Invisible Buyer takes that option off the table. You cannot pay an LLM for shelf placement, and there’s no trade deal that gets you onto its shortlist. Brand equity is now the only route in, and the only way to be chosen. In other words: marketing.
When I moved from Johnson & Johnson to Facebook, the number one ask from FMCG marketing leaders was simple: give us a playbook, and give us the language to pressure test what our teams and agencies are telling us.
Back then, social strategy usually got handed to the most junior person in the room, that is until social started influencing consumer choice, and leadership finally took note. AI is following the same story.
Over the past few months I’ve sat down with more than twenty senior marketing leaders, and the questions barely change. Why does ChatGPT recommend our competitor? Is our share of AI recommendation rising or falling? Does my team even know how to do this?
This is the simplest answer I’ve found. There are three jobs: get found, get chosen, get bought. GEO gets you found. Marketing gets you chosen. The commercial team gets you bought.
Job one – be found. Presence is whether you are there at all, which means your data exists across the surfaces that shape choice. Retrieval is whether the machine pulls you into the candidate set for the way people prompt. It sounds like housekeeping, but it is critical. If a shopper asks for a vegan supplement and you have not tagged yours as vegan, you are absent from the answer. This is GEO’s job.
Job two – be chosen. Now the machine decides whether to put you forward by reading the evidence the world has left about you. Your reviews and ratings. What experts and creators say. Your PR, the claims you make and whether they are consistent. The Reddit threads you may never have read. This is your job as a marketer.
Job three – be bought. You must be purchasable. In stock, priced consistently, easy to check out, and reachable by an agent doing the buying on someone’s behalf. This is where marketing and the commercial team have to be in lockstep. If you are out of stock, a human consumer may wait. An agent will simply buy the next brand on the shortlist, and the lost sale is likely to be invisible to you.
Recommendation itself is not a job. It is the outcome of the first two.
And it goes round. Reviews, sales rank and repeat purchase feed back into what the machine knows about you next time. The machine will remember.
You can test this yourself this afternoon. You do not need budget to start. Take your three most important category searches and run them across three platforms. If your brand comes back in the top three and is named as a top pick, well done. If it does not, the answer is not a GEO brief. Go back to your marketing plan and work out which part of your evidence is lacking.
And do not mistake appointing a GEO agency as your miracle pill. SEO helped people find you. GEO helps machines read you. Neither decides whether you get chosen. That was always marketing’s job, and it still is.
