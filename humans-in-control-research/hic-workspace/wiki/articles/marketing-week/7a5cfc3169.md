# Marketing is a mindset, not just a function

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketing-mindset-not-just-function/)

Marketing is a mindset, not just a function
The value of marketing to a business is more than the efficient execution of marketing activity.
ROI measurement will never prove the true value of marketing. The reason is the same that the value of Jude Bellingham cannot be measured by his VO2 Max ratio, important though this ratio is to understand his exceptional athletic performance.
The value of marketing to the business rests on three related foundations – the importance of the ‘outside’; the importance of the long term; and the need for a business to explore, not just exploit.
Let’s examine each of these foundations in turn, which illustrate why the contribution of marketing is much more than delivering one metric.
The importance of the Outside
In our recent piece on Double CFO Syndrome, Chris Burggraeve and I borrowed the insight from Peter Drucker that the CEO should serve as the link between the ‘outside’ (the market and social context in which the business operates) and the ‘inside’ (how the business organises operations).
The success of the business depends on the CEO matching the capabilities of the business – which include the ability to run an efficient marketing function – with the opportunities in the market. ROI is a measure of the efficient execution of marketing activity. It does not address the value of the marketing mindset.
The marketing mindset comprises the outside half of the enterprise value equation. The finance mindset is more concerned with the Inside.
The sustainability of the commercial success of any business depends on the degree to which it maintains and evolves its ability to deliver the benefits that customers want, based on a cost structure that enables it to make an adequate profit.
Nike’s recent delisting from the S&P 100 (after 18 years in the index) illustrates how a focus on revenue management and cost optimisation (the CFO View above) may help a business generate exceptional profits in the short-term but risks a collapse in customer demand once the existing brand equity has been depleted.
The importance of the Long Term
This leads into the second point, that business management is a marathon, not the series of quarterly sprints that revenue management involves. You cannot understand who won the World Cup by only studying the goals scored in each quarter (FIFA has a lot to answer for in turning football into a game of four quarters, not two halves).
The strategic contribution of marketing lies in providing the context in which to judge whether revenue management is causing the business to “over earn” in the short term by borrowing from the future. To use a farming metaphor, marketing ensures that the seed corn is being planted, not eaten.
Marketing is the sowing, growing and harvesting of cash flow. The value of marketing lies in its understanding of the entire process for generating sustainable, mutually beneficial relationships with customers.
If you look at the valuation of most businesses, the discounted present value of their earnings over the next five years hardly ever represents more than 25% of their market value. This means that 75% of the value of most businesses depend on their ability to develop and maintain a customer franchise that lasts over decades, not quarters.
This is known as the ‘terminal value’ – the truly unfortunate way that finance refers to the ongoing value of the business (it is terminal because it is the final column in a spreadsheet that only has explicit forecasts for performance in Years One through Five). The underlying assumption of the terminal value is that the business has developed a franchise that will enable it to earn a return over the cost of capital from Year Six onwards. The company is betting on the business’s ability to continue generating earnings in the long term.
In other words, it has established a brand – whether deliberately or not.
Marketers play a vital role in ensuring businesses do not optimise for the 25% at the expense of the 75%.
The importance of Exploration, not just Exploitation
Markets are dynamic. Customer needs change, technology changes, competitors are constantly trying to take market share. This means that strategy is the discipline of purposeful change.
CFOs dislike change because change involves uncertainty, and uncertainty involves a higher discount rate being applied to future earnings. Marketers need to be more forceful about the need for innovation and marketing (both forms of adapting the business to future customer needs) because, as former US Army chief of staff Eric Shinseki famously remarked, “If you dislike change, you’re going to dislike irrelevance even more.”
The third strategic contribution of marketing is to guide the process of exploration. That is, to identify the new products or services and new customers that the business has the best probability of developing successfully.
This demands a marketing mindset that focuses on the potential for value creation beyond the existing product or service offerings of the business and beyond its existing customer base.
How marketers can articulate business performance beyond shareholder returns
An exploitation mindset will always conclude that the best ROI will come from optimising the cost base of the existing business. The math is irrefutable – if you can maintain current revenues with lower costs, then all the savings drop straight to the bottom line, and the current earnings of the business soar.
However, as noted above, a business can only expect to have a Terminal Value if it continues to maintain the quality of its franchise in the market and this means recognising the necessity of creating new value to offset the inevitable decline in the attractiveness of your existing product/service offerings due to the actions of competitors and the changing expectations of your customers. This is the job of exploration.
The contribution of the marketing mindset is to narrow the vast range of possible actions that the company could pursue to the ones that it should pursue because their probability of success is heightened because they represent a purposeful evolution of the existing franchise of the business – in other words, its brand.
Brand is what the business means to its customers. It expresses the trust and permission the business has established by being useful and distinctive in their eyes such that they continue to perceive benefit from continuing to transact with the business, even as the product and service offering change. This is not brand love. This is the value proposition that comes from offering benefits that are not achievable through a different relationship.
Brands draw their value both from the customer lifetime value of current customers and the future customer relationships that the business has earned the right to establish.
In an earlier article, I described how marketers can express this in terms of the four levels of certainty around the revenues of the business:
- Predictable revenues – these are the baseline revenue in the marketing mix model that would occur even in the absence of promotional marketing
- Probable revenues – these are the incremental revenues that can be unlocked either through opportunistic sales activation (“performance marketing”) or through attracting new customers based on the appeal of the brand (“brand building”)
- Possible revenues – these are new revenue opportunities made possible through incremental innovation in the product, its distribution, its pricing, or its market positioning
- Potential revenues – these are new revenues that result from stretch goals (maybe even “moon shots”) but which offer the potential to introduce a revolutionary innovation that disrupts the category or even creates a new one
The “exploitation” activities favoured by CFOs focuses only on predictable revenue and part of probable revenue. The “exploration” activities that the CMO must champion involve a marketing mindset to consider the probable, possible or potential revenue that the business can generate based on evolving the relationships enabled by its brand.
Marketing is the sowing, growing and harvesting of cash flow. The value of marketing lies in its understanding of the entire process for generating sustainable, mutually beneficial relationships with customers. This is the marketing mindset that ROI does not measure because it is only focused on the efficient execution of marketing programs.
