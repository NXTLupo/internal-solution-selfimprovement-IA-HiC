# Price vs volume: How are brands fighting consumer fatigue to drive growth?

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/big-brands-growth-fatigued-consumer/)

Price vs volume: How are brands fighting consumer fatigue to drive growth?
Recent earnings calls from the likes of P&G, McDonald’s and Unilever provide insight into how some of the biggest B2C brands plan to deliver growth.
There’s no single path to growth and it can be tough for businesses to decide which road to take.
Some routes to growth take longer, which can be challenging for businesses accountable to investors who expect quarter-after-quarter returns. Other paths are accelerated, but less sustainable.
Marketers keen to position themselves as drivers of growth must understand the different paths, as well as why a business might choose one route over another. Fact is, companies may change tack over time depending on market conditions, consumer sentiment and their role in the category.
Post-pandemic, most big businesses leant on pricing as their main driver of growth. This era saw inflation skyrocket and companies pass on cost increases to consumers. In turn, many consumers traded down to private label alternatives (or in some cases, out of the category completely). This meant businesses relied on high prices to drive revenue growth, rather than volume given consumers were generally buying less.
The last couple of years have seen companies gradually move away from pricing as their main lever of growth. Late July and early August saw many of the world’s biggest B2C firms report results and a clear theme emerged – pricing alone cannot fuel growth.
Nestlé ups marketing spend as it pursues ‘more and better’ investment strategy
Food and drink giant Nestlé told investors they should expect to see “less pricing” going forward, with the business focused instead on “real internal growth” – a metric encompassing volume as well as portfolio mix.
Elsewhere, during Coca-Cola’s first half results, CEO Henrique Braun said the business aspired to drive volume and pricing “in tandem” in 2026 to drive growth.
In a different category, McDonald’s leadership made clear during the fast-food chain’s most recent results call that while “cheque size” (the amount customers spend in its restaurants) is important, “guest count” (the number of customers it attracts) is crucial for growth.
There appears to be a consensus among big consumer businesses that driving more sales (rather than pricing higher) is essential for growth in the near future. But there is far from a consensus on how exactly to do it.
A ‘fatigued’ consumer
Following those years of pricing-led growth, many commentators argue success presented a victory for the power of brand.
It’s true that during that period brands did successfully increase prices without losing droves of consumers. The gap between brands and private label increased, yet most consumers continued to buy brands, apparently accepting the price premium.
The issue is, while most companies didn’t see volumes that were disastrously lower, there still were consumers who abandoned brands during this time. Whether it was for private label, or trading out of the category completely, many consumers made choices to save money during a cost of living crisis.
For example, the chart below shows Unilever’s performance over the past five years in terms of volume and pricing. You can see all business units saw volumes decline during at least a couple of quarters as the FMCG giant increased prices to drive growth.
Unilever, as well as many of FMCG competitors, has reverted to a focus on volume rather than pricing going forward.
However, that is easier said than done. The consumers who switched to private label during those years of high inflation may well have found that, actually the own-brand alternative to their usual dish soap, yoghurt or shampoo works just as well. Having already made the switch, these consumers will be hard to tempt back to a more expensive alternative with a promise of “brand superiority”.
In the UK, the rise of Aldi and Lidl, with their relentless own-brand versions (anyone fancy some Nordpak butter or a Professor Peppy fizzy drink?) has also meant many consumers are shopping in grocery environments where brands are second place to much cheaper, similar alternatives.
The sticky behaviour of private label shopping is one challenge for brands. The lasting impact of inflation is another.
It would be a mistake to ignore the cumulative impact of inflation in recent years. While the rate of inflation has slowed over the past couple of years, consumers still face markedly higher prices than they did pre-Covid.
According to data from the Office for National Statistics (ONS), inflation as measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) has risen cumulatively by 28% over the past five years. In practical terms, that’s equivalent to a £100 basket of shopping rising to £128.
In many of the earnings call this quarter, businesses have acknowledged the impact of cumulative inflation on the consumer. There is a fatigue among shoppers and a (correct) sense that things cost much more than they used to a few years ago.
The quest to sell more
Brands are tasked with driving sales in an environment where private label is extremely competitive and consumers are sick of paying more. Easy, right?
While pricing is no longer seen as the sole mechanism for delivering growth, brands aren’t dropping prices either. Most of the consumer goods businesses to report in recent months have recorded low-single figure pricing increases.
Brands don’t, generally, drop prices across the board. It’s seen as a dangerous and unprofitable tactic. However, faced with a fatigued consumer who has access to cheaper products, being seen as value-for-money is absolutely paramount.
‘This is a result of stronger brands’: Unilever drives best volume growth in 15 years
That was clear in the recent results calls. Companies are looking at ways to prioritise value.
Prices rarely come down wholesale, but in an effort to pull consumers away from private-label and into brands, companies may look at price promotion and discounting, temporarily bringing down prices.
Unilever was very successful in its goal of driving volume-led growth in its most recent quarter. The FMCG giant delivered 5.5% volume growth, its best figure in 15 years. That remarkably successful quarter was backed by increased promotional activity, particularly in line with the firm’s World Cup activations.
Unsurprisingly, Unilever was keen to emphasise this approach was “deliberate” and attributed its volume growth primarily to “stronger brands”, not increased promotional activity.
‘Not a growth strategy’: How marketers are rethinking price promotions
Promotion (at least in the pricing sense) tends to be something of a dirty word among many brand-oriented marketers. Among the criticism is that promotion provides a short-term hit and encourages a race to the bottom across the category, potentially damaging brand equity and the ability to charge a price premium.
However, Unilever is not alone in leaning on price promotions. Marketing Week’s 2026 Language of Effectiveness research finds 40.4% of marketers are leaning on promotions as the focus of their pricing strategy.
During its recent results, P&G CFO Andre Schulten said the company had seen promotional activity rise by five points in Europe over recent times. While Unilever presented promotional activity as part of the strategy for its most recent quarter, P&G was keen to downplay its impact.
While the business stressed the importance of driving what it called the “consumer value equation”, Schulten was clear promotion is not central to the strategy.
“We don’t believe that promotion, in any way, shape or form, is a way to build the business or to acquire users on a sustainable basis,” the CFO told investors.
So how do you drive value?
P&G has been praised by many for its resistance to rely on price promotion. And yet, promotions remain an important tactical lever to pull.
If anything, the 40.4% of Language of Effectiveness respondents who confessed to prioritising price promotions are probably a conservative estimate. For FMCG brands, for example, promotions are part-and-parcel of their relationship with retailers.
If you ask brand marketers how they can drive value perceptions versus private label, they will often cite the ‘genuine superiority’ of their product. While a superior product is something that can be stated in ads, consumers must trial the product to get a true sense.
When many consumers are perfectly content using private label alternatives, a special offer is sometimes what’s needed to drive trial. Hope is the product ‘superiority’ is enough to bring them back the next time, at full price.
P&G: Price promotion is not a way to acquire consumers sustainably
There are other methods to drive trial, such as smaller packs. Tony’s Chocolonely drove 1.3 points of incremental penetration in six months just by launching a smaller-sized bar.
Of course price promotion is another valid way to drive trial, if used strategically. Everyone knows the cautionary tales, but there’s no shame in using promotions in a restrained way that makes sense for your brand.
Marketers ought to be honest with themselves about the uses of promotion, but they also need to be honest about its limitations. In its results, Unilever was clear it doesn’t expect to repeat the lofty heights of five points of volume growth every quarter.
There’s no silver bullet or shiny new toy to drive value perceptions in a challenged consumer environment. Businesses, by and large, have the same overarching levers at their disposal they always did: price pack architecture, meaningful innovation and renovation, price promotion and investing in brand.
Every marketer knows value-for-money comes from more than pricing. There is concrete evidence investing in brand can drive value perceptions, without ever touching the price.
