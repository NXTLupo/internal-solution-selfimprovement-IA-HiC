# Should marketers take responsibility over profit? It’s not as simple as it sounds

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/marketers-responsibility-profit/)

Should marketers take responsibility over profit? It’s not as simple as it sounds
No-one would argue that marketers shouldn’t care about profit, however, the argument that marketing is being held back by a lack of ownership over P&L neglects to consider how much control they reasonably have over it.
In 2008, just as the world was about to enter one of its most unforgiving financial crises, a group of Japanese scientists published what would become a landmark paper called ‘Traffic jams without bottlenecks’. It described an experiment in which the researchers put 22 cars on a circular track 230 metres in circumference, arranged them at roughly uniform intervals, and told the drivers to drive at around 30 km/h, follow the car ahead, and maintain reasonably steady motion. There were no junctions, no traffic lights, merging lanes, accidents, hills, roadworks, cyclists, or environmental activists from which congestion could originate.
At first, it seemed to work as one might intuitively expect. That is to say, the cars circulated in a homogenous flow the likes of which Londoners could only dream of. But then, something happened. Suddenly, the flow broke down and a localised cluster of ever slower traffic formed. This congestion then travelled backwards around the circuit as if a wave, even though every car was moving forward, precisely in the same way that one might observe on real highways.
In brief technical terms, what the study showed was that traffic flow is a complex non-equilibrium system of interacting entities. Above a sufficiently high density, ostensibly stable free flow becomes unstable. Small fluctuations can grow rather than dissipate, eventually producing a qualitatively different macro state: congestion. Hence, while a bottleneck may trigger a jam, it is not required for one to exist.
The finding was rather important, as most people would reason that something had to have caused the traffic jam; by removing the obstacle, it would go away. The crux, though, is that there does not need to be a singular proximate cause corresponding to the phenomenon being observed. The jam can be a collective consequence of local interactions under particular system conditions.
So, I hear you ask, what the hell does this have to do with marketing? Well, as it turns out, a great deal.
You see, what the experiment described is a complex scientific phenomenon known as emergence. What it means, in the simplest of words, is that different properties become meaningful at different levels of organisation, and that explanations that work at one level often fail to transfer to another. For instance, liquidity is a property of the chemistry of water, but its defining features can only be established at a certain scale; observing molecular structures reveals nothing about properties such as viscosity. Similarly, computers may be understood at the level of wires, transistors, and flows of electrons, or they may be understood at the level of software such as word processing programs or video editing suites, but observation of one provides no meaningful information about the other.
From this follows that there are things in life about which one learns less the more one takes them apart – a counterintuitive fact that often frustrates those inclined to believe (or sell the proposition) that everything needs to be broken down into optimisable pieces. But it is scientifically well-established, and provides a crucial lesson for marketers.
Take, for example, the still ongoing debate between the Ehrenberg-Bass acolytes and the group one might refer to as the traditionalists. Much of what appears to be a fundamental contradiction may just be a level-of-analysis problem; one camp predominantly observes emergent macro-level patterns, while the other attempts to explain micro-level mechanisms. Neither group has yet realised this is the case.
A piece published a few weeks ago, written by Marketing Week’s Niamh Carroll, highlighted an insight from the 2026 Language of Effectiveness Survey: while growing sales revenue was “a top priority” for roughly 60% of marketers, only about 25% saw “protecting or growing profit margins” as a prioritised task. This, the article concluded, meant that they were looking at “only
half” of what ultimately matters to CEOs and CFOs. The implication being, of course, that they are doing themselves a disservice.
But as someone with a lengthy background in management consulting and executive decision-making, allow me to let you in on a little secret: what matters to the chief executive is not necessarily what should be used to steer every constituent function – and this is especially true when the object of conversation is something like profit. There are two main reasons why.
First, although there is nothing overly complicated about the calculation that is subtracting costs from revenues, both arise from the interaction of multiple contributing factors such as price, volume, mix, product, production costs, procurement, distribution, channel economics, capacity utilisation, wages, overhead, capital allocation, and so on. Marketing certainly affects several of these directly or indirectly, but it does not therefore follow that it determines the resultant.
Second, while a company ought to (as a rule) strive for profit, it does not mean that every part of the company should. Such attempts may actually lower the overall profitability; in complex systems, optimising parts does not automatically improve the whole. This is easy enough to illustrate with basic examples. If, say, manufacturing lowers the cost of materials to improve margin, and marketing increases the price charged to do the same, customers may realise they are paying more for an inferior product and switch to a competitor. Similarly, just as one might improve ROI by lowering the denominator, a CFO or CMO may decide to cut marketing spend to improve short-term profitability at the cost of long-term profitability.
Emphasising part profitability under the belief that it improves whole profitability can also lead to perverse incentives. And it often does. For instance, as illustrated by Malmi et al. in a 2023 report for Contemporary Accounting Research, Nokia used to organise both its businesses and markets with their own P&L responsibility; managerial incentives were tied to the profits thereof. Thus, when the company acquired Navteq for $8.1bn, the mapping company was left as a separate business with its own P&L. This created an absurd situation whereby if a Nokia handset business wanted to put Navteq navigation into a phone, the cost appeared against that product unit. Some product categories therefore refused to include navigation because doing so would prevent them from reaching their target margin and price point, damage their profit, and lower bonuses. It would require a lot of, shall we say, analytical imagination to suggest that this strengthened Nokia’s relative competitiveness in the mobile phone market.
Perhaps I digress.
My point is that company profit is not merely the sum of independently generated departmental profits; no single function produces it, even though the actions of every function may contribute to it. This means the various senior marketers Carroll interviewed are correct in observing that accountability for profitability is extremely difficult. But it also means that they are incorrect in concluding that by giving marketers more responsibility for something largely out of their control, they would somehow become more commercially sophisticated.
And since I know that some people read my columns much in the same way that one might expect the Devil to read the Bible, I want to be clear on this: this is not an attribution problem. It is an ontological problem. The difficulty does not begin with our inability to measure marketing’s share of profit, but with what kind of “thing” profit is in the first place.
Notably, this does not mean that marketers should stop caring about it. Quite the opposite. A marketer who cannot read a P&L, understand margins, or explain how marketing activity contributes to commercial performance, is not conveniently liberated by emergence. No, they are simply commercially illiterate. I have made that point for years. What it does mean, however, is that the more useful question is not whether marketing should be responsible for profit, but what marketers can reasonably be held responsible for.
As for the answer, well, I am sure that will be debated for a long time too. I just hope that whoever partakes remembers the conclusion of the Japanese traffic jam study.
