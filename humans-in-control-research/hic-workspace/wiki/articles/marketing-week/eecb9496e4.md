# Brand purpose was little more than lip service – and AI proves it

Source: [marketing_week](https://www.marketingweek.com/brand-purpose-lip-service-ai/)

Brand purpose was little more than lip service – and AI proves it
Marketers were quick to jump aboard the brand purpose trend – but the industry’s eager adoption of AI proves few truly believed in its mission.
Once upon a time, as if partaking in an industry-wide rendition of the 1971 Hilltop campaign by Coca-Cola, marketers around the world came together to declare that the most important thing for everything and anything was something called “purpose”.
This “purpose” took many forms – a political stance, an organisational value, an environmental conscience, inclusivity, diversity, and so on – but common to all was that they provided a promised panacea. All brands had to do in order to thrive was to find, develop, or simply invent their own.
The evidence for the extraordinary claim, however, was not even ordinary. The foundational texts, such as Jim Stengel’s ‘Grow’, provided not so much case studies of company behaviours as case studies of exceptionally bad methodology.
Among other faux pas, Stengel picked top performers, tried to find anything in their public narratives that could be labelled a “purpose”, and called correlation causation – leaving aside any questions about whether underperformers shared the trait.
To paraphrase Dave Snowden, it may well be that the executives of 20 high-performing firms have regular bowel movements, but it does not therefore follow that one’s recruitment strategy should be based on covert observation of the toilet habits of those applying to join the organisation. There may be some linkage due to lack of stress, but it is not a causal linkage. The logic too is shit.
However, many high-profile analysts, supposed experts and chest-thumping commentators nonetheless followed suit, sometimes going so far as to utterly embarrass themselves in their attempts to make “purpose” work. And marketers, ever the easiest cohort to market to, went with it.
Customer loyalty is as much about acquisition as it is retentionToday, the landscape has shifted slightly, but only slightly. According to the Sustainable Marketing 2030 report, 93% of marketers still think that brands have a responsibility to help people live more sustainably. A further 93% are convinced that marketing can make a difference on this journey.
While 90% agree that sustainability agendas must be more ambitious; 84% believe that companies need to be braver in communicating their sustainability efforts. Roughly half of all brands also now claim to have sustainability as a KPI, though I have no idea how one would define or measure such a thing.
Meanwhile, a study by the World Federation of Advertisers reported that 81% of marketers are investing more time in defining their brand purpose. Research by CNN found that 81% of marketers planned to incorporate community-building and Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) themes into their marketing efforts.
A study released by the American Marketing Association New York concluded that “purpose is alive and well and growing”.
It all seems a bit weird. Most buyers do not really care that much about the brands that they buy and, as a result, have no idea what the brands stand for. That is not to say that they are not happy to claim otherwise when others are looking, but humans have a propensity toward virtue signalling, answering when questioned what they believe to be “correct” regardless of whether or not they actually hold those values in practice.
When it is their own money on the line, purchase patterns show it is a different matter entirely; survey radicals turn into fiscal conservatives faster than one can say “Greenpeace”. Unsurprisingly, therefore, “purpose”-driven brands do not actually outperform other brands.
Perchance the reason for the incongruity is to be found in the above paragraph: though engineers are likely to disagree, marketers are humans too, and thus also prone to virtue signalling. Maybe it is pluralistic ignorance – a situation whereby people privately reject or doubt a view, norm or practice (such as the supposedly universal importance of “purpose”), but mistakenly believe that most others support it, and therefore keep it to themselves. Or perhaps marketers truly believe that one can only really do well by doing good.
I would much prefer it if the last one were not the case, because if so, they would come across as wholly hypocritical. You see, for all their Thunbergian posturing, they still make a point of emphasising the importance of AI implementation at every opportunity.
Does having a brand purpose actually lead to growth?According to Gartner’s 2026 Spend Survey, 70% of CMOs say that becoming an AI leader is a critical goal for the current fiscal year. BCG’s CMO Survey released last month saw 96% of respondents claim that AI is driving end-to-end transformation of marketing. And if you do not believe either, or any of the many pieces of research that provide the same narrative, just go to a marketing conference. Or open a window. Trust me, you will hear it.
So what does artificial intelligence have to do with “purpose”, I hear you ask? Well, a hell of a lot, actually. AI carries costs beyond the financial that go directly against what one would have expected “purpose” proponents to believe in. For example:
Sustainability is exceptionally difficult to reconcile with indiscriminate, large-scale AI use. The International Energy Agency projects that global data-centre electricity consumption will roughly double to about 945 TWh by 2030, growing around 15% annually from 2024 to 2030 – more than four times faster than total electricity-consumption growth from other sectors. In the US, it is expected to consume between 6.7% to 12% of the total electricity output by 2028. Data scientist Alex de Vries-Gao estimates that AI systems alone could account for 32.6–79.7m tons of CO₂ emissions in 2025, with a water footprint of 312.5–764.6bn litres.
Although it is entirely possible to be creative with AI, or to use AI in creative, its use comes with displaced creative labour in the visible economy. The short-term effects of LLM use on freelancers in highly exposed occupations such as creative, writing, design, and marketing-adjacent markets, have proven notable. Meanwhile, the IMF estimates that about 40% of global employment is exposed to AI, rising to around 60% in advanced economies. For roughly half of those exposed jobs in advanced economies, AI could complement workers, for the other half, it could reduce labour demand, wages, or hiring.
The Nuremberg Institute for Market Decisions reports that only 21% of respondents trusted AI companies and their promises, and only 20% trusted AI itself. This is of note because its experiments also suggest that attitudes toward AI shape reactions to AI-generated ads, with stronger negative bias among those who believe creativity is uniquely human. Other research has found that AI disclosure can reduce trust and purchase intent, especially for high-involvement products. One study published late last year in the Quarterly Journal of Economics and Economic Policy reported that transparently labelled AI-generated ads caused lower trust and intent than human-labelled ads.
The list, as they say, goes on. My point is not to be some kind of AI alarmist, nor to belittle those who use the technology in a professional setting. As I have disclosed before, part of my own work is directly related to AI and I only see that growing.
It is merely that it seems difficult, if not impossible, to square “purpose” with enthusiastic AI use given that the former relies on authenticity and the commercial need to save both brands and the planet, while the latter can be convincingly said to drive inauthenticity and externalised costs at the expense of, well, both brands and the planet.
I have never been a fan of the slightly sneering holier-than-thou attitude that “purpose” proponents so often bring to the conversation, but one would at least have expected some consistency.
Ultimately, beyond the inflated profitability claims, “purpose” was sold as a way of making brands more meaningful, more human, and more trustworthy. Yet the moment a technology arrives that promises efficiency at scale, many of the same people who spent years lecturing the rest of us seem remarkably quick to forget the hymn sheet – all of which adds up to the feeling that some of us have had for years, namely that the whole “purpose” thing was and remains less a movement than a jingle.
Back in 1971, Coca-Cola wanted to teach the world to sing in perfect harmony. Half a century later, the lesson is that a catchy chorus travels well, especially when everyone else is already singing along.
