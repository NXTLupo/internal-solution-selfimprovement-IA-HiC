# How to Pick the Right Paint Color for Any Room, According to Designers

Source: [veranda](https://www.veranda.com/decorating-ideas/color-ideas/a73484243/how-to-pick-paint-colors-for-any-room/)

Sometimes when a room is beckoning for a makeover, it doesn’t necessarily mean it needs an entirely new suite of furnishings, but rather a fresh coat of paint. Compared to other expenses, painting a room is a rather inexpensive change that can instantly transform the look and ambiance of a room.
“Paint allows you to express your personality and make a space your own,” says Kerrie Kelly, CEO and creative director of Kerrie Kelly Design Studio.
But when you’re mulling over hundreds of paint swatches and color combinations, you may not know where to begin. With so many options, how to pick the right paint color for any room can seem a bit intimidating. How do you know which precise shade to choose, especially if you’re not updating the entire room with new window coverings or furniture? Do you go bold and energetic? Or is calm and soothing a better choice? And what if you hate the new color once it’s on the walls?
Never fear: There are a few expert ways to narrow down your choices. For starters, there’s not only one right color. “The truth is that there are probably dozens of nearly identical paint colors that will work,” says Bethany Adams, principal, Bethany Adams Interiors. “Don’t stress about finding the perfect one. Instead, focus on finding one that ticks the boxes of what you need it to do for you.”
Whether that means swapping out a tired shade for a fresh, new hue, brightening up a room with a lighter shade, or making the space more moody and fun, it’s the right color if it meets your primary goal. If you’re trying to decide how to pick the perfect paint color for any room, follow these tips from some of our favorite designers so you can choose a color you’ll love for years.
How to Pick the Best Paint Color for Your Room
Look in your closet.
“What colors do you wear?” asks Kelly. “Think about what colors you feel good in or tend to gravitate toward.” If you love navy, look at all the various hues of this color as options for a room, or consider using navy as an accent color for fabrics or furniture while choosing a lighter shade of blue for walls.
Think about what colors you don’t like.
Not a fan of terra cotta or blush pink? “If you have a strong opinion about certain colors, take those off the table,” says Kristina Phillips, principal and owner of Kristina Phillips Interior Design. “Color has an emotional component, so don’t choose a color you dislike, even if it’s popular or trending.”
Focus on the mood you want to create.
Think about how you want the room to feel. “If you want light and cheerful, look to whites, pale pinks, and pale yellows,” says Adams. “Dark and moody? Dark blue, green, or aubergine. Vibrant and energetic? Red, orange, or an intense green. Once I’ve honed in on a color and intensity, I’ll pull several different samples to try.”
Work with what you have.
It’s difficult to make a room into something it isn’t—such as bright and airy if it only has one window. “I always start with what natural light is doing already in the room,” says Avery Cox of Avery Cox Design. “If it’s a bright room or faces east, I will narrow down the selections to something with a warm undertone. If the room is small and dark already, I will lean into that and select colors that enhance the existing condition.”
Tie the color into your current décor.
If you won’t be updating the entire space with new window and floor coverings or upholstered furniture, you’ll need to coordinate the paint with these furnishings. “If there are drapes or a rug you’re keeping in the room, pull out an unexpected color from the existing patterns,” says Shannon Eddings of Shannon Eddings Interiors.
Coordinating with your existing palette also helps a space feel more cohesive. “Consider shading up or shading down from one of the foundational colors in your textiles,” says Ashley Macuga of Collected Interiors. “This will ensure that you get a sense of contrast, and it gives the eye a backdrop to see the beauty of the details in the space.”
Match the color to the room’s function.
Allowing natural light and existing architecture to guide your final color decision yields the best results, but consider how you’re going to use the room, too. “Generally, calming or gentle shades are great for rooms where the primary function is rest and relaxation. Think pastels, neutrals, or dark shades for bedrooms,” says Cox. “Rooms that prioritize entertainment can handle a color with more activation such as orange or bright yellow.”
Think about the sheen.
Consider which sheen to use, whether it’s flat, eggshell, semi-gloss, or high gloss. “The finish can change every color,” says Phillips. And be realistic: A lacquer or high-gloss sheen is striking, but it requires a smooth-as-silk wall because it will show every surface imperfection. It’s also not great if you have kids whose hands end up on the walls. If you like the drama of high gloss, consider painting the trim in this sheen to add a little punch.
Test the paint before committing.
Every designer emphasizes this absolutely critical step. “You must always sample a color on the walls,” says Adams. “How a color looks on a little chip, held horizontally, is not how it will look on your walls, at a vertical angle. Your existing woodwork, lighting, natural light, and even furnishings can all lend a cast to the paint, and you won’t know what it’s going to look like until you try it out.” Also, be sure to test the paint in the exact sheen you’re considering, and paint on several coats, just as you would for the finished product.
You also can try one of the online color visualization tools, which most paint companies offer on their websites. Upload a photo and try on a few different colors, just like you would clothes in a dressing room. “You shouldn’t rely on digital tools alone, but they can be a good way to narrow down choices,” says Macuga.
Don’t forget about the ceiling and trim.
“The ceiling is another wall, and it’s often painted white, but that can look gray and dingy,” says Phillips. “I like to tint ceiling paint with a little blue so that it looks more white and clean.” Trim can be painted the same shade or a slightly different shade for contrast and interest.
Live with the sample for a few days.
Your initial reaction may not be how you feel in a few days. Light shifts at different times of day and can totally change our reaction to a color. “It’s important to see if you still like a color three days after you’ve chosen it,” says Cox. “Our brains are hardwired to be intrigued by new ideas, but that doesn’t mean they’re right.”
If you’re stuck, go neutral.
If you find you can’t commit or simply can’t find a color that speaks to you, stick with a neutral paint color as your base. “Do all white walls, then express your personality with statement fabric, rugs, or artwork,” says Kelly.
Trust your instincts.
A gut feeling or emotional response is a strong clue that you’ve found the right color. “More often than not, a client will say something like, ‘This color makes me so happy,’” says Eddings. “Sometimes it’s just that we have looked at enough options to feel confident that the one we continue to go back to is ‘the one.’” And after all, it’s your space; if you love it, go with it!
