# How to Choose Paint Colors *Exactly* Like a Designer

Source: [veranda](https://www.veranda.com/decorating-ideas/advice-from-designers/a73338756/designer-paint-colors-guide/)

When narrowing down the paint plan for a recent project, Manhattan-based designer Elizabeth Lawrence requested no less than 85 samples to test on site, including lots of fun colors—the client works in the art world, a professional color lover—and a smattering of neutrals. “The contractor, who is lovely, said, ‘But there are so many different white trims,’” Lawrence recalls. “And I said, ‘Exactly, because white is not just white.’”
Paint is nuanced, finicky, and dependent on the environment around it. As English antiquarian and designer Max Rollitt says, “There is no prescription.” A paint chip in the home improvement store may seem the one, but coat your walls in it, and you could be looking at a completely different hue (hence the need for all those samples!).
The good news? The same elements that make paint tricky also make it one of the most powerful and exciting tools in decorating.
To help you navigate the (seemingly endless) sea of swatches and sheens, three design stars share the strategies they swear by, from working with tricky shades to harnessing high-gloss finishes and building a palette that feels true to you and your home.
Meet the Experts
In Elizabeth Lawrence’s studio apartment days, her tiny kitchen was a testing ground for paint, the walls morphing from yellow to melon-y pink and topped with fresh art with each iteration. Today, she brings her distinguished, thoughtful, and creative design approach to Williams Lawrence, which she helms with luminary Bunny Williams. The duo’s new book Decoration hits shelves on September 8 and is available now for presale (you’re gonna want to jump on this one—it’s one for the library).
Furniture restoration laid the foundation for Max Rollitt’s design career, shaping his enduring appreciation for history, patina, and craftsmanship. From his grain store-turned-interiors mecca in Hampshire, England, where his design studio, antiques gallery, and bespoke furniture business are housed, he creates timeless interiors, like this 19th-century stone estate, informed by that same deeply rooted sensibility.
Born in England, educated in Canada, and based in New York, designer Kate Reid is a partner at Marshall Watson & Reid Deane Ganes, where she has brought her palpable, infectious, and inspiring love for color (her own floral- and color-soaked Victorian-era apartment is a testament) to projects across the globe for more than 20 years.
Assess Your Hopes and Dreams
It’s not just about the colors you love—it’s about considering which colors will look best on your walls. In early design discussions, Reid gears the focus toward finding out what hues her clients gravitate toward, yes, but also “what their dreams are, what their hopes are, and what they’re envisioning.” You should make time for this, too: think about what excites you about your space and start there.
Read the Room
All three designers stressed that selecting paint begins with observing each room on site, taking note of the natural light the home receives, ceiling heights, any decorative or structural details, architectural style and age, and overall character. “Architecturally, if we’re going historic, then I tend to use sort of grubbier colors,” Rollitt notes. “I’m not a purist in my approach, but historically, old paint was never light, it was never pure, there was lots of lead in it. So often, white was excluded, because it was very expensive and showed the dirt.” Formality plays another crucial role. “If it’s an Upper East Side apartment, then there’s a great case to be made for making the walls much more shiny and elegant, and bringing in a lot more gloss, whereas in a recent Utah project we did, it was much more rustic,” Reid recalls.
Take Stock of Your Surroundings
What’s outside the walls is of equal significance. “It’s really important to consider the light that’s coming into the house, and that’s affected by where you are geographically and what the environment is outside,” Rollitt says. “If you’re in the middle of the countryside, you have a lot of reflected green light; if you’re in an urban environment, there’s a lot of gray reflection. Where you are within the world affects it enormously.”
The natural environment not only impacts the way light comes in, but it also serves as an excellent muse. Selecting colors from the landscape itself helps the interior feel rooted in place. “For a project in Utah, we did a red kitchen, which works, because the land is red,” Reid says. “When you bring those colors into the house, it becomes an inside-outside situation.”
Keep an Eye Out for Difficult Spaces
Very bright rooms. “These are very tricky,” Lawrence says. “They are hard for a whole host of reasons.” Chief among them: “Sun-soaked spaces change what your colors look like once applied,” she explains. “And because bright light is going to fade all of your things.”
Light-restricted spaces. “You have to be careful,” says Rollitt of darker rooms, like those underground, “and I think you could almost go brighter or even a little fluorescent, otherwise you’re just molding down there,” he jokes. Reid concurs and calls for “shine, shine, shine. You want to bring light in and make spaces feel bigger than they are.” Her number one trick: gloss. “A big thing is sheen level, to bring them as much life as possible.”
Approach Some Colors With Caution.
Our three experts delight in using color and lots of it, but warn that some shades require a bit more care and consideration.
Yellow: “The thing is, yellow works at certain times of year” and then not as much in others, Rollitt says. “It can make you feel ill.” But there’s a ton of examples of historic yellow rooms that not only shine, but are instructive in their beauty, so it’s about finding a shade that works year-round in your house and with your levels of natural sunlight. He uses Farrow & Ball’s India Yellow as an accent on woodwork and notes yellow fabric can be lovely on walls.
Pink: Because pink paint can read as similar to some skin tones, this one “can go horribly wrong,” Rollitt says. He likes to pair dusty pinks, like Farrow & Ball’s Dead Salmon, with a glossy white “to breathe life into it.”
Green: This can be a bit thorny. Says Reid, “You want to think about what the color is going to do to people.” For instance, green walls can cast an unappealing tint on skin if the room is not balanced with warm materials, rich textures, and flattering light. “You can do a green room, you can do a yellow room,” Reid says. “You just have to really consider all the other aspects that are going into it.”
Advice for Every Space
For powder rooms: “It’s the only room where guests will lock themselves in, be nosy, and take selfies,” Lawrence jokes. “So don’t be afraid to be bold and pick glossy colors and a great mirror.”
For kitchens: With a rainbow of ranges, fridges, coffee makers, and more on the market, kitchens are opening up to a world of color like never before, a development Reid and Lawrence both celebrate. Lawrence just finished a mossy green creation, and Reid’s own kitchen is a two-tone, berry-hued triumph. Meanwhile, Rollitt delights in a classic white kitchen so he can layer lots of framed art on top.
For dining rooms: “In dining rooms, what works well are these warmer tones, like terra cotta, that work well against skin and candlelight,” Rollitt says. “They create a really warm environment.”
For libraries: “This is a place where you can really go for it,” Reid says. Her trade secret: adding high-gloss paint to cabinetry for a woodwork wow moment that makes the curiosities inside “stand out like jewels.”
For bedrooms: Think of the best boutique hotels, where every room has its own personality and guests compare notes and give private tours. Lawrence encourages bringing that same sense of surprise to bedrooms at home. Because they are separated by hallways versus open sightlines like living areas are, sleeping quarters are the perfect place to play with color.
Build Your Palette
Gather every finish that will live in the room—fabrics, rugs, cabinetry, flooring, trims—and look at them together. “I sit down with my whole team, the house plans, the elevations, numerous paint decks, the fabric selections, et cetera,” Lawrence says. “It’s detailed. It takes hours. A lunch break is needed.” Let paint be your helper here. “Paint is the great coordinator—it ties everything together,” Reid says, adding that since paint is available in virtually every imaginable shade, it’s often the easiest design element to adjust. “So if the client’s fallen in love with a beautiful Tabriz rug, a paint color that harmonizes it with the rest of the room is the way that you can incorporate it.”
Find Your Common Thread
Even the most freewheeling color ethos needs cohesion. “There should be a consistent color story through the home,” Reid says. “I love color so much, but it has to all tie together.” After all, a room does not exist in isolation, and neither should your color scheme.
“What’s important is the journey you’re creating through the house,” Rollitt says. “Your hallways and main passageways become the core that grounds the home, and from there you can begin to play.” Reid suggests tying rooms together by applying the same color to all the trim or doors, for example. “You can have different experiences room to room, but our houses should feel cohesive. I think it disarms and relaxes people when they enter rooms and it doesn’t feel jarring.”
Take Samples to the Site
“There’s no way you can pick paint colors if you are not at the site. It’s a cardinal rule,” Reid says. Paint appears differently depending on the light, architecture, and surrounding finishes. “A paint color in the Bahamas is going to look completely different than it will in New York City or California or Utah. The change might be incremental, but the increments are so important,” Reid says.
