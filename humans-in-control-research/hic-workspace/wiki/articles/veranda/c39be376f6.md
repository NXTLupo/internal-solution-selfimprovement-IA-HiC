# How to Decorate With Warm and Cool Colors Like a Designer

Source: [veranda](https://www.veranda.com/decorating-ideas/color-ideas/a62830063/warm-and-cool-colors/)

Selecting the colors that fill our homes is one of the most personal choices we can make. Some of us may know we want a sunny yellow kitchen, a monochromatic library bursting with emerald hues, or a bedroom drenched in soothing blues. However, knowing the exact paint colors and varying tones to use within a room’s palette certainly is surprisingly tricky to understand. That’s why understanding warm and cool colors can go a long way when establishing a room’s atmosphere.
What Are Warm Colors?
Joa Studholme, color curator for Farrow & Ball, defines warm colors—such as red, orange, and yellow—as “colors which are often associated with fire, the sun, and heat.” She adds, “They can make an area feel closer and more intimate, and also create a sense of energy or excitement.”
Farrow & Ball Global Brand Ambassador Patrick O’Donnell says that warm colors in particular can be a “great ally” in poorly lit spaces, such as north-facing rooms or areas with small windows.
“Mid-tone warm colors, such as soft caramels, gentle earthy pinks, or warm yellow-based whites, work well in almost any environment,” he adds.
What Are Cool Colors?
On the other hand, interior designer Summer Thornton says that cool colors literally make you feel “cool or crisp when you see them.” In shades of blue, green, and purple, Studholme agrees with Thornton’s assessment, saying that these hues “give off a cold and clear atmosphere.”
“These can include blues, whites, and grays, resulting in rooms that are often sharper and flatter in feel but can also have a soothing appeal,” Studholme observes.
How Color Temperature Relates to Warm and Cool Colors
Color temperature is defined as a scale that measures how warm or cool a light from a source is. Specifically, as Bethany Adams, principal at Bethany Adams Interiors, puts it, color temperature refers to the color your light bulbs give off. She says that nowadays, LED lights come in a variety of color temperatures.
“What most people don't realize is that the higher the number kelvin, the cooler the light, which means the cooler everything in your home will look,” she explains. “So, if you choose your paint color under a 2700K light, and then paint it and install 4,000K light bulbs, the color you have on your walls will look entirely different, and much, much cooler than what you originally selected.”
She adds that as a rule, she installs bulbs with a 2700K color temperature “because it is the most flattering light to people and furnishings,” meaning that it will work with either a warm or cool color palette.
How Warm and Cool Colors Can Impact Mood and Emotions
These two color categories can also affect our emotions and overall moods. For instance, Thornton believes that warm colors “feel like a hug—they feel safe, they surround you, and calm you,” she says. Thornton goes on to say that cool colors feel more energizing and “more like daytime.” She adds, “They can inspire you to accomplish and feel crisp and pulled together.”
Studholme notes that lighter cool colors can feel tranquil and calm, while stronger cool tones can result in a more formal feeling in spaces (think: a lacquered eggplant lounge).
“Of course, one home generally incorporates both warm and cool tones, and things can shift seasonally, but people tend to lean either warm or cool. This can tell you a lot about a person,” Thornton says.
How to Decorate With Warm and Cool Colors
Thornton says that while the preference really comes down to each individual, she finds that in general, warm colors work best for spaces that are meant to embrace you, such as a cozy den or library, whereas cool colors work well in an office or a kitchen.
But it also can come down to your goals for a space. Thornton says that for a bedroom, morning people tend to like cool colors because they can energize and prepare them for the day ahead. Evening people may see their private spaces as a place to relax and unwind at the end of the day and prefer a warmer palette.
Before anything else, Adams says that it’s important to identify which colors make you feel comfortable. And you may not have to choose between cool and warm hues.
“You may find that it's a mix of both warm and cool,” she says. “If you do like both warm and cool colors—great! You can absolutely employ them in the same space. Just allow one to be the star and one to be the accent.”
Studholme adds that a small amount of a warm color can act as a perfect accent color, which can “totally change the atmosphere and make it feel significantly more friendly.” Or you can go all in with a warmly hued wall color.
“Nothing warms the heart like a door painted in rich Eating Room Red or a window reveal in rich Babouche, which will make your space appear to be full of sunshine,” she says. “Soft warm tones on ceilings like Pink Ground or Hay can change chilly spaces to those with a much more inviting ambiance.”
Studholme also says that cool, strong hues can add a “sense of chic” to many spaces if used in moderation.
“When it comes to cooler colors, I believe the rules are more straightforward,” O’Donnell says. “While no one wants a room to feel cold, cooler shades—especially the paler ones—benefit from ample natural light. Think coastal living, where soft, pale, icy blues on the walls work beautifully due to the environment. These shades also contribute to a cleaner, more decorative aesthetic, requiring a simpler palette.”
