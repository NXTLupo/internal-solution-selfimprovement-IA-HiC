# Inside a North Carolina Lake House That Offers a Striking Rebuttal to the All-White Kitchen

Source: [veranda](https://www.veranda.com/decorating-ideas/house-tours/a73415899/dungan-anderson-north-carolina-lake-house/)

Nothing is perfect and everything is perfect in nature, as the saying goes. Architect Jeffrey Dungan came face-to-face with this duality when a couple approached him to design a second home for their young family on Lake Norman in North Carolina. The perfect: a serene waterfront property protected by trees. The imperfect: a long, narrow lot with several pinch points, whose shape Dungan likens to “a gerrymandered voting district.”
Yet he accepted the geometric challenge. “I seized upon the site for inspiration, for dictation. The lot tells me how to design the house,” he says. “There are views we want to shield against, and there are views we want to celebrate. And I think by the time you solve for all those problems, if you’re really paying attention, then the house lays itself out, in some ways, for you,” says Dungan, whose second book, The Soulful House, publishes on September 1.
As he focused on capturing the natural light and framing water vistas, a rambling home unfolded in stone, glass, and wood. “It’s almost like a dance,” Dungan says of the design. Each piece of the floor plan shifts slightly from the previous one as it stretches along the property, highlighting views of the lake from different angles at every turn. “There’s a whole other level of discovery that should happen, needs to happen, when you think about what it’s going to be like to move through a house,” he says.
A glass-enclosed passageway opens to a soaring stair hall. Richly stained hewn beams reach across the great room ceiling like a warm hug. Sunlight pouring through tall windows illuminates the all-black kitchen—an act of decorative rebellion by Dungan—replacing any heaviness with a surprising sense of brightness and calm. “These elements tell the story of the house,” he says.
Designer Sean Anderson stepped in to carry Dungan’s narrative throughout the interiors. “I wanted what I was offering and integrating into these environments to complement the architecture and continue the language that had already been developed,” says Anderson, whose debut monograph, Interior Narratives, also publishes on September 1.
In the great room, for instance, he followed the architect’s sight lines to devise a furniture plan for the long, linear space. “Your eye should never fight your natural movement through a room. I think you should innately know how to travel through a space. It’s part of how we maneuvered where the furniture would land,” he says. Nothing could rest against the walls of windows, so Anderson floated one seating area beside the bar and another in front of the fireplace, comfortably filling the room while maintaining outdoor views.
Dungan’s selection of materials offered Anderson a neutral canvas. “The home is crafted in such a way that it is flooded with natural light all day long from every angle, so I got to really play with darker hues,” he says. He pitched staining the main level’s poplar ceiling a deep brown hue, which Dungan initially worried might feel too heavy but instead brought instant warmth to the tumbled limestone floors.
Anderson reached for earthy shades of terra-cotta and mossy green to recall the surrounding environment while also warding off any sense of coldness. “Jeffrey and I share a love of materiality and texture,” Anderson says. He played off the roughness of the architecture’s stone and wood with softer textiles, introducing plush shearling, nubby bouclé, and thick linens. “The texture study also helped us achieve warmth and livability,” he says.
The free-spirited homeowners encouraged Anderson to experiment with unique furniture and lighting, which stand like sculptures throughout the home. “My hope for this family is that pieces like these live with them and help to create lasting memories,” he says. A vintage Mexican rope chair, for example, converses with a kidney-shaped sofa in the great room, while in the dining alcove, a sleek metal console with a graffiti-like patina floats against the plastered-masonry walls. Above it hangs a textural chandelier crafted from resin-bonded sand by artist Steven Haulenbeek. “Lighting is my baby,” Anderson says. “I love pairing new contemporary fixtures with old vintage ones. It creates more visual interest and a more storied approach, especially for a new build, where you don’t have that history somewhat ingrained into the structure already.”
Any seriousness of the design is tempered by the stainless steel slide swooping through the house, beginning by the children’s rooms, twirling through the craft area, and ending in the great room. “We made it like a piece of modern sculpture moving through these spaces. You not only get to experience it, but you get to enjoy the funky shape,” Dungan says.
After all, what’s a lake house if not a destination for letting loose and escaping the city’s stresses? The homeowners’ four kids aren’t the only ones taking a turn.
Front Exterior
Dungan pulled inspiration from a site’s location to design a lake house that feels in harmony with its environment. “Like you couldn’t imagine that little piece of the Earth’s crust without the house,” he says.
Great Room
Sculptural Coup d’Etat chairs dressed in plush shearling make cozy fireside perches.
Dining Alcove
A textural Steven Haulenbeek chandelier, crafted from sand-bonded resin, illuminates the dining alcove. “The chairs and floating console are clean and sleek to let the chandelier have its moment,” says Anderson.
Kitchen
Dungan was tired of all-white kitchens. “I would even say sick of it, maybe nauseous of it,” he says. And so were the homeowners. The architect devised a moody scheme with dark-stained millwork and Jatoba Brown stone surfaces. “Look past the very first glance and pay closer attention to the details. They’re all whispering and talking to you,” says Dungan.
Breakfast Nook
The breakfast nook looks out over a leafy canopy. Anderson pulled in earth tones to reflect the lakeside setting.
Pantry
The richly stained millwork extends into the pantry, which Anderson grounded with a Tuareg rug for another layer of texture and pattern.
Laundry Room
The laundry room’s terra-cotta millwork (painted Ralph Lauren’s Oxfordshire) contrasts the soapstone countertops. A sheer linen shade dresses the window. “The clients wanted the shades to feel light and airy while also expressing a mood,” Anderson says.
Primary Bedroom
The primary bedroom’s walls of windows give the space a treehouse-like feel. The room is coated in a deep espresso hue (Ralph Lauren’s Turret Stair). “Because we had the natural light, we could achieve that same kind of airiness and go darker,” Anderson says. A contemporary chandelier by Reduxr stretches across the ceiling. “I love interesting lighting and knew this vast space could get very dark at night, so I wanted to make sure the homeowners had ample light,” he says.
Primary Bathroom
A clay soaking tub by Studio LOHO fits snugly into an alcove of the primary bathroom.
Bedroom
A tartan quilt brings playfulness to one of the children’s bedrooms while maintaining the home’s mood.
Craft Room
The stainless steel slide weaves through the children’s craft room, painted Benjamin Moore’s Jungle Canopy.
Outdoor Dining Area
“Outdoor spaces can be very tricky, especially in the South’s humidity and sun,” Anderson says. The weather-resistant dining chairs feature bolster-like backs, nodding to the curvy furniture used throughout the interiors. “I was also leaning into the modernity and materiality that we were seeing elsewhere and expressing some of that with the organic forms,” he says.
