# Friday Fragments: Connecting the Dots

Source: [Inside Higher Ed (RSS)](https://www.insidehighered.com/opinion/columns/confessions-community-college-dean/2026/11/06/friday-fragments-connecting-dots)

Inside Higher Ed
In my radio days, we had a running joke about a “collision mix.” That was when two songs played back-to-back made absolutely no sense next to each other. Sometimes they were intentional, but mostly they resulted from us having more enthusiasm than competence.
I had a collision mix this week reading two articles about college vice presidents. The more recent one from Community College Daily (published by the American Association of Community Colleges), addresses how important, but difficult, it is to find and keep good vice presidents and deans. It lists multiple skill sets and nods toward economic circumstances, but seems determined to put a happy face on what is actually a much more complex challenge.
The other ran a few months ago in IHE. In conversation with Rachel Toor, serial president Gordon Gee offered this:
In my experience, any new president—particularly one coming from the outside—needs to think very carefully, and fairly quickly, about building their own team. I would almost always replace the provost, because any good provost sitting there really wanted my job and was disappointed not to get it."
Gosh, why would talented and dedicated people hesitate to step into a role in which they could be kicked to the curb at any moment regardless of performance? Truly a mystery. We may never know. That’s especially true in the community college world, where admins hired from the outside typically don’t receive faculty rank. Here, being let go doesn’t mean returning to the classroom; it means being unemployed. I read a while back that the average length of service of a chief academic officer at a college or university in the U.S. is three years. (I didn’t know that when I started out.) Given that it takes about a year in the role to get your bearings, that’s not great.
As I learned the hard way early in my career, “pet or threat” is a real thing. This is not a dynamic that encourages good people to step up.
Having been in multiple provost-equivalent roles under multiple presidents—though never with that title, for whatever annoying reason—I’ve seen two flavors of president. One sees talent on their team as a threat; the other sees it as an asset. The former are toxic, and the latter are blessings. If you want good vice presidents and deans, hire presidents who don’t see them as threats. I’m lucky enough to have one now, and it makes all the difference.
And no, we don’t all want to be presidents. We want to work for good ones.

The Girl is racking up lessons in the school of hard knocks.
Her career goal is to be a librarian, either at a college/university or at some sort of research facility. She had an internship at the Folger Shakespeare Library while in college, where she learned about handling delicate archival materials and had a blast.
After a few months in New York City, she finally got an offer, albeit a part-time one, at a private library. Being part-time, it pays terribly, so she would need to supplement her income with another job. She’s OK with that, and she has already been working at a downtown restaurant. So far, so good.
But the 23 hours a week the library offered her are at awkward fixed times, and the restaurant requires greater and more fluid availability. She has tried finding another part-time job to make the library gig possible, but nothing has come through. (The distribution of hours at the library pretty much prevents much else.) The restaurant pays a lot better than the library, and she has rent to pay. So she did what she had to do, rescinding her acceptance of the library job in service of eating.
Sometimes, adulting can really suck.
I keep hearing about a “low hire, low fire” economy. From what I’m seeing, that’s partially true. There’s plenty of hiring in the low-wage, part-time sector, but much less in jobs that pay a living wage. (Higher education isn’t immune to that, obviously.) If you have a full-time job that you like, it’s not terrible, but if you’re trying to break in outside of health care and a few skilled trades—like, say, you’re a 22-year-old college grad—it’s brutal.
As brilliant as she is, I can’t help but assume that she’ll break through soon. Talent like that has to attract notice at some point. I just hope it comes soon enough to keep her spirits up.

The midterm election was wild. It’s normal for the president’s party to lose seats at midterm, but losing this many is far outside the norm. I was particularly struck at some of the states that were competitive.& South Carolina? Mississippi? I didn’t expect either of those.
During my brief sojourn in Iowa, I got to know Josh Turek a little. He struck me as a genuinely good guy; if I still lived there, I would have happily voted for him. As it happened, Pennsylvania didn’t have a senate seat up for election this year, and the gubernatorial election wasn’t really in question, so I mostly followed other states.
Midterms aren’t perfect predictors of general elections, of course; as the last two years showed, a lot can happen in two years. But it did make me wonder about the staying power of some of the more …ambitious? … new regulations promulgated by the federal Department of Education.
All of that said, I can’t help but draw the connection between The Girl’s travails and the blue wave of the election. People aren’t happy with the economy, and when that happens, they blame the party in power. (Given that she lives in New York City without a car, gas prices don’t affect her much directly. But the low hire market absolutely does.) They also (rightly) resent the myriad excesses committed in the name of immigration enforcement. A divided government isn’t likely to make a massive positive change, but the fact of division may prevent some of its more mercurial elements from going entirely off the rails. That’s something.
In the meantime, though, rent comes due faster and more often than political change.
