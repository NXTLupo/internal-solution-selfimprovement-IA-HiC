# Organizational Chart Examples 2026: 6 Structure Types (With Free Templates)

Source: [piktochart_blog](https://piktochart.com/blog/organizational-chart-examples-2026/)

An organizational chart is one of those documents every company needs and almost nobody enjoys building. You know the shapes, you know the boxes, but turning a mental map of “who reports to whom” into something clean enough to hand to new hires, investors, or the board takes more design effort than it should.
This guide walks through the six organizational structures most companies actually use, with a real-world example of each, then hands you free, ready-to-edit templates so you can build your own in minutes instead of starting from a blank canvas.
What Is an Organizational Chart?
An organizational chart (org chart) is a visual diagram that shows the internal structure of a company: who reports to whom, how departments relate to each other, and where decision-making authority sits. It typically uses boxes to represent roles or people, connected by lines that show reporting relationships.
Org charts aren’t just an HR formality. They get used for:
- Onboarding — helping new hires understand the team and who to go to for what
- Fundraising and investor decks — showing a clear, credible leadership structure
- Restructuring — mapping the “before” and “after” when teams change
- Succession planning — identifying gaps and backup coverage for key roles
- Client-facing proposals — introducing your team on a project or account
Why a Clear Org Chart Matters
A messy or outdated org chart causes real friction: new employees don’t know who owns a decision, cross-team requests stall because nobody’s sure which department is responsible, and leadership presentations look unpolished in front of investors or the board. A well-designed chart fixes all three by making reporting lines and ownership obvious at a glance, without anyone needing to ask.
6 Types of Organizational Structures, With Examples
Most org charts fall into one of six structural patterns. Picking the right one depends on your company’s size, how centralized decisions are, and how much cross-team collaboration you need.
1. Hierarchical (Top-Down) Structure
The most common structure. Authority flows from the top down: CEO at the top, then executives, then managers, then individual contributors, each level reporting to the one above it.
Example: A mid-sized manufacturing company with a CEO, a VP for each function (Operations, Sales, Finance), department managers under each VP, and floor staff or reps reporting to those managers.
Best for: Established companies with clear chains of command, or industries with regulatory reporting requirements.
Watch out for: Slower decision-making as requests move up and down the chain, and less visibility into cross-department work.
2. Functional Structure
Employees are grouped by specialty, marketing, engineering, sales, finance, rather than by product or region. Each function has its own manager, and those managers report to a central leadership team.
Example: A software company with distinct Engineering, Product, Marketing, and Customer Success departments, each led by a director who reports to the COO.
Piktochart’s Business Organizational Chart template is built for exactly this structure, with a clean purple-and-white layout designed to present department groupings clearly.
Best for: Companies that want deep expertise within each function and clear specialization.
Watch out for: Silos. Functional teams can lose sight of the bigger cross-company picture if collaboration isn’t deliberately built in.
3. Divisional Structure
The company is split into self-contained divisions, by product line, geography, or customer segment, each with its own functional teams underneath (its own marketing, its own finance, and so on).
Example: A global consumer goods company with separate divisions for North America, Europe, and Asia-Pacific, each division running its own sales, marketing, and operations teams.
Best for: Larger companies operating across multiple markets or product lines that need local autonomy.
Watch out for: Duplicated resources across divisions, since each one builds out its own functions independently.
4. Matrix Structure
Employees report to two managers at once, typically a functional manager and a project or product manager. It’s more flexible than a strict hierarchy but requires clear communication to avoid conflicting priorities.
Example: A marketing agency where a designer reports to the Head of Design (functional manager) and also to the account lead for the client project they’re staffed on (project manager).
Best for: Project-based industries like agencies, consultancies, and construction, where staff move between initiatives.
Watch out for: Dual reporting lines can create confusion over priorities if the two managers aren’t aligned.
5. Flat Structure
Few or no layers of middle management between leadership and staff. Common in early-stage startups where everyone works closely together and titles matter less than output.
Example: A 15-person startup where all employees report directly to the founder or one of two co-founders, with no department managers in between.
Best for: Small teams and early-stage companies that need speed and flexibility over formal process.
Watch out for: This structure breaks down fast as headcount grows past 20 to 30 people. It’s usually the first thing that gets restructured as a company scales.
6. Team-Based (Network) Structure
Instead of fixed departments, the company is organized into cross-functional teams (often called “pods” or “squads”), each with the mix of skills needed to own a specific goal or product area.
Example: A product company organized into squads, each with its own engineer, designer, and product manager, all reporting to a central product leadership team rather than sitting in separate departments.
Best for: Product and engineering-led companies that prioritize speed and cross-functional ownership over strict specialization.
Watch out for: Requires strong internal communication tools, since there’s no single functional manager keeping every discipline aligned.

Common Org Chart Mistakes to Avoid
- Letting it go stale. An org chart that doesn’t reflect last quarter’s hires or reorg is worse than no chart at all, it actively misleads people. Revisit it every time the team changes meaningfully.
- Overcomplicating small teams. If you’re under 20 people, resist the urge to invent a matrix structure. A flat chart is usually more honest and more useful.
- Ignoring dotted-line relationships. Matrix and team-based structures often have informal or secondary reporting lines. Leaving them off the chart hides real dynamics that new hires need to understand.
- Designing for yourself, not the reader. A chart built for an internal HR file needs less polish than one going into an investor deck. Match the level of design effort to where the chart will actually be seen.
Frequently Asked Questions
What software is best for making an org chart? Any tool that lets you edit boxes and connecting lines without needing design skills works well. Piktochart’s org chart templates are pre-built and fully editable, so you’re customizing an existing professional layout rather than building one from zero.
What’s the difference between a hierarchical and a flat org chart? A hierarchical chart has multiple layers of management between leadership and staff. A flat chart has few or none, with most employees reporting directly to a founder or senior leader. Company size is usually the deciding factor: flat works well under about 20 to 30 people, hierarchical becomes necessary beyond that.
Can one company use more than one org chart structure? Yes. It’s common for a company to run a functional structure overall while individual teams operate as matrix or team-based pods for specific projects. Large companies with multiple divisions often combine a divisional structure at the top level with functional structures inside each division.
