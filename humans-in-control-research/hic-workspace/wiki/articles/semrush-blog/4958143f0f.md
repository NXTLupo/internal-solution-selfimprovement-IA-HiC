# SEO for a new website: A sequencing guide for your first 90 days

Source: [semrush_blog](https://www.semrush.com/blog/seo-for-new-website/)

Starting SEO for a new website feels like being handed a 40-item to-do list and told not to break anything.
Luckily, you only have to do a few of the right things, in the right order. We'll walk you through exactly that, even if you're a complete beginner.
This guide treats SEO for a new website as a sequence: what to decide before you publish a single page, what to do in your first few weeks, and what can safely wait.
You'll also learn how to earn visibility inside AI answers like ChatGPT and Google's AI Overviews, where a fast-growing share of people now discover brands they've never heard of. ChatGPT alone reached 900 million weekly active users (OpenAI) in early 2026.
What SEO does for a new website
For a new website, SEO is less about how much work you do and more about the order you do it in, because early structural decisions get expensive to reverse once content and links pile on top of them.
What separates a new site that compounds from one that stalls is sequence. For example:
- Choosing your URL structure before you publish costs nothing. Changing it after 80 pages are indexed and linked means redirects, lost equity, and a week you'll never get back.
- Picking keywords you can realistically win before you write saves you from a library of well-crafted pages that never rank. The order is the strategy.
This matters more for a brand-new site than for an established one because you have no authority buffer to absorb mistakes. An established site can publish in the wrong order and still rank on domain strength. A site launched last month cannot.
SEO and AI search expert Bill Widmer often does SEO for new websites, and he puts it like this:
“Doing SEO in the right order from the start can be the difference between an afternoon of work and months of headache. I’ve seen it firsthand. Follow the sequence and you avoid that.”
Why SEO is important for a new website
SEO is how a brand-new website gets found by the people who don't yet know it exists, and starting early turns search into a compounding asset instead of a rescue project.
Search sends strangers who are actively looking for what you offer. And unlike paid ads, that traffic doesn't stop when you stop paying. A page that ranks keeps working for months or years after you publish it. That compounding result is why SEO is worth doing sooner rather than later.
For a new website specifically, two things make early SEO pay off.
- It builds equity you can't buy overnight, since authority accrues slowly and the sites that start sooner are further ahead when a buyer finally searches.
- It positions you for AI-driven discovery, because the same signals that help you rank also help AI systems find and cite you.
If you already have a few pages live, a quick SEO audit will tell you what to fix first.
How search engines (and AI answers) work
Search engines find your pages by crawling, store them by indexing, and order them by ranking.
- Crawling is when a bot like Googlebot follows links to discover your pages
- Indexing is when the search engine stores and understands those pages so they can be served later.
- Ranking is when it decides, for a given search, which indexed pages appear and in what order
If a page isn't crawled, it can't be indexed. If it isn't indexed, it can't rank. This is why the very first thing to confirm on a new site is that search engines can reach and index your pages, not whether you've optimized a title tag.
AI systems now add a fourth step: generating a response.
Tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google's AI Overviews pull passages from sources they trust and synthesize an answer, sometimes citing you by name. Showing up in these answers is an extension of SEO, called generative engine optimization (GEO) or answer engine optimization (AEO).
These AI tools draw on two sources of information, often blending them in a single answer:
- Their training data, the large snapshot of text they learned from when they were built. This is their internal memory. It's powerful, but frozen in time: it doesn't include anything published after the model's training cutoff, and it doesn't reliably attribute where a fact came from.
- Live web results, which they retrieve in real time to answer questions their training data can't. When a tool does this, it pulls passages from current pages and often cites the sources it used by name, which is where a new site can realistically show up.
Your new website SEO checklist: before and after launch
The cheapest SEO wins happen before launch, so lock in the structural decisions first and treat everything after launch as ongoing rather than one-time work.
Use this SEO checklist for a new website as the scannable map for the rest of the guide.
The left column is what to decide or set up before you publish a single page, because it's cheap to get right now and painful to change later. The right column is the continuous work that starts once you're live.
The same sequence applies to SEO for new websites of any kind, from a local service site to a SaaS product.
How to do SEO for a new website
To do SEO for a new website, work top to bottom through this sequence:
- Set up the technical essentials
- Lock your architecture
- Choose winnable keywords
- Create content worth citing
- Build authority
- Make yourself visible to AI answer engines
Each step assumes the one before it is done. You'll notice the early steps are decisions and setup, and the later steps are ongoing work, which is why you want to do them in this order.
Here's roughly how that sequence maps to your first 90 days:
1. Set up the technical essentials
Before anything else, make sure search engines can crawl and index your site, because no amount of keyword or content work matters if your pages can't be found.
It's what experienced practitioners check first, too. As Isaac Church, Director at 3WH, puts it:
"When our team kicks off SEO for a brand-new website, the first thing we check is indexing."
Start with the four accounts and files that give you eyes on your site.
- Create a robots.txt file to tell crawlers which pages to access and which to skip
- Set up Google Search Console (GSC) to see how Google crawls, indexes, and ranks your pages
- Submit an XML sitemap through GSC so search engines have a complete list of the pages you want indexed
- Connect Google Analytics 4 (GA4) to track how visitors behave once they arrive
On a new site, GSC won't show much at first, and that's normal: it takes time for Google to gather data, so set it up early and let it fill in.
Once your accounts are live, run a technical crawl with Site Audit, Semrush's technical SEO tool.
When the crawl finishes, head to the "Issues" tab, click the "Category" drop-down, and select "Indexability" to surface the technical problems that stop pages from being indexed, like noindex tags or blocked resources. Fix those first, since they gate everything else.
While you're here, confirm the site itself is technically sound:
- Serve every page over HTTPS
- Make sure the design is mobile-friendly (Google indexes the mobile version of your site first)
- Ensure your site loads fast (a Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds)
Site Audit flags these performance and page-experience issues alongside the indexability checks. See our SEO audit guide for the full step-by-step walkthrough.
2. Plan your site architecture and URLs
Decide how your pages are organized and how your URLs are structured before you publish. This is a decision that's easy today but difficult and time-consuming to reverse once content and links stack up.
Site architecture is the map of how your pages relate: which pages are top-level, which nest beneath them, and how a visitor (or a crawler) moves between them.
Sketch the skeleton before you build. Most new sites need a homepage, an about page, core product or service pages, a contact page, and a blog, with room to expand each into subpages. That skeleton tells both users and search engines what your site is about and how its parts connect.
Decide your URL slugs now, too. Will blog posts live at /blog/post-name or /articles/post-name? Will products sit under /category/product-name/ or at the root? Keep slugs short, descriptive, and keyword-focused. Avoid numbers and special characters.
Once a URL is indexed and other pages link to it, changing it means setting up redirects, and every redirect is a small tax on equity and a new chance to introduce errors. Get it right the first time and you never pay that tax.
3. Find keywords and prompts you can win
Keyword research is how you learn what your audience types into search, so you can build pages around terms worth ranking for. For a new site, the goal isn't the highest-volume keywords, it's the ones you can realistically rank for and that lead to revenue.
Use Keyword Magic Tool to find keyword ideas. Enter a seed keyword, your domain, and your target location. For each keyword you'll see its search volume (how often it's searched each month), search intent (informational, commercial, transactional, or navigational), and Personal Keyword Difficulty (PKD%), which estimates how hard it is for your specific site to rank.
On a brand-new domain, a good rule of thumb is to filter for a PKD% under 20 and look for terms with 100 or more monthly searches. Open the "Questions" filter to find long-tail queries, which are usually easier to win because they're more specific and less contested.
Use Keyword Gap to compare your domain against your competitors and see the keywords they rank for but you don't. This is a fast way to find winnable targets you'd otherwise miss.
After you have your target keywords, find the prompts your buyers type into AI tools. Prompts are longer and more conversational than keywords (e.g., "what's the best CRM for a two-person real estate team?" rather than "best CRM").
