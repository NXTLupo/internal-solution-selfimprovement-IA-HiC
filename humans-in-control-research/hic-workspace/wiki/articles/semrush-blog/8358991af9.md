# Internal links: ultimate guide + strategies

Source: [semrush_blog](https://www.semrush.com/blog/internal-links/)

A site with a deliberate internal linking strategy gives both users and crawlers a clear signal about which pages matter most.
But poor internal linking can lead to issues like orphaned pages, redirect chains, pages buried too deep in your site, and more — all of which can hurt your site's visibility.
Below, we'll cover what internal links are, why they matter for search and AI, how to build a link structure from scratch, and how to audit and fix the internal linking problems already living on your site.
What are internal links?
Internal links are hyperlinks that lead users to other pages on the same website and help search engines and AI crawlers understand a site's structure.

<a href="https://www.example-site.com/">fixing crawlability issues</a>Types of internal links
Internal links fall into two groups: structural links, which stay the same across your site, and contextual links, which change with your content.
Types of structural links include:
- Navigational links: Appear in your site's main menu
- Footer links: Placed in the site's footer area
- Breadcrumb links: Show a page's location within the site hierarchy
- Sidebar links: Located in the sidebar for easy navigation
And types of contextual links include:
- Call-to-action (CTA) links: Prompt conversions or actions within the text
- Image links: Hyperlinked images
- In-content links: Links within content like blog posts and web pages
Internal links vs. external links
Internal links direct users from one page of your site to another whereas external links point from your site to a page on another domain.
Internal links improve navigation, pass link authority among your pages, and help bots crawl your site. These links keep visitors within your domain, which can increase user engagement and conversion rates.
External links help you cite references and provide extra context. They also build trust and authority when you link to reputable sources. Getting external backlinks from other websites can improve your domain authority and rankings.
Why are internal links important for SEO?
Internal links help search engines, AI search platforms, and users navigate your site and pass authority to weaker pages.
- Search engines: Internal links show how your pages relate to each other, which tells Google which pages are most important. This distribution of link equity can boost the visibility of less prominent pages.
- AI search platforms:These systems retrieve and cite passages, not whole pages. When your cluster pages consistently point to one pillar, that pillar becomes the page most likely to get cited as the source on that topic.
- Users: A clear path to more information keeps users on your site longer, which gives them more chances to get familiar with your brand and convert
Let's take a deeper look.
1. Internal links help search engines understand your site's structure
Internal links help search engines understand your site’s structure as Google’s crawlers discover your pages by following internal links.
As Google explains: "Some pages are known because Google has already visited them. Other pages are discovered when Google extracts a link from a known page to a new page."

2. Internal links pass authority
Internal links pass authority to other internal pages, and a page that earns many backlinks (and thus has higher authority) can strengthen other pages through internal links.
Let's say Page A has lots of external links pointing to it, which means it likely has more authority via PageRank. This authority can then be passed to Page C and other connected pages by way of internal links.
Like this:
Identifying these pages helps you spread the authority your backlinks have already earned across the rest of your site.
However, keep in mind that only part of that authority is passed, and contextual relevance matters — links are most effective when they're aligned with user needs and content topics.
3. Internal links help users navigate between relevant pages
Internal links encourage visitors to navigate relevant pages which keeps them on your site longer, increases the likelihood of conversions, and helps them find the exact information they need.
Let's say a user lands on your article about "how to build a PC."
You can link to product pages for the parts you mention, or to related content like an article about "how to fix a PC fan."
The goal is to keep users on your site so they complete your desired action, like making a purchase, filling out a form, or buying a product.
Showing users relevant, useful content on the right pages is the perfect way to do that.
How to build your internal linking strategy
Building an internal linking strategy comes down to a few key moves, starting with your site structure and ending with the links you place on the page.
In this section, we'll walk through the seven-step process.
Grab our free topic cluster template to map your own structure as you go.
1. Plan your site structure
Planning your site structure (how your content is organized and linked) gives you categories and a logical hierarchy to help users and search engines move through your site.
A pyramid-like structure often works best:
- Top: Homepage or main pillar pages
- Middle: Subcategories or cluster pages
- Bottom: Specific content pages
Like this:
This structure is often called hub-and-spoke — the homepage or pillar acts as the hub, and the pages below are the spokes that link back up. You'll also see silo structures, which take the same hierarchy and keep each topic's pages linking mostly within their own category.
Whatever the label, the goal is the same: keep your most important pages just a click or two from the homepage, and organize supporting content into clear topics rather than scattering it across unrelated categories.
For more on structuring your site, see our guides to silo SEO and website architecture.
2. Identify pillar pages
Identify pillar pages (a comprehensive resource that covers a broad topic) that can link to more specific pages (known as cluster pages) to strengthen your site’s structure.
Pillar pages help you build topic clusters, which are groups of related content.
Pillar pages often target broad, high-volume keywords. Consider your pillar page the top of the marketing funnel. It provides general information and sparks interest.
For example, a retail site might have a pillar page for "washing machines." That page would link to sub-pages about specific washing machine types.
You can check the search volume for a candidate pillar topic in Semrush's Keyword Overview — "washing machines" gets 14,800 searches per month, which is exactly the kind of volume that justifies a dedicated pillar page.
Each section for the “washing machines” pillar topic covers a different type of washing machine, and then links to even more specific product categories. These product categories may link out to individual product pages.
Avoid making pillar pages too narrow. You need enough cluster pages to support them.
3. Create topic clusters
Creating topic clusters expands on your pillar topic in more detail, where each cluster links back to the pillar page to reinforce topical relevance.
For example, if your pillar is "copywriting," you might have clusters on "what is copywriting?" or "email copywriting tips."
This structure also serves AI search platforms well: When a system breaks a query into related sub-questions — a process called query fan-out — a pillar-and-cluster structure signals which page holds the deepest, most authoritative coverage of the topic.
You can also create clusters within clusters. For instance, the "email copywriting" cluster might include sub-pages about subject lines and CTAs. These pages should link back to the main pillar page to show that it's the primary resource on the topic.

4. Use authority pages to pass link equity
Authority pages (pages with high-quality backlinks) pass link equity to the pages they link to, meaning you can strengthen weak pages by including internal links from high-authority pages to weaker, relevant ones.
To do this effectively, identify your most authoritative pages using Semrush's Backlink Analytics.
Enter your domain and click "Analyze."
Click the "Indexed Pages" tab.
This report will display a list of your website pages, which are (by default) sorted by number of referring domains.
These are your highest-authority pages.
Export your results as a .csv or .xlsx file. Paste at least 10 of your high-authority pages into your strategy doc.
Add internal links from these pages to less authoritative pages on your site.
5. Support new content
Support new content by linking to it from relevant high-authority pages. New pages have few backlinks of their own, so internal links are what get them discovered and ranked.
To start, choose a new piece of content or a page that needs better performance. Then, look for relevant interlinking opportunities.
Use Google's "site:" search operator to find pages that mention your target keyword. For example, we might search for "site:semrush.com internal links" to find related articles.
Collect all relevant pages from the search results.
Paste those URLs into your strategy document.
Add links from each of those pages to your new document. Track your progress as you go.
Alternatively, Google Search Console's Links report shows which of your existing pages already discuss related topics, giving you another way to surface interlinking candidates
