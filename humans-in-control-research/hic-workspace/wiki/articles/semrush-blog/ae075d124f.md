# SEO strategy in 2026: how to build one (+ free workbook)

Source: [semrush_blog](https://www.semrush.com/blog/seo-strategy/)

An SEO strategy in 2026 has to account for a search landscape that changed quickly. Search results now mix AI summaries, videos, forum threads, and blue links.
Your buyers are turning to AI assistants like Gemini, ChatGPT, and Perplexity for the harder questions. Visibility now means showing up across those surfaces and being the one buyers trust, not just ranking on Google.
But the underlying job hasn’t changed. You’re still earning visibility in the places people search.
A working strategy is a set of business decisions, not a to-do list.
This article walks you through those six decisions, which are applicable to any business, in any vertical.
Every decision below has a matching exercise in the free SEO Strategy Workbook, so you can build your strategy as you read. If you'd rather think out loud, there's a copy-paste AI coaching prompt near the end you can drop into ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini.
How do you define an SEO strategy?
An SEO strategy is a plan for earning unpaid visibility across the systems people use to find answers, including search engines and the AI assistants built on top of them.
Four parts of that definition matter:
- Unpaid: This separates SEO from PPC, the paid ads on the search results page. Both show up on the same results page, but they run on separate budgets and get measured in completely different ways.
- Visibility: This is what powers search demand, even if it doesn’t drive clicks. Think about a zero-click answer, when Google answers the question right on the results page, so nobody clicks through. Or a citation in an AI summary, or a brand mention in a comparison thread. None of those sends a visit to your site, but any of them can shape whether the buyer converts a few weeks later.
- Systems people use to find answers: This covers search engines (Google, Bing), and the AI answers built into search (AI Overviews, Google AI Mode). Plus, the AI tools your customers are turning to (Gemini, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, etc.).
- Plan: A set of decisions you’ve committed to.
That last word, plan, is where most “SEO strategies” fall apart.
They’re typically inventories of activities. For example, publish four comparison posts, build ten backlinks, and add schema (the structured tags that help search engines understand your pages) to every URL.
None of that is wrong on its own. It just doesn't tell you whether those are the right activities for your business.

What’s inside an SEO strategy
Most SEO strategies work with the same set of operational components. You'll see these in most SEO advice, and each one sits inside one of the six decisions ahead (Decision 1 is the objective the others serve):
- Audience research: Understanding who you serve, what they’re trying to solve, and where they research it → Decision 2
- Keyword research: Pulling the queries customers type for the topics you compete on → Decision 3
- Competitor analysis: Studying what your competitors are publishing, ranking for, and earning traffic from → Decision 3 and Decision 4
- Authority (E-E-A-T): The experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust signals that earn visibility on Google and inside AI summaries → Decision 4
- Backlinks: Third-party links to your website that signal recognition from others in your category → Decision 5
- Technical SEO: The foundation that lets search engine crawlers reach every page, with structured data that helps systems understand what SEO is doing on each one
- Measurement: The set of signals you’ll watch to know whether the strategy is working → Decision 6
Knowing these terms is the easy part. The six decisions below are where you commit to what each one does for your business.
Decision 1 (Visibility): What does search visibility mean for your business?
For your business, search visibility has to add up to an objective: the business result you want from SEO.
A good answer is concrete. It forces trade-offs, and it sits close enough to revenue that someone outside marketing can tell whether it's working.
The trap is letting platform metrics (rankings, impressions, sessions, organic traffic) drive the answer. None is wrong to track, but they don’t tell you much about the business results you’re getting from search.
A page can rank #1 and never bring in a sale. You can post a record traffic quarter while search revenue falls.
Routedesk’s objective:
“We want to be the default consideration for mid-market teams evaluating a Zendesk alternative — the help desk that buyers in the 200-2,000 employee range already have in mind before they start comparing.”
This objective covers who (mid-market, 200-2,000 employees), what position (default consideration), and in what context (evaluating a Zendesk alternative).
Later in Decision 6, we’ll show you how to pick the signals that show how you’re progressing toward your goal.
Decision 2 (Audience): Who's your priority buyer, and where do they research?
Your priority buyer is defined less by demographics than by the situation they're in when they start looking, and where they go to research it. Pin down both.
Personas have their place. They’re useful for sales coaching or brand anchoring, and they’re easy to reference and remember.
For example, “In SMBs in the 20-200 employee range, the decision-maker is usually a director-level operator.”
But an SEO strategy needs to go further: what this customer is in the middle of when they come looking, where they go to figure it out, and in what order.
That’s the audience research that informs what content to create.
For example: “Mid-market IT and CX leaders trying to consolidate two or three help desk tools. They typically research on Reddit and G2 first, then validate on Google and ChatGPT.”
This describes a customer with a real problem, and their chosen research path.
Most of this comes from talking to customers and your sales team, reading support tickets, and watching how people move toward a decision.
To check where a category spends its time, Semrush's Audience Profile (in the Traffic & Market toolkit) can help. Its Behavior report breaks down the platforms a competitor's audience uses.
Zendesk's audience over-indexes on YouTube (68%), with Instagram and Facebook close behind. Reddit sits at 39%, which is high for a B2B category and a signal that community presence is worth taking seriously.
Just remember that you're looking at the full domain's audience, not your specific buyer. Use it to point you in a direction, then confirm with your own customers.
Surface order can vary. Focus on capturing the channels your customers spend the most time researching and validating in your category.
Depending on your brand's size, you could have multiple situations. Answer this question as many times as necessary.
Your answer becomes part of the brief for every piece of content: who you create for, where you publish, and what objections to handle. It also helps you say no to channels that don’t serve those buyers.
Decision 3 (Territory): What topics define your space?
Your territory is the set of topics you'll compete to own. Mapping it means seeing what your customers search, what already ranks when they do, and where the gaps are.
A topic is what your customer is trying to figure out. Queries are the words they type to get there.
Example topics:
- Comparing help desk software
- Handling an emergency leak
- Choosing a vitamin C serum
Pull the customer queries
Tools like Semrush, or even Google’s search suggestions, give you the keyword data.
For comparing help desk platforms, the queries might be “Freshdesk vs Zendesk,” “Zendesk alternatives,” “best help desk for SaaS.” It’s the same theme with different angles.
We looked up Routedesk's category, "help desk software," in Semrush's Keyword Magic Tool to see what the demand looks like.
A few things jump out:
- The seed keyword (the broad starting query) has 2,991 variations across 48,460 monthly searches in the US. Most of the demand sits in the long tail.
- "Best help desk software" shows up at a keyword difficulty (KD) of 40 (out of 100, where higher is harder to rank), with commercial intent (the searcher is ready to evaluate). This suggests a good opportunity.
- “Help desk software for small business” at KD 21 hints that segmenting by company size could be its own angle
- “Help desk software” itself is KD 52, too competitive to take on through organic rankings alone. But the topic might still be accessible through other channels: a strong YouTube video, an AI chat citation, or community presence on visible forums.
From here, you narrow in on your specific topics. For Routedesk, this includes a comparison topic, where the queries become “Freshdesk vs Zendesk,” “Zendesk alternatives,” “best help desk for SaaS.”

Review and reverse-engineer the search results
Start with the SERP (search engine results page). Go to Google, type one of the queries, and look.
- What formats are on the page? Examples include articles, videos, an AI Overview, comparison tables, Reddit threads, Maps, and knowledge panels.
- Who’s there? Vendor sites, review aggregators, communities, individual creators.
- If there’s an AI summary, what does it cite?
Analyzing the SERP gives you competitor intel at the strategic level.
