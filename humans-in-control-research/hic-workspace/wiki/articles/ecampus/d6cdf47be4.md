# Beyond the script: Why every faculty interview needs a few unexpected questions

Source: [ecampus](https://www.ecampusnews.com/campus-leadership/2026/10/30/beyond-the-script-why-every-faculty-interview-needs-a-few-unexpected-questions/)

Title: Beyond the script: Why every faculty interview needs a few unexpected questions

URL Source: https://www.ecampusnews.com/campus-leadership/2026/10/30/beyond-the-script-why-every-faculty-interview-needs-a-few-unexpected-questions/

Published Time: 2026-10-30T09:00:00+00:00

Markdown Content:
Faculty interviews are, by necessity, somewhat formulaic. Candidates expect questions about their teaching philosophy. They anticipate discussions about their research interests, publication agenda, student mentoring experience, commitment to diversity and inclusion, service contributions, and long-term professional goals. For many positions, they prepare responses about assessment practices, online instruction, interdisciplinary collaboration, grant activity, and the use of emerging technologies such as generative AI.

None of these questions are inappropriate. In fact, they are essential. The problem is that candidates have often rehearsed these answers dozens of times. By the time a finalist reaches a campus interview, they have likely refined their responses through multiple screening interviews, conversations with mentors, mock interview sessions, and extensive preparation. Search committees hear polished narratives that are informative but not always revealing. Candidates know the expected questions and have carefully crafted their answers.

**The value of the unexpected**

As a result, interviews sometimes become exercises in confirmation rather than discovery. One way to move beyond the script is surprisingly simple: ask a few unexpected questions. Unexpected questions create a moment when candidates must think rather than recite. That distinction matters. A well-designed interview should not merely assess whether candidates have prepared effectively. It should also provide insight into how they think, communicate, reflect, and respond when faced with novel situations. Faculty members routinely encounter unforeseen challenges. They must respond to difficult student questions, adapt to changing institutional priorities, engage in scholarly debate, and navigate complex interpersonal relationships.

The ability to think on one’s feet is often just as important as technical expertise. Unexpected questions can help reveal these qualities. For example, asking a candidate, “What was the last book you read?” may initially seem unrelated to the position. Yet the answer can provide valuable insight into intellectual curiosity, lifelong learning habits, and the ability to communicate enthusiasm about an area of interest. Similarly, asking, “What is a strongly held opinion you’ve changed your mind about?” can illuminate self-awareness, adaptability, and openness to evidence. These are qualities that many institutions claim to value but rarely assess directly.

**Learning who the person is**

Faculty hiring often focuses heavily on credentials. Candidates discuss degrees earned, institutions attended, publications produced, grants awarded, and courses taught. These accomplishments are important, but they tell only part of the story. The most successful faculty members bring more than expertise to their roles. They bring curiosity, empathy, resilience, creativity, and judgment. Unexpected questions can help uncover these dimensions.

Consider questions such as:

*   What was the last book you read and why did you choose it?
*   If you could have dinner with any historical figure, who would it be?
*   What accomplishment are you most proud of that isn’t listed on your CV?
*   What is something you’ve become better at in the last year?
*   What question were you hoping we would ask today that we have not?

None of these questions directly address teaching or research. Yet each can reveal how candidates see themselves, how they learn, and what they value. The answers often generate richer conversations than another iteration of “Tell us about your teaching philosophy.”

**Assessing critical thinking, not memorization**

Some unexpected questions are useful because they require candidates to construct an argument rather than retrieve a prepared response.

For example:

*   Who was the most effective U.S. president and why?
*   What innovation has had the greatest impact on higher education?
*   What leadership principle do you disagree with?

The goal is not to evaluate whether the committee agrees with the answer. Rather, it is to observe the candidate’s reasoning process. Can they support a position with evidence? Can they discuss potentially controversial topics respectfully? Can they acknowledge complexity and uncertainty? Higher education increasingly emphasizes critical thinking as a student learning outcome. It seems reasonable that institutions should also assess critical thinking in faculty candidates.

**Creating authentic moments**

One of the greatest benefits of unexpected questions is that they often create authentic interactions. Most faculty candidates have practiced answers to standard interview questions. Few have rehearsed a response to, “Teach us something in two minutes that has nothing to do with your discipline.” The responses can be remarkably revealing. Some candidates explain a hobby. Others describe a historical event, demonstrate a practical skill, or share an unusual personal interest. In just a few minutes, committees often learn more about communication style, enthusiasm, and personality than they did during an hour of structured questioning. These moments frequently become memorable because they allow candidates to relax and engage as human beings rather than applicants.

**Balancing fairness and discovery**

Of course, unexpected questions should not replace structured interviews. Search committees have legitimate obligations to ensure fairness, consistency, and compliance with institutional hiring practices. Core questions related to teaching, scholarship, service, and institutional fit should remain central components of every faculty interview.

Unexpected questions work best as a supplement. A committee might ask eight to ten standard questions aligned with the position description and then include two or three questions designed to reveal additional dimensions of the candidate. This approach preserves consistency while creating opportunities for genuine discovery. Importantly, the questions should remain professional, relevant, and appropriate. The goal is not to surprise candidates with trick questions or create unnecessary stress. Rather, it is to invite thoughtful reflection and authentic conversation.

**Hiring for the whole faculty member**

Faculty careers span decades. Institutions are not simply hiring someone to teach a class next semester or produce a publication next year. They are selecting colleagues who will contribute to a community of learning, innovation, and scholarship over the long term. The most important qualities of successful faculty members often extend beyond what can be captured on a curriculum vitae or assessed through standard interview questions. Curiosity matters. Adaptability matters. Humility matters. The capacity for reflection matters.

A few carefully chosen unexpected questions can help reveal these qualities. They remind us that interviews should be opportunities to learn about the individual behind the prepared statements and polished presentations. In an era when both candidates and committees increasingly rely on scripts, perhaps the most valuable interview question is the one nobody saw coming.
