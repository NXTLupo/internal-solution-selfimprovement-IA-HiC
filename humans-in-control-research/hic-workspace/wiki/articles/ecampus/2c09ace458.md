# Expect the unexpected: Lessons learned from conducting online survey research

Source: [ecampus](https://www.ecampusnews.com/campus-leadership/2026/10/21/expect-the-unexpected-lessons-learned-from-conducting-online-survey-research/)

Title: Expect the unexpected: Lessons learned from conducting online survey research

URL Source: https://www.ecampusnews.com/campus-leadership/2026/10/21/expect-the-unexpected-lessons-learned-from-conducting-online-survey-research/

Published Time: 2026-10-21T09:00:00+00:00

Markdown Content:
Online surveys have become one of the most widely used tools in educational and social science research because they provide access to geographically dispersed populations, reduce data collection costs, and allow researchers to gather substantial amounts of information quickly. However, a well-designed instrument alone does not ensure a successful study. Researchers must give equal attention to recruiting participants, establishing credibility, protecting privacy, minimizing participant burden, and adapting when data collection does not proceed as planned.

These lessons emerged during a recent survey examining higher education faculty perceptions of whether and how students should disclose their use of generative AI. The experience demonstrated that successful survey research involves much more than creating a valid instrument: it requires deliberate communication, visible markers of credibility, careful management of participant expectations, and a willingness to respond to the unpredictable realities of research in a digital environment.

**Trust is now a research variable**

One of the most significant changes in survey research during the past decade has been the growing skepticism of online communications. Faculty, administrators, and professionals receive constant reminders about phishing attacks, fraudulent links, and identity theft. As a result, researchers can no longer assume that a professionally written survey invitation will automatically be viewed as legitimate.

During a recent recruitment effort, multiple recipients responded not with questions about the survey content but with questions about whether the survey itself was authentic. One faculty member specifically asked whether the invitation had actually been sent by the researcher before deciding whether to participate. Another initially misinterpreted a forwarded survey invitation and only later clarified that they intended to complete the survey. These exchanges illustrate an important reality: participants increasingly evaluate the credibility of a survey invitation before they evaluate the value of the research itself.

Researchers must therefore treat trust-building as an integral component of the research design. Participants need clear signals that a study is legitimate, ethical, and professionally conducted. This includes clearly providing institutional affiliations, complete contact information, a clear description of the study’s purpose, and appropriate ethics or Institutional Review Board (IRB) contact information. Survey invitations that answer potential questions before they are asked are more likely to gain participant confidence and generate responses.

**Recruitment is often harder than instrument design**

Researchers frequently spend months refining survey questions while devoting relatively little attention to recruitment strategy. Yet recruitment is often the determining factor in whether a project succeeds. Many researchers can relive nightmares of trying to make sure they reach their desired sample size.

A perfectly designed survey cannot generate useful data if it fails to reach participants. In practice, many recruitment challenges have little to do with the survey itself. Institutional spam filters may block messages. Recipients may be overwhelmed by competing survey requests. Some may ignore messages from unfamiliar institutions, while others may postpone responding until they forget altogether.

These realities highlight the importance of developing a recruitment plan that extends beyond a single email invitation. Reminder messages, professional networks, institutional partnerships, and disciplinary contacts often play a greater role in response rates than minor changes to survey wording. Researchers should recognize that recruitment is not a one-time activity. It is an ongoing process that requires monitoring and adjustment throughout the data collection period.

**When social media makes sense**

As response rates from traditional email recruitment have become less predictable, many researchers have turned to social media platforms and professional online communities. Social media can be an effective recruitment tool, particularly when studying populations that are geographically dispersed or difficult to identify through institutional lists. Professional communities on LinkedIn, discipline-specific Facebook groups, professional associations, online forums, and academic networks can substantially expand a study’s reach. However, as many researchers reach out via social media, a survey can be lost in the forest of information that confronts users every day.

However, social media recruitment comes with important limitations. Participants who choose to respond through social media are self-selected. Individuals with strong opinions or interests related to the survey topic are often overrepresented. Consequently, samples recruited primarily through social media should generally be viewed as convenience samples and not truly representative populations.

This limitation does not invalidate the findings. It simply requires transparency. Researchers should carefully describe how participants were recruited and acknowledge any resulting limitations on generalizability.

**Participant burden matters**

Another common challenge in online survey research is respondent fatigue. Many professionals receive multiple survey invitations every week. Faculty members, in particular, are frequently asked to participate in institutional assessments, accreditation activities, program reviews, faculty governance initiatives, and external research projects.

Researchers sometimes attempt to maximize data collection by including every potentially useful question. Unfortunately, longer surveys often produce the opposite effect. Completion rates decline, participant engagement decreases, and open-ended responses become shorter and less thoughtful.

A useful rule of thumb is that every survey question should directly support a research objective. If a question does not clearly contribute to answering a research question, it probably does not belong in the instrument. Shorter surveys typically generate better participation rates and higher-quality responses. Respecting participants’ time demonstrates respect for participants themselves.

**Privacy concerns continue to grow**

Concerns about privacy and confidentiality are increasingly influencing participation decisions. Respondents want to know what data are being collected, who will have access to the information, how long data will be retained, and whether responses can be connected to individual identities. These concerns become even more important when surveys address professional practices, workplace issues, or potentially controversial topics.

Researchers should clearly explain their data protection procedures and avoid overstating anonymity. In many cases, survey responses may be functionally anonymous, but open-ended answers can sometimes inadvertently contain identifying information. Acknowledging these realities and explaining how such information will be handled demonstrates transparency and strengthens participant trust. The AI disclosure study, for example, included procedures for reviewing and sanitizing open-ended responses to remove accidentally submitted identifying information before analysis. A reminder to avoid sharing any potentially identifiable data prior to open ended questions is a good idea. Clear communication about confidentiality protections is not merely an ethical requirement. It is a practical strategy for encouraging participation.

**Expect the unexpected**

Perhaps the most valuable lesson from online survey research is that unexpected developments are inevitable.

Participants ask unanticipated questions. Recruitment channels produce uneven results. Some respondents provide rich and insightful feedback, while others misinterpret questions despite extensive piloting. External events can suddenly change participant attitudes and influence response patterns. Rather than viewing these developments as problems, researchers should view them as inherent features of the research process. Flexibility and responsiveness are often more important than perfect planning.

The most effective researchers recognize that data collection is not simply a technical exercise. It is a human process involving communication, trust, relationships, and adaptation.

**Final thoughts**

Online surveys remain one of the most powerful tools available to contemporary researchers. When thoughtfully designed and carefully administered, they can provide valuable insights across disciplines, institutions, and professional communities.

However, successful online survey research requires more than a reliable platform and a well-constructed questionnaire. Researchers must establish credibility, communicate transparently, protect participant privacy, select recruitment channels strategically, and remain prepared to adapt when circumstances change.

Perhaps the most important lesson is simple: participants are not merely sources of data but partners in the research process. Researchers strengthen that partnership by anticipating participants’ concerns, respecting their time, explaining how their information will be protected, and responding promptly when questions or misunderstandings arise. These practices are not peripheral courtesies; they are essential to ethical research and to the collection of meaningful, trustworthy data.
