### Key takeaways

AI is the first general-purpose technology introduced to CEOs, tenured professors, and college freshmen at the same time. Now seniors, the Class of 2026 is the first wave of talent to have lived through this shift across their entire college career — from ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 to their first post-graduation job search today.

Drawing on data from Handshake, this report documents how the Class of 2026 is adapting to a changing early-career market, developing skills employers increasingly demand outside the classroom, and navigating a transition the institutions around them are still figuring out.

###### Takeaway 01

AI adoption has gone from split to near-universal in two years

Close to half of seniors in the class of 2024 reported never using AI. By the class of 2026, 85% use it — and more than a third use it daily.

###### Takeaway 02

The job market for early talent remains tight — and seniors feel it

Job postings are 2% down from last year and 12% below pre-pandemic levels. Senior pessimism has climbed from 46% to 62% from two years ago — a 16-point rise.

###### Takeaway 03

Employer demand for AI talent took off in 2025

While students rapidly embraced AI since its launch, employer demand for AI skills was essentially flat for years — inflecting sharply in the past year. As of March 2026, more than 10% of active internships now mention AI keywords while the share of full-time postings mentioning AI has nearly doubled year-over-year to 4.2%.

###### Takeaway 04

Seniors feel caught between classrooms that discourage AI and employers that expect it

58% say they'll need stronger AI skills to succeed at work, but only 28% say their program has meaningfully integrated AI.

As one graduate put it, "AI appears to be a skill you teach yourself at your own risk. When I was in school, my professors were strongly against it, but now employers expect you to have already mastered it."
