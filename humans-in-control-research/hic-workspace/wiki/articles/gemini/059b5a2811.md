July 22, 2026

* * *

*   Gen Z uses AI at higher rates than older Americans yet remains sharply pessimistic about its effects on the labor, creativity, and personal relationships.
*   The backlash continues a decade of declining youth trust in technology and social media firms in general.
*   Regaining Gen Z’s trust will require guardrails and public interest protections historically deployed with transformative technologies, including labor, intellectual property, and environmental protections.

*   11 min read

This spring, college graduates [routinely booed](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/17/if-youre-giving-a-commencement-speech-in-2026-maybe-dont-mention-ai/) commencement speakers who praised artificial intelligence (AI). A real estate executive drew [jeers](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/14/style/ucf-commencement-ai-booed-gloria-caulfield.html) at the University of Central Florida for calling the rise of AI the “next industrial revolution”; a record-label CEO [told](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/05/ai-graduation-speeches-booing/687266/) booing Middle Tennessee State University graduates to “deal with it”; and former Google CEO Eric Schmidt was [heckled](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/former-google-ceo-booed-graduation-speech-ai-rcna345585) at the University of Arizona for praising a world dominated by AI.

These reactions reflect a growing body of survey evidence that Gen Zers—those born between 1997 and 2012—are increasingly skeptical of AI even as their own use of the technology has risen. About half of Gen Z respondents [report](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/09/style/gen-z-ai-gallup-study.html) in [surveys](https://hbr.org/2026/01/how-gen-z-uses-gen-ai-and-why-it-worries-them) that they use generative AI at least weekly, even as they express concerns over AI’s impact on human connection, creativity, the environment, and more. In fact, a [vocal subset](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/920401/gen-z-ai) is turning away from the technology altogether, calling use of AI embarrassing or a “red flag.” Tech leaders appear to have sensed this reaction and softened their pitch, [walking back](https://time.com/article/2026/05/26/sam-altman-ai-job-losses-openAI-/) predictions of mass layoffs in favor of a story about AI augmenting work rather than replacing it. This public relations shift on their part misdiagnoses the root cause of these frustrations and does not address the more fundamental problems swaths of Gen Z have with AI.

As Brookings scholar Darrell West [has](https://www.brookings.edu/books/the-automated-society/)[argued](https://www.brookings.edu/books/turning-point/), every transformative technology produces winners and losers—and it is near-term policy choices, not public communications, that determine which way the balance tips. At present, survey after survey suggests much of Gen Z has concluded it is on the losing side.

Younger adults are the most exposed to AI and, increasingly, the most wary of it. In a September 2025 Pew Research [survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/how-americans-view-ai-and-its-impact-on-people-and-society/), 62% of adults under 30 said they had heard a lot about AI—the highest of any age group, yet this same group was also the most pessimistic about its effects: 61% [said](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2025/09/17/ai-in-americans-lives-awareness-experiences-and-attitudes) it would worsen people’s ability to think creatively, and 58% said it would erode their capacity to form meaningful relationships, both roughly 20 points higher than respondents 65 and older. A [February 2026 Pew survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/) found just 14% of adults under 30 expect AI’s effect on society to be positive—two points below the average across age groups.

These concerns are elevated in four areas where the public has historically expected protection.

### Cognitive offloading

A large majority of Gen Zers believe AI will make [future learning harder](https://www.gallup.com/analytics/651674/gen-z-research.aspx) and people [lazier](https://hbr.org/2026/01/how-gen-z-uses-gen-ai-and-why-it-worries-them); in one workplace survey, [46% of Gen Z workers](https://www.govtech.com/education/higher-ed/graduating-students-boo-ai-at-commencement-ceremonies) said AI is making them “dumber,” against 39% of workers overall.

The supporting evidence is early: A June 2025 not-yet-peer-reviewed pre-print from MIT Media Lab [found](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/) weaker neural connectivity and poorer recall among participants who wrote an essay with a large language model (LLM). Those assigned an LLM partner struggled to accurately quote their own work and exhibited under-engagement, [suggesting](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/) that use of AI may pose some cognitive costs to neural connectivity.

Researchers at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University [surveyed](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf?msockid=3946951d52b16d430ba8839653906c8b) 319 knowledge workers across 936 real-world AI-assisted tasks and found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less critical thinking and that the technology shifted cognitive effort away from analysis and toward information verification and task stewardship. A separate [2025 study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387701784_AI_Tools_in_Society_Impacts_on_Cognitive_Offloading_and_the_Future_of_Critical_Thinking) by Michael Gerlich of SBS Swiss Business School found that higher AI use is correlated with greater cognitive offloading and lower critical thinking scores, especially among younger participants.

### Job displacement

One of AI’s biggest uncertainties is the potential for [mass disruption](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/ai-growth-acceleration-versus-distributional-fairness/) in the labor market. Research shows that, at least at present, mass labor disruptions are [not happening](https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/04/use-of-gen-ai-in-the-workplace-and-the-value-of-access-to-training/). Instead, companies using AI as an excuse for layoffs driven by other economic motivators (or “[AI-washing](https://fortune.com/2026/05/11/ai-automation-layoffs-gartner-study-roi/)”) may be more common. Nonetheless, many are convinced that the technology will lead to [massive changes](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/the-ai-labor-debate-three-views-on-the-future-of-work), if not, a complete upheaval of the job market. With very early labor market data, and most experts mixed or uncertain, it is difficult to affirm or negate these fears.

These concerns are common among Gen Z, many of whom are recent graduates inheriting a less-than-ideal job market. This is reflected in several surveys: 48% of employed [Gen Z workers](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708224/gen-adoption-steady-skepticism-climbs.aspx) say AI risks outweigh benefits in the workforce, up from 37% last year. Similarly, 69% say [they trust](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708224/gen-adoption-steady-skepticism-climbs.aspx) work completed by only humans more than that assisted by AI. And just 3% have the most trust in fully AI-generated work.

### Human relationships

Gen Z is also worried that use of generative AI could deteriorate or replace existing human social relationships. A July 2025 Common Sense Media [report](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions) found that 72% of U.S. teens have tried AI companions and 52% use them regularly; one-third reported telling a companion important or serious matters instead of another person. There is a significant [amount](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Generations/Jean-M-Twenge/9781797150949) of [research](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2025/04-05/teen-social-emotional-support) demonstrating that teens today report having fewer friends than in previous decades and that a “loneliness epidemic” is largely due to social media. In a New York Times interview, Abigail Hackett, 27, [said](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/04/09/style/gen-z-ai-gallup-study.html) that she does not use AI in her personal life because “she does not want her social muscles to atrophy.”
