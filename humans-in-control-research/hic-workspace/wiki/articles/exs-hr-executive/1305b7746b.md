# Fortitude Re’s AI hiring playbook: Faster hires, fewer exits

Source: [exs_hr_executive](https://hrexecutive.com/fortitude-res-ai-hiring-playbook-faster-hires-fewer-exits/)

As chief people officer at Fortitude Re, Denise Nichols led an effort to redesign the reinsurer’s talent acquisition function with an AI-first approach. Nichols is scheduled to join SVP of Talent Acquisition Claire Conneely to detail how they did it at HR Tech 2026. They will present their case study, which delivered a 50% reduction in time-to-fill, a 43% decline in new-hire attrition and $2 million in agency savings.
Nichols spoke with HR Executive about the ways AI has changed her team’s hiring process and how to avoid risks that surface when algorithms are part of both the candidate and employer communications.
AI is changing roles
Nichols pushes back on the idea that artificial intelligence is simply letting companies run leaner. “AI has increased demand for new roles and changed the capability profile of existing ones,” Nichols says. “Increasingly, people need to understand where AI can help, how to use it effectively and responsibly, and when human judgment matters most.”
She breaks jobs down into their component tasks, rather than treating them as single units. Nichols says AI tends to take on the more foundational or repeatable pieces, which frees capacity for judgment, problem-solving and collaboration without eliminating the role itself. At the same time, she finds AI is generating jobs that didn’t exist before, which makes the overall effect a rebalancing one.
Early-career workers aren’t left out of her calculation. “AI should not eliminate the need for entry-level development,” Nichols says. “It should help people progress through foundational work faster.” She also expects companies to hire more and more for adaptability and judgment rather than for a longer list of technical capabilities.

Built AI-first
When Fortitude Re rebuilt its talent acquisition function, Nichols says the team designed it around AI from the start. The company uses tools including SeekOut, LinkedIn Recruiter, Puck, Greenhouse and BrightHire to identify and engage candidates, match talent to roles, support interview preparation and scheduling, capture notes and structure interviewer assessments, according to the case study.
The efficiency gains are impressive. A role that once took more than 100 days to fill now takes as few as 20, Nichols says. Search fees dropped from more than $3 million to under $100,000. Voluntary turnover fell too, which Nichols reads as a sign the company is making stronger matches, in addition to moving faster.
When AI screens AI
A speedy process isn’t automatically a simpler one, according to Nichols. Candidates now use AI to tailor resumes and prepare for interviews, while employers use AI to screen and match candidates for open roles. She points out that in this situation, AI can end up assessing AI. Layered on top are new societal risks including fraudulent candidates, impersonated interviewers and fake job offers.
That combination raises the stakes for a skilled real-life recruiter. “A strong process now pairs automation with deliberate human assessment,” Nichols says. AI handles sourcing, matching, scheduling, documentation and pattern recognition. Recruiters and hiring leaders still have to judge whether a candidate can collaborate, exercise sound judgment, lead people the way the organization expects and fit its culture. She says deciding on those factors isn’t something that should be delegated to an algorithm.
Nichols doesn’t expect a return to manual, search-heavy hiring cycles, fragmented interview notes or scheduling handled entirely by administrative staff. She’s just as wary of the opposite extreme, where the process becomes computer-to-computer with no human check. She views AI as helpful for speed, consistency and better information, and humans key for discernment, trust and the final decision.
