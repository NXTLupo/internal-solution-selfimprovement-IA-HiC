# 3 in 4 employees have skipped work over safety concerns

Source: [exs_hr_executive](https://hrexecutive.com/3-in-4-employees-have-skipped-work-over-safety-concerns/)

Three-quarters of U.S. employees have skipped going to work because of hazardous conditions, such as weather, civil unrest, security concerns or infrastructure failures, according to a recent survey by workplace safety/security firm Envoy.
“That number alone should shift how organizations think about workplace safety,” said Shannon Sweetser, content and social manager at Envoy. “But the research that produced it reveals something more troubling—the gap between the threats workers face and the support they receive.”

Fifty-six percent of employees have felt unsafe at work; 53% have felt unsafe while commuting; and three-quarters said disruptive events have affected their ability to do their job, according to a recent survey by the workplace management platform. Severe weather is the leading concern, with more than half of workers having stayed home because of blizzards, heavy rain or extreme heat or cold. Other top safety concerns include:
- Travel risk or safety advisory, 20%
- Transit or transportation disruptions, 19%
- Environmental hazards, 17%
- Infrastructure failure, 16%
- Local events or public gatherings, 15%
- Crime or violence near workplace, 14%
- Civil unrest or protest activity, 9%
Gen Z is significantly more likely to avoid work and report feeling unsafe. Eight in 10 Gen Z employees have avoided work because of hazardous conditions, nearly 70% have felt unsafe at work and two-thirds have left work early for any reason.
Envoy recommends four steps that employers can take to mitigate concerns:
Centralize threat visibility. Organizations should have a single, authoritative view of threats that affect their people and places. This should include external signals (weather, civil unrest, crime, infrastructure and travel advisories) and internal signals (access control events, occupancy data and employee reports).
Make communication proactive, not reactive. Organizations aren’t communicating with sufficient speed or authority. Waiting for employees to bring concerns to them is too late.
Connect presence data to incident response. A notification sent to 500 people on an organization chart might reach 200 who actually are in the building. Adding another 40 visitors who checked in that day changes the accountability picture entirely.
Build an audit trail from first alert to full accountability. When regulators ask, “What did you do, and when did you do it?”, organizations need a time-stamped record that provides answers. This audit trail should be automatic, not archaeological.
“The workplace safety landscape has shifted,” Sweetser said. “It’s no longer acceptable to have fragmented tools, slow communication or incomplete occupancy data when decisions that affect people’s safety are on the line. Workers are telling organizations what they need—visibility, decisiveness and proof that their safety matters—not just for executives but for everyone.”
