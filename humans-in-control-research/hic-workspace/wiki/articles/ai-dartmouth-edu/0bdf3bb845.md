We report findings from the first wave of a survey of undergraduates including 
separate samples of Dartmouth students, graduating high school seniors, and U.S. 
college students more broadly .  Students report extensive use of AI  both for tasks 
which are likely harmful to learning and tasks which are likely helpful to learning.  Students report much higher peer misuse of AI relative to reports of own misuse. 
Ninety -two percent of students agree or strongly agree that use of AI is harmful to 
writing.  U se of graded out -of-class assignments has become increasingly unfair 
due to cheating with AI.   But students see the value of using AI to answer 
conceptual questions in real time, create practice quizzes and study guides and to 
enable meaningful and efficient progress on research projects.   Eighty -four 
percent of Dartmouth respondents  report that AI is changing the nature of jobs in 
their desired (future) field as opposed to eliminating jobs .   A I ’s  perceived value 
and harms vary widely by academic discipline suggesting  that use, rules , and 
approaches must also vary greatly based on the discipline  and context .  Students 
are largely self -taught in AI but have strong demand for incorporating AI 
effectively in the curriculum.

Artificial intelligence is among the most disruptive technologies of the last two centuries.  
Annual AI investment in the U.S. in 2026 will exceed $800 billion and totals more than  $1 
trillion globally  (Goldman 2026) .  Whether measured as a fraction of GDP or in absolute terms, 
the AI buildout is  among the largest ( possibly the  largest ) capital investment programs  in history.  
AI already touches most  human endeavor s and occupation s in the United States.  
Higher education is one of the heavily impacted areas; our survey data show that most college 
students use AI tools for a wide range of tasks, including explaining difficult concepts, 
summarizing readings, solving math problems, generating study guides and many other uses.   At 
the same time, there are significant concerns around AI’s impact on student learning ( Strömberg, 
Lei, and Wu 2026), reading skills, and brain activity.   
Furthermore , there are myriad questions about whether AI might eliminate college entry- level 
jobs altogether ( Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen 2026) or whether new college graduates will 
lack the skills needed to be productive and employable in a new AI powered world.  ( Green 
2024; World Economic Forum 2025).   
This paper sheds some light on a range of these questions using the first wave of a new panel survey which includes  Dartmouth undergraduates, incoming students before they arrive, and a 
representative sample of U.S. college students.  The survey addresses  areas such as AI use and 
misuse by the respondent and by peers, whether assessment practices need to change, and where 
AI is helping and harming learning.  We ask whether students are partnering with AI to produce better work and whether  they feel more confident in the final product .  We also cover labor 
market hopes and concerns .   We ask  whether students feel prepared to use AI effectively in this 
new world.  We also cover what their college or university is doing well with regard to AI 
guidelines and teaching and what could be done better.  
Students use AI extensively for dozens of different tasks , some of which are likely to be very 
helpful to learning , such as generating quizzes and study guides, and others which are almost 
surely harmful to learning , such a s copying and pasting answers from AI while doing little or no 
reflecting on the answer.  Students report that , relative to their own AI use, their peers are much 
more likely to use AI in  learning harmful ways .  Ninety two  percent of Dartmouth students agree 
or strongly agree that use of AI harms writing skills.  A majority also see graded take home assessments as being increasingly unfair because of the potential for AI cheating , and many 
students feel the pressure to use AI on graded take home work to keep up.

[Page 3]
Students offer  numerous examples of courses successfully integrating AI into the teaching and 
learning process.  However, students still feel largely self -taught in their AI skills and would like 
more such teaching in their college  courses AND in college sponsored workshops.  While some 
students worry that AI will eliminate jobs in their field, many more students (8 4 percent) see AI 
as simply changing the nature of jobs rather than eliminating jobs.  Dartmouth is currently 
surveying employers that frequently hire our students to see if the views from employers match those of the students and to explore whether new hires are arriving with the AI skills they need to 
be successful.  
Many of our findings are consistent with recent national and international surveys showing AI 
use has become routine in undergraduate studies. Gallup and Lumina Foundation surveys report 
weekly AI use of 57% in U.S. undergraduates . The HEPI survey of UK undergrads reports AI 
usage to assist  with assessed work of 88%. Across these studies explaining difficult materials is 
commonly cited as reason behind usage. Our findings distinguish between beneficial and potentially harmful usage of these technologies and measure students ’ perception of both self -use 
versus  peer use ; gaps in peer  versus self-usage  are striking .  
 
II. Data  
 
The first wave of the survey was launched in June of 2026 and continued through September of 
2026.  So far there have been three separate groups of students surveyed: Dartmouth 
undergraduates, incoming Dartmouth first years (pre -arrival), and a representative sample of U.S. 
college students via College Pulse.  Each of the groups received a similar,  though not identical 
survey;  we tailored the language to match the students ’ most recent institution (Dartmouth, high 
school, another college).  The survey used for Dartmouth undergraduates is in Appendix B . 
Dartmouth s tudents were contacted via email and the enrolled students were offered a $20 gift 
card for completing the survey.  The surveys of Dartmouth students and incoming students were 
conducted via the Qualtrics platform.  The survey  of US college students was conducted by 
College Pulse  on their platform using their panel of students  at four -year colleges and 
universities . 
Table 1 shows the means for demographic or background variables for all three samples.  There 
are 1,201 Dartmouth undergraduates and these are roughly evenly split between the graduating classes of 2026 -2029.  Additionally there are 332 incoming students who are in the graduating 
class of 2030.  The College Pulse (U.S. college students) sample has 1 ,003 students.  
The Dartmouth sample is 21 percent Pell eligible which matches the Dartmouth average across 
all students (i.e. the population).  The Dartmouth sample is skewed towards female students (56 
percent in sample versus 50 percent in the population) and international students  (26 percent 
versus 13 percent in the population) .  The Dartmouth sample and College Pulse sample have a

[Page 4]
similar distribution of majors with the Dartmouth sample having 9 percent of students in the 
humanities, 27 percent in the social sciences, 44 percent in the sciences and the remainder other 
(interdisciplinary) or undeclared.  Figure 1 presents a bar graph  with some of these descriptive 
variables shown for each of the three samples.  
Dartmouth is also in the field with two related surveys.  Dartmouth is interviewing employers to 
gather employers’ perspectives  on whether entry -level jobs for college students at their firm and 
in their industry are a) growing in number b) shrinking in number c) changing in responsibilities 
and skills needed because of the AI revolution.  
The employer s are offered a choice of an interview  with a human or  alternatively with a 
sophisticated AI voice interviewer  built by Expected Parrot , which is a spinoff from MIT faculty 
who study how AI can further social science data collection . 
 
III. Results  
We begin by exploring the tasks for which students use AI.  Table 2 details some of the uses of 
AI which are likely to be harmful to learning , and the survey questions intentionally made this 
explicit.  We ask respondents how they use AI and how their peers use it.  Only 17 percent of 
Dartmouth undergraduates report that they draft essays or papers using AI, but they say that 63 percent of their peers do so.  When it comes to more brazen uses such as copy -pasting text 
responses with little editing, only 5.4 percent of students admit to this but they believe  that 37.6 
percent of their peers do so.   Nine -and-one-half percent of students use AI to find problem set 
answers without taking the time to understand the answer but 41.6 percent report that their peers do this.  
In short there is a large gap between what students report they are doing and what they say their peers are doing.  There are , of course , several possible interpretations for this finding.  One is 
simply that cheating is rampant and students will not admit to it themselves but will volunteer 
that their peers are guilty. Undoubtedly , this is part of the story.  However , it could also be that 
students overestimate the degree to which their peers are cheating frequently on many assignments.  
Two facts suggest that  the desire to avoid self -incrimination is not the whole story.  First, the 
large gap between self -reports and peer reports does not change even when the survey is  
anonymized as in the College Pulse survey.  See Table 2 Panel B.  The large gaps on these self 
versus peer measures are the same in the College Pulse survey of U.S. college students.  Second,  
when we  conducted small focus groups with students and presented them with the results, 
students ’ own explanation of the results is a n even mix of not wanting to self incriminate plus an 
overestimation of the peer cheating rate .

[Page 5]
Meta- studies of underreporting of sensitive or illegal behavior like shoplifting, plagiarism and 
drug use suggest that subjects underreport these actions by about 40 percent for the most 
sensitive and most underreported actions  (Lensvelt -Mulders et al 2005) .  Scaling up the self 
reports in Table 2 by a factor of 2x still leaves a large gap between self -reported behavior and 
beliefs about peer behavior.  A separate issue is whether respondents are reporting the single 
worst observed behavior across many peers as opposed to the typical peer behavior.  We can’t 
rule this out,  but we would note that for other questions about peer versus self -behavior, the own 
versus peer rates match up quite nicely (see the Table 3 discussion next).  Future waves of the survey will ask additional questions to examine  both of these concerns more precisely.  
Table 3 presents the rates at which students and their peers use AI for tasks which are potentially very helpful to learning.  Fifty -three percent of  Dartmouth  students say that they use AI for 
checking grammar and 69 percent say their peers do this.  Two thirds of students say they use AI 
for explaining difficult concepts and that 72 percent of their peers do this.  Fifty seven percent of student s use AI to generate practice quizzes and study aids and they report that 66 percent of 
their peers do the same.  
Students and their peers are much less likely to use AI as a confidant or sounding board. 
Table 4 shows that there is broad agreement on the harms of AI to writing skills and to take home 
assessments.  Ninety -two percent of Dartmouth undergraduates agree or strongly agree that AI 
harms writing skills.  Eighty -four percent of U.S. college students agree or strongly agree with 
the same statement.  And both groups agree that take home quizzes, problem sets and essays are 
increasingly unfair because of AI use.  Consequently , about 40 percent of students in both groups 
feel that they have to cheat to keep up with their classmates.  
In Table 5, we explore whether the findings from Table 4 differ by the academic division 
(Humanities, Sciences, Social Sciences).  The short answer is not much.  The scientists are more worried about peers cheating on out of class assignments, but for both groups this i s a major 
concern with 69 to 74 percent agreeing or strongly agreeing with th is fear.   
Table 7 shows the domains where students do not trust AI.  About half of students would never 
trust AI with personal or social decisions including relationships.  Dartmouth undergraduates use AI for decision making in more domains than do U.S. college students in general.  For example , 
only 7 percent of Dartmouth students would never use AI for financial decisions versus 46 
percent for U.S. college students.  Five percent of Dartmouth students would not use AI for 
career and major choice decisions whereas for U.S. college students the n umber is 43 percent.  
Beliefs about AI’s impact on the labor market are shown in Table 8.  For both Dartmouth 
students and U.S. college students, about 56 percent believe that AI is reducing jobs in their field while 31-33 percent think AI is increasing jobs  in their field .  Importantly though, among 
Dartmouth students 84 percent agree or strongly agree with the statement that AI is changing the “nature of jobs in my field but not necessarily eliminating them.”  The second panel  of Table 8

[Page 6]
shows this number for the incoming students who just left high school and have not begun 
college.  Even more of these students believe that AI is changing but not eliminating jobs in their 
field.  A majority of students believe that having strong AI skills will be important for success in 
their career with that percentage at 74 for Dartmouth students and 55 for U.S. college students.  
A bit fewer than half of Dartmouth students feel competitive in the job market when it comes to 
AI skill s and a similar percentage are proactively learning AI skills to increase employability.  
Table 9 looks at these career questions by academic division of one ’s major (among the 
Dartmouth undergraduates).  Several of the differences between students in the human ities and 
the sciences and social sciences are noteworthy.  In particular , 56 percent of arts and humanities 
students believe that having strong AI skills will be important for success in their  career  but this 
number is 78 (79) percent for the sciences (social sciences).  When it comes to proactively 
learning new AI skills to increase employability, the figure is 28 percent for the humanities and 46 (47) percent for the sciences (social sciences).  
Table 10 asks whether this is a gap in access to AI tools by family income.  We show this for 
Dartmouth undergraduates and cut the data by Pell versus non -Pell eligible students.  Twenty 
percent of Pell -eligible students have paid for  premium AI tools versus 30 percent for the non -
Pell students.  However , a similar percentage of students in both groups use AI for routine 
academic tasks.  Dartmouth provides free access to top tier AI tools.  
We now turn to permitted uses of AI in high school using the data for the incoming Dartmouth 
class.  These numbers are tabulated from a free form response question which is why the sample 
sizes are smaller than the 300+ students in that sample.  The main conclusi on from Table 11 is 
that the high schools for the incoming students appear to greatly limit permitted use of AI.   Only 
13 percent of students report that they were permitted to use AI to find sources.  Such regulation 
could be counterproductive if AI is in fact very good at identifying sources and scholarly articles for students to read and to broaden the materials they are using in an essay.  Similarly , it is rare 
for the high schools to permit use of AI to summarize readings  (at 6 percent).  In college courses, 
AI can be (though is not always) helpful to learning if students are both reading the assigned 
material but also looking at a summary before or after reading the assigned text.  Many 
Dartmouth courses intentionally assign such a larg e amount of reading that the expectation is that 
students are forced to make intelligent choices about how to allocate their time and which 
sources to read in full and at what speed.  
Table 1 2 examines student reports as to whether their college courses are providing meaningful 
guidance on how to use AI effectively and preparing students to use AI post -college.  About half 
of Dartmouth students report that faculty are providing meaningful guida nce on AI use while 38 
percent think Dartmouth is adequately preparing them for AI use in a future career.  The 
percentages for US college student s are higher with half saying that their college is adequately 
preparing them.  Related to this , 86 percent of Dartmouth students report being mostly self -
taught in AI skills.

Panel A shows that 22 percent of Dartmouth students report that guidance is inadequate or

IV. Concluding Remarks and Implications for Higher Education and the Liberal 
Arts   
 The survey data paint a picture in which student use of AI in higher education is rampant.  
Students agree that AI can be very detrimental to learning when misused  and when used as a 
substitute for student thinking.  However , there are clearly also very productive uses of AI , such 
as seeking explanations for difficult concepts, generating practice quizzes and study aids, and 
debugging code to learn where things went wrong.  Students report partnering with AI to 
accomplish more and to produce a better work product in which they feel more confident.    
 
There is a large gap between how much students self -report AI misuse and what they believe 
their peers are doing.  Some of this gap is likely that students will not self -incriminate  even on a 
zero stakes (and in the case of the U.S. college  student sample) anonymized survey.  However , 
discussions with students indicate that much of the gap may stem from peer misuse being  
overestimated .   Perhaps  reports are upward biased by a few egregious and memorable but not 
representative incidents.  Out -of-class assessments are increasingly problematic because the 
temptation to use AI to help with responses is too great.  
 
Students largely agree that AI skills will be important in their future jobs with Dartmouth 
students expressing this sentiment at a higher rate than U.S. college students.  The jobpocalypse 
has not arrived and is not necessarily coming at all.  Over 80 percent of respondents agree or 
strongly agree that AI is changing the nature of jobs but not eliminating them.  
 Students are largely self-taught  in AI tools currently.  They request more explicit AI skills 
training both integrated into courses and in standalone workshops.

[Page 8]
The rise of AI misuse need not portend the end of higher education and true learning and 
building of skills.  Many faculty at Dartmouth have already switched most assessments to 
proctored exams which are conducted on paper without the use of phones and lap tops.  Students 
frequently acknowledge their need to learn the material since they , not AI , will be taking the in -
class exams and quizzes.  Furthermore, the survey data show that AI is useful for removing some 
of the less intellectual work from student projects (e.g. cleaning and merging messy data or 
finding the exact code to draw a complicated overlay graph) .  This in turn  leav es much more 
time for conceptual thinking about the goal of the project and what additional analyses should be added and checked.  Because so many students use AI to re -explain difficult concepts, this means 
that students have a way to get office hours -type questions answered during times  when faculty 
are not easily available.  
 
A productive response to the rise of AI is to further double down on the use of class time and 
office hour s time for human -to-human interaction  on key course concepts .  Like many faculty, 
the authors  have banned the use of phones , laptops , and iPads during class.  This removes 
distractions (e.g. phone notifications) and causes students to discuss the assigned academic journal articles with me and their peers.  AI tools are then used to their full effect in the students ’ 
own research projects in which many more original and messy data sets can be integrated 
quickly using AI as a hardworking research assistant  which complements rather than substitutes 
for learning .   
 
Note that these adjustments are tailored  to our  subfield which is applied (empirical) social 
science.  One size does not fit all.  Humanities students rate AI as more destructive to learning and less applicable to their future jobs  than do other students .  Many of their faculty are also 
finding the same thing.  Clearly , colleges need to be flexible, thoughtful and intentional in 
proposing AI guidelines ; almost surely the particulars of rules and guidelines need to vary across 
disciplines and course types.    Future waves of the survey will allow us to capture how the picture is evolving across cohorts 
and even within  cohorts .  Dartmouth is currently surveying employers to capture the view from 
their angle.
