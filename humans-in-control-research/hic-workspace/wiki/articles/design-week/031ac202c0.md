# How Mazda resolutely retains sculpting craft as part of the design process

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/how-mazda-resolutely-retains-sculpting-craft-as-part-of-the-design-process/)

“Our target is to create a timeless form as much as possible,” Mazda’s global chief of design explains. Delivering his words with a quiet but fierce conviction, he notes that he is entirely unmoved by corporate pressure to rush production. “No matter if the company asks us to make it shorter [the delivery timeline]… I don’t care.” For Ikuo Maeda, great design means protecting the craft, which means protecting the hours required to create the designs by hand.
Maeda’s refusal to yield to standard corporate schedules is striking, but it reflects a historical pattern.
Mazda has long functioned as an automotive outlier. In the 1960s, the Japanese government wanted to consolidate the country’s automotive industry and would have forced Mazda to merge with a larger company unless it could prove it possessed unique, world-class technology. Mazda bet everything on the difficult to manufacture Wankel rotary engine. A gamble that succeeded, defining models from the Mazda Cosmo Sport through to the electrified MX-30. And so has their devotion to being different and refusing to be overly corporatised.
In an industry that is currently driven by the high margins of supersized SUVs, Mazda’s emotional core remains an unpretentious, lightweight sports car. As Europe’s head of design, the Belgian Jo Stenuit, puts it: “The MX-5 is the heart of Mazda.”
We were in Venice for the 4th edition of Homo Faber, the international contemporary craft biennial held in Venice. And one where Mazda has invested a lot of support, sponsoring a display room and workshop activities, allowing any attendee to learn either how to carve Hanko stamps or the Kintsugi craft of repairing pottery in a way that creates something more beautiful than the original.
Hanko relies on the concept of Ma – the Japanese aesthetic concept of subtraction. A key part of the design process is working out what can be taken away from a design as opposed to other car makers who have added faux vents, busy body panels and giant, glowing grilles with enthusiasm.
Mazda supports a team of both Western and Japanese artisans whose work is on display at the event. More on that in another article, where we report on their crafts and thoughts on how it interlinks with design.
Venice, a city empty of cars, provides the perfect backdrop to this thesis. It’s a city where the inability to jump in a car or even ride a bicycle, forces you to slow down. Perhaps this is why Venice is historically aligned with making. Craft demands time, patience, and skill.
Why hand-sculpted clay beats computer code
In this Chinese-led era of increasingly fast car design and development cycles, Mazda is one of the rare firms that eschews purely digital design methodologies. Their car designs start life as a sketch on paper or a tablet – something that remains standard across almost every car designer I’ve spoken to, but instead of going digital with CAD software followed by shortlisting towards virtual reality renderings, Mazda creates multiple small clay models. These are then foiled and illuminated carefully so that the interplay between the sculpture and light can be seen, reviewed, and refined.
“When the car moves or when you move around the car, the whole environment starts dancing on the surface and we say the surface or the car comes to life. It reflects the whole environment in a very natural way.”
As I considered what was being told to me, I couldn’t get a doubt out of my head – I know that many cars are designed strictly on computer, and that these processes have proven successful to others. I pressed on why they couldn’t start in CAD instead.
Maeda quickly pulled out his iPad and showed me two images of the current Mazda MX-5 and one of a digital-only concept replacement. He then scrolled down to show a computer analysis of the curves in both, and it was arresting how much subtlety this analysis revealed in the hand-sculpted car.
With each change of curve represented as a different colour, it looked kaleidoscopic. Unfortunately I can’t show you this image as these are closely guarded – suffice to say the difference was convincing and it revealed that the visual simplicity of Kodo-styled Mazdas is deceptively complex to create. Because the forms are harmonious and natural, they don’t jar our senses.
This sculpted design doesn’t just apply to the car exteriors – the interiors are subject to the same philosophy, keeping them flowing and organic in shape and bringing together textures and colours along flowing lines.
For most buyers, a car represents the single largest expenditure after a home; its appearance remains a direct reflection of personal taste. Stenuit connects this aesthetic responsibility directly to sustainability, arguing that beauty drives longevity.
Beauty as durability
To illustrate, Stenuit explained to me how he is a nightmare for tilers. When he was renovating his bathroom, some twenty years ago, he insisted the tiler plan his work carefully and make an absolute minimum of cuts. Although this was more work and more expensive, it resulted in a bathroom that still looks good and brings joy two decades on.
His point is pragmatic rather than romantic. Well executed objects are valued, these valued objects are then retained, and because they’re appreciated the product lifespans are extended. It’s a virtuous cycle, and one that is revealed by UK SORN (Statutory Off Road Notification) data. People save cars they feel are special, in the hope of a restoration.
“It’s really being able to, let’s say, demand from the company that, ‘Please give us time and you’ll get something beautiful that you can sell over a longer time.’… We’ve always said that designers need time. If you want something beautiful, you give us time.”
Like most senior car designers, Stenuit maintains a small collection of vehicles from old to new – a Datsun 240Z classic, a Mazda MX-5 in orange (but he’d actually prefer a red one now) and his daily company car which, many may be surprised to learn, is actually from a rival manufacturer – this is all part of learning what other firms are doing by living the experience of an owner. But less obvious is his collection of Dieter Rams products and of Olivetti typewriters.
These may clutter the house, and whilst he joked that he really had to sell some things, I got the sense that he was in no hurry to do so.
Crucially, this focus on craft is paired with mass-market pricing. Rather than reserving good finishes for ultra-low-volume luxury editions, Mazda targets mainstream affordability. The MX-5 remains an accessible entry-level sports car, while the CX-80 offers three-row proportions at a price point substantially below European competitors like the Volvo XC90.
“I don’t think craftsmanship has to be linked to luxury or one-offs.”, he said. “You know, you can also design products with the utmost quality through dedication, craftsmanship… we are not a premium brand, but we are perceived sometimes as such because we make forms that are very delicate and human, and look more expensive than they are because we took the time to develop the surfaces.”
Ikuo Maeda, meanwhile, is a man who spends his spare time at the racing circuit, enjoying the thrill of competition. This taste for dynamism is reflected in another integral design decision that engineering also has to acknowledge – weight matters for driving dynamics. The design teams work to keep the cars light, as well as aesthetically pleasing.
To the marketing team, slow design is probably the last thing they want to hear. There is always a temptation to run frequent and regular design tweaks according to trends, treating cars almost as fast fashion. Yet when you look at the current Mazda 3, released in 2019, or the Mazda MX-5, released in 2016, you see cars that still look fresh, modern and dynamic. Avoiding trends and going timeless could well be the smartest thing a relatively small car maker can do to protect margins and compete independently with the giants of the world.
Independently unique
In the end I was left with the impression of a car company which is resolutely anti-corporate – something that perhaps rescued them from conglomeration in the 1960s. This was an era when the Japanese government insisted that car firms without unique technology should merge together. My own suspicion is that this, instead, created an environment where car firms realised the importance of innovation if they were to stay independent – Mazda worked hard to create a rotary engine with durability, released in the Cosmo Coupe and it saved the company. Meanwhile, brands like Prince, Hino, and Cony disappeared.
That doesn’t mean Mazda aren’t working in co-operation with other car makers. The latest model they’ve released is the 6e – a large executive electric saloon built using Changan’s dedicated EV platform. This is a necessity for a small car manufacturer dealing with rapid changes in technology. You could, of course, sense a certain amount of discomfort around this need to compromise on the engineering in order to deliver a car the market wants and which they had to deliver more quickly than the firm was comfortable with. But the European and Chinese markets are electrifying at a ferocious pace, and that requires adaptation.
All designers have a constant life of negotiation between art and commerce. Realities drive compromise. Pressures today risk turning cars into short-lived digital appliances. Mazda is engaging in a stubborn refusal to abandon slow. It’s as if the entire C-suite took time to read the influential 2002 book, In Praise of Slow. It’s a pragmatic position, rather than one dictated; it appears to come from compromise.
Where does this lead for future cars? When I asked, Maeda wasn’t drawn but turned to Stenuit and joked, “Well, he wants me to retire… but-”
Stenuit then interrupted with, “Maeda-san’s in charge!”
