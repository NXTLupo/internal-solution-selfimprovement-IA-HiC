# How Oktra and Material Index are rethinking commercial fit-out waste

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/how-oktra-and-material-index-are-rethinking-commercial-fit-out-waste/)

The UK construction sector is responsible for over 60 per cent of all national waste and between 12% and 14% of global carbon emissions, yet a meagre 1% of building components are currently reused. In commercial workplace design, where standard corporate lease turnarounds regularly trigger wholesale interior strip-outs, the scale of unnecessary disposal is vast. An estimated £60bn of valuable building assets were scrapped across the UK and US last year alone.
To disrupt this systemic waste, workplace design and build firm Oktra has partnered with circular economy platform Material Index. Using AI-driven site audits, material passports, and a network of over 300 trade partners, the collaboration aims to keep high-value fit-out elements – ranging from acoustic ceiling tiles to task chairs – out of landfill skips and inside a closed-loop marketplace.
Covering six sites across 10,000 sq m of commercial floor space, the initiative has already identified over 3,000 tonnes of material for reuse, saving roughly 1,000 tonnes of embodied carbon. Jamie Firman, head of sustainability and environmental at Oktra, who brings over 15 years of construction sustainability experience, sat down with Design Week to discuss the day-to-day realities of circular sourcing, overcoming client scepticism, and why the future of workplace aesthetics relies on reclaiming the past.
Mapping the invisible inventory
At the heart of the partnership is a fundamental change in how strip-outs are planned. Instead of treating existing interiors as waste to be cleared post-lease, Material Index conducts a digital material reclamation audit before deconstruction begins.
“It’s really unintrusive in terms of the work—it’s just a person with an iPad taking photos of what’s on site, and then that lands on their marketplace,” explains Firman. “From that point, we instantly start brokering that material and finding suitors prior to us starting that strip-out, so we know what needs to be dismantled and collected.”
Crucially, the process bypasses storage entirely. Material Index routes reclaimed stock directly through specialist trade partners who clean, refurbish, and re-warrant the items.
“Those trade partners are responsible for refurbishing and rewarranting that material,” Firman notes. “The ultimate aim for us is to get to a point where we demonstrate that there is no difference in quality, in standard, or in the lifespan of these materials.”
Overcoming the ‘second-hand’ perception
For many corporate clients, the biggest barrier to circular specification remains psychological. Reclaimed materials have historically suffered from a reputation of being worn out, mismatched, or second-rate.
“There is a definite perception change that needs to happen,” argues Firman. “We’re working really hard to showcase that these materials aren’t second-hand or old materials that we’re putting into a new project.” In fact, choosing second-life stock often unlocks unexpected quality tiers: “It can even be the case that a client is able to get a higher quality second-life product than they would if they were to specify a new product on a lower-spec budget.”
This shifting approach also offers a richer storytelling framework for workplace branding.
“A well-designed office doesn’t need to mean brand new,” he maintains. “Reuse can become part of how a space’s identity and character are built, rather than a constraint. By extending the same thinking we already apply to workspaces to the materials themselves, we can give furniture, flooring and fittings a life beyond a single lease.”
The logistics of supply chains and legislation
Beyond carbon reduction, the commercial arguments for circular sourcing are rapidly compounding. In an era marked by global supply chain volatility and unpredictable lead times, sourcing locally reclaimed stock offers immediate practical relief.
“These typically long-lead items – lighting as well can be long-lead – are all available with trade partners, so you’re effectively cutting quite lengthy lead times to a fairly quick turnaround,” observes Firman. “With supply chains being more volatile and unpredictable as they are, it’s another real plus for going down a second-life route.”
Simultaneously, regulatory frameworks are tightening. With the government introducing mandatory digital waste tracking rules this October, businesses and contractors will be held legally accountable for material lineage and waste disposal. Coupled with regional mandates, such as London’s strict circular economy strategy requirements for major developments, the design community faces an immediate operational requirement to adapt.
“It also makes it a lot easier sell when you can make money from what would be waste,” Firman adds. “We are literally getting value from what would be thrown in a skip or what a client would have to pay to dispose of. That value goes back to the client, into the project, or into a social enterprise donation.”
The unwanted task chair
When asked about the operational challenges of managing oversupply, such as excess inventory of standard office desks, Firman dispels the myth entirely.
“I don’t think oversupply is an issue for us at the moment—Material Index is actively seeking more material,” he counters. “Seeing an abundance of choice on their marketplace is reassuring because for the projects we work on, we need large volumes. There is amazing pod furniture on there, and an abundance of really high quality task desking and chairs.”
The initiative is expected to be applied by Oktra across multiple enterprise projects.
Design’s not dead. But it’s under pressure.
