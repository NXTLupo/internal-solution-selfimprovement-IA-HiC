# The CBI’s latest AI report on how design businesses can bridge AI’s execution divide

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/the-cbis-latest-ai-report-on-how-design-businesses-can-bridge-ais-execution-divide/)

The Confederation of British Industry (CBI) and Oliver Wyman last week released their report on AI, titled The Adoption Decade, which makes one thing clear: the first wave of AI experimentation is now over. For independent design studios and in-house creative teams, success is no longer measured by how many generative tools you test, but by how effectively you succeed at integrating them into core operations.
Mind the execution gap
While 63% of the surveyed firms reported a move toward scaled or agentic AI in product design and R&D (see page 26), profitability gains haven’t been coming from surface-level software rollouts such as giving everyone access to ChatGPT or Gemini. The report highlights an “execution divide” – the widening gap between companies running isolated pilots and leaders embedding AI into end-to-end operating models.
For design teams, particularly within manufacturing, the real value now lies in connecting initial digital concept generation directly to the downstream procurement, assembly, and delivery workflows. This chimes with what we saw when surveying car manufacturers at a recent summit, where AI was helping in more than just the design software.
Augmentation over automation
Despite persistent anxiety around job replacement, the near-term economic gains stemmed from augmenting workers rather than headcount cuts (see page 16). AI speeds up initial concept exploration and rapid prototyping. We’ve seen this when talking to 3D modellers about how they create their concepts today – they never use it for final work, but instead of rough sketches they can now present far more complete ideas to stakeholders before the laborious and expensive modelling stage.
As software takes over routing technical execution, the non-automatable skills of taste, critical judgement, emotional intelligence and the ability to audit AI outputs become the defining differentiators of creative work.
Asset provenance and IP traps
Training or fine-tuning models on client assets or third-party work carries major legal risks. An ongoing government review in March 2026 stated that until further evidence is acquired, there will remain ambiguity until copyright law is reformed. Design firms, therefore, must proactively establish clear data licensing, metadata, and asset provenance protocols. Clients will increasingly demand proof that generated designs are legally sound and commercially protected before they can be signed off.
Protecting the junior talent pipeline
The automation of routine drafting, concept image generation, and basic layout tasks threatens to collapse the traditional studio structures from pyramids into diamonds, thus squeezing out entry-level positions (see page 74). If junior tasks disappear without clear alternative learning pathways, then the long-term pipeline of talent will break. We’re already seeing hints of this, with a recent survey suggesting a 50% decline in graduate job vacancies this year compared to last year. Design leaders must actively redesign entry-level roles around AI-assisted workflows, teaching junior staff to direct, evaluate and refine synthetic outputs from day one (see page 75).
Moving forward, winning design businesses aren’t just going to be those that create the best prompts – they will be the ones that treat AI as a structural operational shift whilst carefully protecting human taste, creativity and accountability.
In conclusion, for design businesses, we believe that these workflow changes will improve output, create opportunities for growth, and drive efficiency, meaning that for a given budget, there will be a greater output.
Although AI isn’t a disaster for employment, it’s right for young, up-and-coming designers to worry about where their opportunities will come from, and design leaders must consider this and factor the pipeline cost into future work.
Our fear is that this won’t happen; there’s always a temptation in competitive bids to deliver better value than your rivals. Ignoring the pipeline saves money, and it can feel like “somebody else’s problem.”
The consequences, however, may only be felt a decade or so down the line.
The full report can be downloaded for free from the CBI’s website.
