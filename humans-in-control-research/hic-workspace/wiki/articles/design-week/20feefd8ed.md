# No fonts on a dead planet: a website case study

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/no-fonts-on-a-dead-planet/)

In 2022, my team and I went to a trivia night, and one of the questions was: ‘Which has the bigger carbon footprint: sending 100 emails or driving 1 km in a car?’
We confidently said the car. We were wrong. This sent us down a deep rabbit hole. We discovered that if the internet were a country, it would be one of the world’s largest carbon emitters. But as designers, we do not really think about the environmental impact of our work.
So we looked at our own.
The results were alarming. According to the Website Carbon Calculator, our site produced more carbon than 76.5% of web pages tested. At around 10,000 monthly page views, that is around 145.6 kg of CO₂ a year.
Every time someone visits a website, data has to travel from a server through networks to their device, and all of that infrastructure requires energy. Most of the time, that energy comes from fossil fuels. The more data a page needs, the more energy is used to load it. A site’s digital footprint is not only where it is hosted, but also how much data it sends every time someone visits. Multiply that by thousands of visits and the small decisions we make as designers become a much bigger problem.
So here we were – a design studio with a reputation for building radical brands in environment and tech – while our own website was heavily consuming resources.
This is where I think the design industry has a problem. We have become very good at adding things. Another animation, another interaction, another font, another video, another slideshow. Another this, another that. Digital technology has made it almost frictionless, and somewhere along the way we started equating more with better.
We could not ask our clients to rethink their environmental impact without confronting our own. So we redesigned and rebuilt our site, treating energy and emissions as creative constraints instead of technical limitations.
The old MEK site had seven full-screen images on the homepage, video, and animations averaging around 8 MB per file. Images averaged around 700 KB.
So we questioned everything. Does this need to be here? Does it need to be this size? Does it need to move? What is it actually contributing? And, most importantly, does it even need to exist?
Some answers were easy. We replaced high-resolution JPGs with optimised, resized AVIF images. We removed almost all motion and video. We simplified the navigation and reduced the amount of content.
Other decisions were harder. Typography, for example. We switched from Dinamo’s Monument Grotesk to Arial, a system font that, unlike Monument Grotesk, does not need an extra font download, which in turn reduces data transfer and energy usage.
It was technically efficient. It was also Arial.
There is a limit to how much even designers can compromise. So we decided on Google Fonts, accepting a small energy increase for a typeface that better supported our brand and user experience.
That compromise matters. Low-carbon design is not about making the lowest possible number at any cost; it is about understanding the cost of our decisions and what is worth it.
The same thinking applied to colour. We moved from a white interface to dark mode, reducing energy consumption while maintaining accessibility. We were not just making a website ‘greener’ – we were asking whether each design decision earned the resources it consumed.
We launched the new site and ran it through the carbon calculator again. Emissions per page view fell from 1.21 g of CO₂ to 0.42 g – a 67% reduction. What I learned the most is not the 67%, but what the process revealed about design.
We talk a lot about designing for people, but rarely about designing within planetary limits. We obsess over pixels, performance, and conversion rates while treating carbon emissions as someone else’s problem. It’s not.
Every image we upload, every video we autoplay, and every interaction we add has a cost. The fact that people do not see it does not mean it is not there.
This is not an argument for boring websites. It is actually the opposite. Constraints can force better ideas. When you cannot rely on spectacle, you have to ask what actually communicates. When you cannot throw another 8 MB video at the problem, you have to make the design do more with less.
Designers have spent years becoming experts at making digital worlds more engaging, more immersive, and more visually impressive.
If we believe design can change behaviour, systems, and culture, that thinking has to extend to the systems we create ourselves.
We cannot design for a better world if we keep ignoring the cost of the digital one.
Mirella Arapian is the founder and executive creative director at MEK (mek.studio). Further study on reducing website carbon emissions can be found here.
Design’s not dead. But it’s under pressure.
