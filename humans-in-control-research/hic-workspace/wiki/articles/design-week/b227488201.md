# design/leader: René Morency, Product Director at Browser London

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/design-leader-rene-morency-product-director-at-browser-london/)

This week, we interview René Morency, founding partner and Product Director at Browser London. An award-winning digital leader whose studio recently shared the stage with Huge and AKQA at the Web Awards, René specialises in digital product strategy, long-term agency resilience, and sustainable circular design. He has applied nearly two decades of product leadership to direct digital builds for major institutions and global brands like Ford, British Airways, the FIFA World Cup, and the UK Government.
René talks to us about the importance of listening, the confidence to say ‘I don’t know’, and the mechanical wristwatch.
Design
What would your monograph be called?
‘Stop talking, start doing.’
Hesitation is the mind-killer of good ideas. Most ideas don’t fail because they’re wrong; they fail waiting for permission. I think that real creativity lives in that moment where you just start doing it without hesitation. It’s something I’ve learned over the years; practically, I’m far more ‘think fast, fail fast’ than I used to be, and far better for it I must add.
What recent design work made you a bit jealous?
SpaceX’s Starship.
I’m a designer, but an engineer at heart, so the engineering alone would be enough. When you look at Starship, it’s beautiful. Something that size, built to survive the violence of launch and re-entry, and yet you know that every minuscule piece of design has been considered. The bare stainless steel, the proportions, the absence of anything unnecessary. Seeing that level of perfection at that scale is insane. It’s less jealousy than awe at what those designers and engineers have pulled off.
What’s an unusual place you get inspiration from?
The deck of a racing yacht.
I’m typically one of a six-person crew, and for eight months of the year we race every week off the south coast. It can be exhilarating and terrifying in equal measure, especially in storm conditions. But the colour out there is unreal. Layers of blue, bright whites, and oranges as the sun and clouds break across the water. It’s mesmerising, and a constant reminder of how much beauty lies in the raw nature around us.
Name something that is brilliantly designed, but overlooked.
The mechanical wristwatch.
In a world of screens, there’s an age-old romance in wearing something mechanical on your wrist. The smaller the better, the older the better. Thanks to designers such as Gérald Genta, who shaped the iconic watches of the 60s and 70s, it’s like walking around with a piece of fine art, with only those who know appreciating it and striking up a conversation. It’s product design at its most honest, built to be repaired and passed on, not replaced.
What object in your studio best sums up your taste?
My original Vitsoe 606 Universal Shelving System, designed by Dieter Rams.
Originals from the 60s are hard to find, so when one came up at an auction house in Germany, I bid, won, and had it shipped over without really knowing what state it was in. It sums up my taste because it’s both practical and beautiful at once. The design hasn’t changed much; sixty years on, it still does exactly what it was designed to do. You’ve got to admire Dieter for that.
Leadership
What feedback felt brutal at the time, but turned out to be useful?
“Listening is a skill you’ve yet to learn.”
Whilst it felt like an insult at the time, it turned out to be one of the most useful things anyone said to me. I learned about the practice of ‘active listening’ a few months after, and have been practicing ever since. It’s pretty much the practice of giving someone your full attention and holding back your own judgment and assumptions while they speak.
It’s harder than it sounds and requires a huge amount of real practice. I’ve since written articles about my experiences of applying it, and now pretty much use it every day, in research sessions, in workshops, and in feedback.
What’s an underappreciated skill that design leaders need?
Being able to say “I don’t know yet” with confidence.
The emphasis here is on ‘yet’. People pay for certainty, so there’s huge pressure to walk into a kick-off with the answer. The skill is presenting uncertainty as a plan, e.g. here’s what we believe, here’s how we’ll find out, and here’s when we’ll know.
Executed well, it builds more trust than a polished concept has ever done, because that honesty and open-minded approach is where creativity can flourish. It takes nerve, especially in a pitch, but it’s the difference between designing for users and designing for the C-suite.
What keeps you up at night?
Finding the right words.
I know how a design concept makes me feel, and I know what I want to say about it, but expressing it in a presentation in a way that captures the imagination, from both a design and technology viewpoint, isn’t easy.
For me, the ideas arrive easily; the phrasing doesn’t. So I end up rewriting a talk in my head at 2:00 am, trying to make it sound less like a presentation and more like a conversation. The irony isn’t lost on me; it’s the same problem as designing a good interface. The hard part is making it feel effortless.
What trait is non-negotiable in new hires?
Initiative.
What you’ve done professionally matters, but I’m far more interested in what you do when nobody’s paying you. I want to know what drives someone and how they express it, whether that’s organising a local running club, designing a line of furniture, or making music and putting it out on SoundCloud.
Those projects say a lot about who you are, what you care about, and how focused and dedicated you can be. They’re proof you can take an idea from nothing to something without being asked. It comes back to my monograph – ‘Stop talking, start doing.’
Complete this sentence: “I wish more clients…”
…had read ‘The Art of Thinking Clearly’ by Rolf Dobelli.
A former colleague that I hugely admire recommended it years ago, and I keep going back to it. It’s a short, practical read of the cognitive biases we all fall for, from sunk costs to confirmation bias, and it makes you a noticeably better decision-maker.
Most of the difficult project conversations I’ve had weren’t really about design; they were about bias, such as falling for the first idea, trusting the loudest voice, or throwing good money at a bad decision. If clients and designers shared that vocabulary, we’d spend less time defending opinions and more time testing them.
