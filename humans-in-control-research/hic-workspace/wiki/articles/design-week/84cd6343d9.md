# Shirt storm: the beautiful game and its critical clients

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/shirt-storm-the-beautiful-game-and-its-critical-clients/)

Within hours (if not seconds) of Newcastle United unveiling their 2026/27 home shirt, social media had already reached its verdict. As a lifelong Newcastle fan, so had I.
Dubbed the “barcode shirt” by many supporters, the new strip sparked thousands of comments debating everything from the thickness of the black stripes to whether it had strayed too far from the club’s identity. One fan wrote: “Look, I appreciate there is only so much you can do with black and white stripes, but this really isn’t it.” And then the proposed crest was released to even more uproar.
On the surface, that reaction might seem like a designer’s worst nightmare. But, actually, I think it demonstrates something much more interesting about design: when people care enough to argue about a colour, a stripe or a badge, the design has become part of something bigger than itself.
Few other industries provoke that kind of emotional and personal connection with a brand. Type foundry F37’s multiple Football Type books capture that relationship, documenting how the beautiful game and design have influenced and shaped one another across decades.
Every summer, football becomes one of the world’s biggest design showcases. Thousands of clubs simultaneously unveil new kits, refreshed crests and updated visual identities that are scrutinised by millions before a ball has even been kicked. Some designs become instant classics, others get torn apart, and a handful divide opinion or become iconic years after their launch (usually after a successful season or having been worn by a cult legend).
Yet those reactions, good or bad, don’t change the hard work that goes on through the design process. That dedication behind it feels more intense than ever, because shirts and branding have evolved into something far bigger than what they used to be.
Kits are now more than performance wear; they are fashion pieces, cultural artefacts and visual expressions of identity. And the shirt itself is only the beginning.
Few clubs embody that better than Venezia FC, whose kits have earned a global reputation for blending football with art, architecture and luxury fashion. Speaking about designing for a club with such an aesthetic reputation, designer Diego Moscosoni describes football shirts as carrying “soul, quality and purpose”, while balancing the expectations of supporters with the identity of one of the world’s most distinctive cities.
Elsewhere, Walthamstow FC demonstrated how football design can transcend sport altogether when its William Morris-inspired shirt became the first football kit to win a prestigious D&AD Award. Even clubs with fewer obvious design credentials are embracing storytelling. Grimsby Town’s new away shirt reimagines AC Milan’s iconic 1995/96 strip, celebrating an unexpected football connection through thoughtful design rather than simply recreating a famous kit.
There is another layer of complexity for designers: the need to make a new visual identity work everywhere. A shirt can look brilliant in isolation, but the real challenge is translating its colours, patterns, typography and visual language across multiple channels without losing what makes the design distinctive. The design becomes the visual starting point for an entire season of brand communications – from launch campaigns and advertising to social content, digital experiences, customer emails and even the way the club presents itself inside the stadium.
For a designer, that might sound like a nightmare. I think it’s the opposite. You rarely get the opportunity to take one of a brand’s most visible assets, rethink it each year, and then see that idea brought to life across an entire communications ecosystem. That combination of constraint, scale, and creative freedom is what makes football such an exciting design challenge.
Collectively, these examples illustrate how football design has evolved. Designers are no longer creating next season’s shirts; they’re expected to balance branding, storytelling, fashion, history, performance and commercial objectives before making that idea work across an entire ecosystem of brand communications. Honestly, I’m not sure there’s a more demanding brief anywhere else in design.
According to ESPN, clubs typically begin discussing concepts 18 to 20 months before a shirt is released. David Bruce, who was until recently Sunderland’s chief business officer, observes that every concept must be “anchored in purpose”, with designers looking far beyond the upcoming season to identify stories worth telling.
For Sunderland, that story became the Keel Crossing bridge connecting the city centre to the Stadium of Light. Lead designer Rob Revel studied architectural drawings supplied by the local council, hand-sketched dozens of patterns inspired by the bridge, and ultimately produced more than 60 different concepts before arriving at the final design.
I think his most revealing observation is also his simplest: “The design is the easy part.”
By launch day, every colour, pattern, collar detail and graphic has likely been debated countless times. Hidden references have been researched, heritage carefully considered, and commercial requirements balanced against creative ambition.
Then, within minutes of launch, millions of supporters decide whether they love it or hate it. That’s because football supporters don’t simply buy into a brand; they feel ownership over it in a way no other sport, and arguably no other industry, can replicate.
A football badge isn’t just a logo, and club colours aren’t simply a visual identity. And a football club isn’t just any old club, as my hero Sir Bobby Robson put it: “It’s a small boy clambering up stadium steps for the first time, gripping his father’s hand, gawping at that hallowed stretch of turf beneath him and, without being able to do a thing about it, falling in love.”
So the badge that represents that isn’t just a badge: it’s a symbol that supporters inherit, celebrate and build lifelong memories around. Changing even the smallest detail can feel deeply personal, which is perhaps why football demonstrates the subjectivity of design more clearly than almost any other industry and teaches us more about our relationship with design.
Football is tribal by nature, too. In past decades, you could have probably gotten away with a little bit of a rival colour on your kit – things like this are now often a no-go – and designers do appreciate it, even if sometimes they get it wrong.
Moscosoni understands this dynamic better than most. Reflecting on Venezia’s creative process, he explained: “We approach each design decision with the fans in mind, because they don’t hold back if something feels off. I respect that. It keeps us honest.”
That pressure can be relentless. Newcastle’s so-called barcode shirt is only the latest example, while Leeds United’s proposed crest redesign in 2018 became one of football’s most infamous branding backlashes, forcing the club to abandon the project after widespread supporter criticism and some good-natured humour from other football clubs.
Not every backlash is immediately resolved. Cardiff City’s 2012 switch from blue to red, and from bluebird to dragon, was defended at the time as a move for global appeal. It never won supporters over. Three years and sustained protest later, the club reverted. Time doesn’t rehabilitate every decision.
And fan expectations are only one of the constraints designers have to navigate. Creating something distinctive can be difficult when the underlying kit can itself be constrained.
Template designs have become increasingly common, giving clubs less control over the fundamental shape and construction of their shirts and making it harder to create something that feels genuinely unique. That makes the club and manufacturers who push beyond the template particularly interesting, whether through custom detailing, unusual materials, local storytelling or collaborations with smaller kit makers.
It’s easy to forget that no football shirt or badge is the work of one individual. Every concept passes through designers, creative directors, manufacturers, commercial teams, and club executives before it ever reaches supporters. Yet criticism is often felt most strongly by the designer, regardless of how many people have shaped the final outcome.
That scrutiny can feel tough at times, but it’s also a reward in itself. If people are debating a design passionately, it means you’ve created something that matters to them. That’s precisely what makes football such a rewarding creative industry, especially when things do go right.
Moscosoni argues that football shirts are fundamentally different from traditional fashion because they are worn with “pride, emotion, and memory”.
James Webb, the Adidas designer behind Arsenal’s kits, has made a similar point, explaining that the best football shirts tap into the culture of the club they represent rather than simply looking good. Increasingly, designers are spending as much time researching local history, supporter traditions, and cultural trends as they do creating concepts.
It’s also why football has become one of the clearest examples of how attitudes towards design change with time.
Some of today’s most celebrated shirts were heavily criticised when they first appeared. England’s Admiral home shirt, worn between 1980 and 1983, broke away from decades of minimalist white kits with bold red, white and blue chest bands. Traditionalists criticised it for being too commercial, too flashy, and too different from what an England shirt should look like.
More than 40 years later, it’s viewed completely differently. The shirt is now celebrated as a pioneering design that helped transform football kits into fashion pieces as well as sporting uniform, with its influence still visible across today’s retro-inspired collections.
