# Highlights from Homo Faber 2026 – ‘An Island of Light’

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/highlights-from-homo-faber-2026-an-island-of-light/)

If you’ve not heard of Homo Faber and your job is designing and crafting pieces of beauty and fascination, then it’s time you paid attention. This biennial event held in Venice presents the work of 500 artisans and designers from over 70 countries to the public, press, and industry. The event takes place in the quiet beauty of the island of San Giorgio Maggiore, at the Fondazione Giorgio Cini.
It represents a chance to escape from the digital world many of us increasingly reside in for our work – and experience a different one, where objects are created as unique pieces. The sheer effort that goes into craft can sometimes feel wasteful to a busy mind, but it’s an opportunity for us to explore a tactile life that exists away from computer screens. We’d all do better for spending a bit of our time crafting. Some pieces will turn out to be available for a reasonable fee to those of us who want to beautify our homes or workspaces, and others will result in commissions from those who can afford it.
While high-end luxury can sometimes feel distant from everyday life, this market plays a vital role in keeping heritage skills alive. High-value commissions allow craftspeople to be fairly compensated for hundreds of hours of painstaking labour, turning modest raw materials like clay or paper into sustainable livelihoods and celebrated art.
The most beautiful pieces made in this world will be kept, cherished and preserved for generations to come. Even mass-produced items can, in some cases, achieve this status whenever craft was an important part of the design process.
Consequently, for inspiration and admiration, we present a small selection of our highlights from hundreds of excellent pieces and exhibitions displayed at Homo Faber 2026, with the exhibition titled ‘An Island of Light’:
‘A Great Turning’
The British artist Es Devlin’s installation is one of the first things you see after exiting the entry ‘indexes’ that set the scene and expectations for what’s to come. Initially, you have a corridor of black and white images, the Artisans Index, showing all the craftspeople who are displaying their work at Homo Faber. After that, you enter a large space designed as an artisanal workshop with the tools and materials of craft all around you… this is a waiting space where, soon, an introductory audio visual wall will light up and guide you through the spirit of the event.
As you exit the interior, blinking into the light, you immediately see ‘A Great Turning’ which does, indeed, turn on two axes. As it rotates it reflects light onto the surroundings, or reflects back to you a different part of the old monastery we stand within. Reflected in water, it’s a striking example of how modern machinery and materials can live in harmony with an ancient building.
‘An Infinite Birdsong’
If you’re someone who likes shiny things, this room is your dream. It will take your breath away. You enter via a curtain designed to minimise the amount of outside light that leaks in. And immediately you pass through that curtain it hits you hard. It was so eye-catching that we finally decided to reawaken our YouTube account after a ten year hiatus:
The light is startling because it repeats and repeats and repeats in every direction you look. Up, down, left and right. You’re in an infinite mirror box full of jewelled masterpieces, surrounded by fine artwork in glassed off niches. It’s hard to know where to look and it took me a few moments to realise what was happening.
‘Il Mondo Perfetto’
This 1.4m high sculpture is crafted primarily from Lego bricks and was built especially for the event. It depicts a cathedral unravelling at its peak and balances control and chaos. It can be seen in the Feast of Light room. Its complexity and fragility stood out. This artisan works almost entirely with Lego.
‘A Full Moon Rising’
As we entered this exhibition space, the ‘moon’ was invisible at its lowest setting and about to start rising from the waters of Fondazione Giorgio Cini’s former swimming pool. Crafted from paper, glass, metal, porcelain and many other materials, the display reveals a selection of moon jars and similar vases in watery colours. The structure showcases how inspiration can be taken from the moon and the sea in a dialogue of light and water. Each vessel on the display is also shown around the edges of the pool.
Austin Stern and Andrés Anza in ‘A Rainbow of Forms’
The ‘Rainbow of Forms’ room is a riot of colour and shape. It’s not a room for the easily distracted thanks to mirrors backing each shelf, and another set of mirrors on the ceiling above. You’ll not be sure of where to look. It’s definitely a room for those who want to see something different and inspirational. The floor rug is a colour theorist’s interior dream. We hope it finds its way into a home somewhere.
Andrés Anza’s red ceramic ‘Traditional Overflow’ rested atop one shelf – daring you to anthropomorphise it into living. And nearby, three other anthropomorphic characters in blown glass by Austin Stern – ‘Crinklefaces’, ‘Pile of Trials’, and ‘State of Play’ – making those who noticed them smile.
Craft workshops in Kintsugi and Hanko
As a group, we were ushered into a workshop to learn how to carve a Hanko block. Surrounded by an installation of painted silk banners by Taró Nordberg, a Swiss-Japanese artist specialising in ink-wash painting and calligraphy, there was no doubt that we were going to learn an element of Japanese craft tradition. These workshops represent part of car manufacturer Mazda’s support of Homo Faber as country sponsor for Japan, along with a programme where masters are paired with rising talents – the output of which, ‘Liminal’, was selected for display here.
Antonin Martineau from France guided us through the process of taking a piece of walnut and carving it to leave, in relief, a selected word in Kanji. I chose 影 (kage) which in this case is taken to represent shadow, or the absence of light. Other blocks included those to represent light and Ma – the Japanese appreciation of meaningful space.
You spend nearly an hour delicately carving away with precise chisels, occasionally hearing curses from neighbours as they accidentally go too far, before ending with a block of walnut you can stamp paper with. Hopefully. It’s a surprisingly thoughtful process as you carefully and delicately guide the chisels with millimetre precision, regularly checked by Antonin who would say “just a little more wood to remove from there”, or a somewhat more assertive “don’t do that, it’s dangerous.” Happily, not one of us shed blood that day.
Sadly, there wasn’t time to also take part in the Kintsugi workshop. But suddenly I find myself searching Google for local crafting classes in the hope of perhaps helping me escape the screens I spend 12 hours of my days staring at.
In summary, the exhibition is so much more than what is happening on the island. Workshops and experiences are being promoted across Venice and the world, with over a hundred in Britain alone. Examples include lessons in marquetry, clay throwing, weaving or steam bending of wood, and experiences such as visits ranging from printmakers and automata builders to see them at work. Some are free, but many carry a fee which helps support our craft heritage.
Homo Faber runs from the 1st to the 30th of September on the Venetian island of San Giorgio Maggiore – it costs €16 and €10 for the workshops mentioned above.
Design’s not dead. But it’s under pressure.
