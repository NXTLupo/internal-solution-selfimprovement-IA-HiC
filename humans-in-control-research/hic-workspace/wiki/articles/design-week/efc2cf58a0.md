# 2026’s Design and Technology GCSE results mapped and what they tell us

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/2026s-design-and-technology-gcse-results-mapped-and-what-they-tell-us/)

IMPORTANT: During the preparation of this article we inadvertently included placeholder test data that threw off all of our analysis and rendered much of the data incorrect, something that was only spotted after publishing. The following article corrects this.
As the happy servant of a sixteen-year-old collecting his GCSE results today, I can confirm that, like many parents, the experience is equal parts exciting and anxiety-inducing. Sadly, I couldn’t persuade him to choose Design and Technology.
This is a familiar frustration. Out of the typical nine GCSEs, creative and practical subjects get squeezed out by the pressure of ‘hard’ academic priorities. Once you lock in the three Sciences, English, English Literature, Maths, a language, and History or Geography, a child is left with essentially one free slot where they can choose something a little different. Driven in part by the EBacc push of recent years, subjects like Art, Drama, Music, Computer Studies, and Design and Technology end up fiercely fighting for that final spot. For my son, a die-hard sports nut, PE won out. I didn’t stand a chance of convincing him otherwise.
The broader consequence of this timetable crush is stark. Analysing recent Ofqual data, we mapped out how regional take-up of GCSE Design and Technology has evolved around England since 2019. Sadly in the time available to create the article we were unable to accurately track Northern Ireland and Wales due to their different data structures. The bleakest fallout is concentrated across the post-industrial North and Midlands. In the North East, subject uptake has dropped from 8.7% in 2019 to a stark 4.9% in 2026 – representing a 43.7% relative collapse in student participation and shedding more than 1,300 candidate entries. Yorkshire and The Humber suffered a similarly brutal decline, where participation contracted by 33.8% over the same period to sit at just 4.9%. Meanwhile, the West Midlands endured the single largest volume loss in the country, shedding 2,250 entries as regional uptake slipped from 9.6% to 6.9%. In former manufacturing heartlands where technical expertise and engineering heritage ought to be systematically nurtured, state schools are progressively shuttering workshops and reallocating starved department budgets toward traditional core subjects.
By contrast, London and the South East have proved far more resilient to the squeeze. The South East experienced the nation’s mildest relative downturn – falling 20.2% from 10.4% to 8.3% – allowing it to maintain the highest overall uptake rate in England. Greater London similarly cushioned the blow, shedding just 1,085 entries (a relative decline of 25.6%) to land at 6.1%. Supported by greater local resource pooling and higher concentrations of well-funded academies, southern pupils remain significantly more likely to retain access to specialist machinery, digital fabrication tools, and qualified design educators.
Here at Design Week, we understand the bind schools face when timetables tighten – especially for pupils tagged as high achievers. Yet design is vital to our collective future. As our recent article about Burnham’s new leadership shows, design shapes everything from our built environment and healthcare to public services and safety. Seeing classroom participation drop so sharply feels disheartening.
However, a closer look at the grade outcomes reveals an intriguing paradox.
Discounting the pandemic spikes of 2020–2022, overall achievement in Design and Technology across England has seen a steady, structural uptick. Grade 5 or above (strong pass) outcomes climbed from 48.5% in 2019 to 52.7% in 2026.
This isn’t because the exams got easier. Rather, as total English candidate entries contracted from roughly 87,600 to 74,200 under EBacc pressure, non-specialist entry streams dropped away. What remains is a highly self-selected cohort: passionate pupils backed by dedicated, well-resourced D&T departments.
Regional performance mirrors this shift. Greater London (61.1%) and the South East (58.0%) lead the country in Grade 5+ outcomes. Yet structural inequality persists: a 14.1 percentage point gap separates London from the lowest-performing regions, such as the West Midlands (47.0%), East Midlands (47.1%), and North East (47.4%). This disparity is driven largely by unequal regional access to CAD/CAM software, modern workshop equipment, and specialist teaching staff.
So, how should we read these results? On one hand, many brilliant designers never studied the subject at school – they discovered their craft through problem-solving in other fields, foundational lessons up to Year 9, or self-directed online learning. Today’s young people have unprecedented access to tools and information.
On the other hand, a shrinking, highly specialised talent pool raises an urgent question for our industry: is this self-selection a sign of student resilience, or are we watching design education retreat into a privileged niche that desperately needs policy intervention? Please comment below.
Design’s not dead. But it’s under pressure.
