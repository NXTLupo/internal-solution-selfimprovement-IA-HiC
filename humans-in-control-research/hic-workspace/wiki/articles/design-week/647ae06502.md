# Beyond brazilcore: finding ‘originality by origin’

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/beyond-brazilcore-finding-originality-by-origin/)

This summer, it’s been interesting to see how the world has embraced Brazilian culture – or more specifically, the irresistible vibe and charm that emanates from our nation.
Vogue and a run of other titles spent the season asking how international brands should ‘tap into Brazil’, as football shirts and green and yellow colours turned up at runway shows and on the streets under the banner of ‘Brazilcore’.
It’s tempting to read this as another fleeting trend, but for me, as the newly returned global chief creative officer of Brazil-born Tátil Design, that reading misses the point.
‘Brazilcore’ isn’t the story. It’s a symptom of something bigger – a fatigue across the creative industry, with brand worlds that could belong anywhere, and therefore nowhere in particular.
The world is tiring of sameness. But the answer isn’t simply more colour, more ‘Brazil’. It’s more originality – and to achieve that, you need to tap into origin stories.
The paradox of proximity
Brazilian creatives, including myself, can be guilty of focusing on what’s ‘out there’ in order to achieve global relevance. But as I’ve found out after many years in the industry, the answer is on our doorstep.
I recently spent a year in Manhattan as the first Latin American executive creative director at a major global design agency, working inside one of the industry’s most connected environments, surrounded by the networks and opportunities that global creative capitals promise. And there, I found something unexpected: proximity to everything can mean connection to nothing that is entirely your own.
Globalisation gives creative talent extraordinary access, but that can have a flattening effect. The same references circulate through every studio, the same agencies chase the same global briefs and, gradually, the work produced starts to sound like it came from nowhere at all. My time abroad was a wonderful experience – and being immersed in so many cultures and diasporas sharpened rather than diluted my sense of where I was from. Distance made me more conscious of origin, not less.
That experience is now shaping my return to Tátil and the broader case the agency is making to the industry: that the traditional trajectory – leave Brazil, build credibility in London or New York, bring the experience home – has the causality backwards. Real originality was never something you found ‘out there’.
I now advocate for the idea of a ‘reverse creative diaspora’ – a movement back towards our origins, not as an act of nostalgia, but as a way of reclaiming our creative potential.
Originality has an origin
This is the idea at the centre of Tátil’s thinking, and it’s deliberately uncomfortable for an industry that has long treated ‘different’ as a design brief. Originality doesn’t come from consciously trying to look unlike everyone else. It comes from having somewhere genuine to look from.
Brazil actually has a century-old word for this. In 1928, the modernist poet Oswald de Andrade published his Manifesto Antropófago, which used cannibalism as a metaphor for how Brazilian culture absorbs outside influence. Rather than rejecting or imitating foreign ideas, it consumes them and produces something new and distinctly its own. That instinct – to digest, remix, and transform rather than copy – has shaped Brazilian creativity for a century, and it’s the real engine behind everything we do at Tátil. It has nothing to do with a flag or a colour palette.
Origin isn’t a synonym for nationality, geography, or a visual shorthand for ‘Brazilian’. It isn’t just green and yellow. It can be history, belief, lived experience, environment, collective memory, or simply a particular way of seeing the world – evidenced and rooted, not invented for effect.
That kind of origin gives work a substance that pure aesthetic borrowing never quite manages. For Tátil, that’s the difference between adding cultural flavour to somebody else’s idea and originating the idea itself. The question stops being ‘How do we make this feel Brazilian?’ and becomes ‘What is the genuine point of origin for this idea?’
Proof, not just philosophy
Our identity for the Rio 2016 Olympic and Paralympic Games, for example, never had to abandon its Brazilian origin to achieve global relevance. We took a similar approach with Rio Carnival, where the brief only made sense once we brought samba dancers, artists and the communities who run the parades into the process alongside us. That is origination, not localisation.
That distinction matters more now than it did a decade ago, because the industry’s centre of gravity is visibly shifting. Brands are noticing that a handful of connected global capitals often produce interchangeable work.
Brazil’s opportunity isn’t simply to be noticed when a trend cycle turns its way. It’s to challenge how the industry defines where good ideas are allowed to come from in the first place.
London and New York’s opportunity, in turn, is to recognise that the most valuable creative relationships going forward may not be the ones that pull every studio towards the same centre, but the ones that let different origins stay visible, distinct and whole.
Ricardo Bezerra is global chief creative officer at Tátil Design.
Design’s not dead. But it’s under pressure.
