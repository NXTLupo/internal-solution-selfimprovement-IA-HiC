# Accidental greenwashing – Chacho Puebla on the launch of his anti-greenwashing course

Source: [design_week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/accidental-greenwashing-chacho-puebla-on-the-launch-of-his-anti-greenwashing-course/)

With new European Union greenwashing penalties now taking effect and high-profile brand claims coming under scrutiny, advertising veteran turned sustainability consultant Chacho Puebla argues that designers are inadvertently misleading consumers. Here, he shares with us his journey from agency executive to impact consultant, and explains why sustainability literacy must become a core creative skill.
The accidental greenwasher in design
Designers and creative agencies excel at simplifying complex ideas into instant visual signals. However, when sustainability knowledge in the studio is thin, those same visual tools – muted colour palettes, leaf imagery, organic textures, and circular icons – can manufacture an environmental virtue that the underlying product simply cannot substantiate.
The problem is rarely deliberate deceit; rather, it stems from a gap in fundamental sustainability literacy. A product contains a minor tweak in materials or a single responsibly sourced ingredient, and the visual identity suddenly radiates environmental perfection. Legal approval at the tail end of a campaign cannot fix a concept built on a poor understanding of environmental evidence.
Design Week: You’ve argued that the creative industries have created a generation of “accidental greenwashers”. How does this happen in practice?
Chacho Puebla: “I think mainly because of a lack of information. When I talk with friends who are still working in traditional agencies, they say, ‘Oh, I didn’t know’. Of course they knew who was the last winner in Cannes, or what was the last season of whatever series they’re watching, but they didn’t know about this.
“Take pizza packaging, for example. If paper has oil on it, like pizza paper, it can’t be recycled. If such a brand gives you a cardboard box and says ‘100% recyclable’, it’s not true because you cannot recycle that cardboard once it has fat on it. Or look at packaging like BuzzBallz – they say 100% recyclable, but there is a metal ring on the plastic container that you cannot easily detach. Some recycling centres just put it into the general bin because they cannot separate it.
“If you put ‘100% recyclable’, it’s like wallpaper. But if you put ‘84.5% recyclable’, that means, ‘Wow, these guys really know what they’re talking about’. It gives you an entrance to communicate with real precision.”
From Cannes Lions to systemic impact
Puebla’s perspective is built on over 30 years in traditional advertising and five years dedicated strictly to sustainability strategy. Formerly Executive Creative Director at LOLA MullenLowe, and Regional Creative Director for Latin America, he has collected over 500 awards, including more than 90 Cannes Lions and 20 Grand Prix.
Despite reaching the pinnacle of ad-land success, Puebla experienced a profound personal reckoning. Encouraged by his partner, Pil, and a former environment minister who challenged him to use his skills “for humanity rather than capital,” he stepped away from network agency life. In February 2020, he launched Felicidad, an impact-driven collective, navigating the sudden arrival of the COVID-19 pandemic, a young family, and financial strain to build an ethically aligned studio practice.
Chacho Puebla at a glance
- Experience: 30+ years in advertising, 5+ years in sustainability
- Former roles: CCO at MullenLowe Western Europe, ECD at LOLA
- Key accolades: 500+ awards, 90+ Cannes Lions, 20 Grand Prix
- Current ventures: Founder of Felicidad, course creator at Do Epic Good
Design Week: Moving away from traditional advertising metrics to impact consulting is a massive shift. What has that transition taught you about real impact?
Chacho Puebla: “The thing I am most proud of in Felicidad was our work in Ecuador. We were close to the guy who was going to be minister of environment, and we helped create a decree by law where all government ministries had to talk first with the ministry of environmental transition. If someone wanted to do something in education or infrastructure, they had to look at the environmental compliance first.
“Thanks to that decree, we protected species, organised street waste collectors, and improved the lives of 10% of Ecuadorians. That gives me more pride than all the awards that have been won in my life.”
The EU anti-greenwashing shift: why precision is the new design code
The timing of this creative literacy reckoning is not coincidental. Stricter European regulations regarding environmental claims come into force on 27 September, introducing steep penalties for brands using unsubstantiated claims like “eco-friendly”, “natural”, or “climate neutral”.
Puebla emphasises that designers must adapt to a new visual and verbal language centred on radical precision.
Design Week: With strict European regulatory deadlines taking effect, how should design language adapt?
Chacho Puebla: “On 27th September, European law is going to change, and there will be penalties for saying things like ‘eco-friendly’ or ‘sustainable’ if you make false claims. Vague terms don’t work anymore.
“We created this course with Do Epic Good because we needed concrete tools. It’s designed warm and human, like a podcast series, giving guides for design juries, agencies, and entrepreneurs. If you have a brand and want to avoid greenwashing, or if you’re in an agency facing conflicts with briefs, it gives you the practical tools to ask the right questions before you design.”
EU Anti-Greenwashing Rules: Key Facts for Designers
- Legal deadline: Under Directive (EU) 2024/825 (Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition), member state enforcement and strict penalties for misleading environmental claims apply from 27 September 2026.
- Generic terms banned: Blanket marketing descriptors such as ‘eco-friendly’, ‘green’, ‘clean’, ‘climate friendly’, and ‘sustainable’ are strictly prohibited without verifiable proof of excellent environmental performance.
- Precision over vague claims: Packaging and advertising must replace overarching promises (such as ‘100% eco’) with specific, quantifiable data (such as ‘contains 84.5% post-consumer recycled material’).
- Material lifecycle limits: Creative identities cannot mask systemic recycling barriers – design choices that combine inseparable materials (such as metal rings on plastic) or grease-contaminated elements render claims inaccurate in real-world facilities.
- Uncertified labels prohibited: Brand-created ‘green’ badges or internal sustainability symbols are banned unless they are established by public authorities or backed by an independent, approved certification scheme.
- Official resources: Review full regulatory guidance and compliance toolkits on the European Commission Sustainable Consumption Portal.
AI, physical craft, and the future of creative value
As digital automation and generative AI sweep through creative workflows, Puebla views the technology through a candid lens. While AI will commoditise average, transactional design labour, he believes it will inadvertently trigger a renaissance in physical craft and personal, human connection.
Design Week: How do you see the rise of AI impacting the role of human creatives and design ethics?
Chacho Puebla: “AI will handle the standard communication, but it’s going to be very expensive to work with humans who have a special reputation. Out of 100 of brands, 98% don’t care about creativity – they just care about money. But the 2% that care about real creativity will work with top human creatives.
“Online traffic is flooded with automated bots now. That is why I think there will be a push back to personal encounters. People really want to meet other people, talk, drink, and have memories. I’ve started making handmade books and drawings. Craft is going to make a comeback, and maybe AI frees us from the computer and brings us back to physical craft.”
