# 9 Fine Porcelain Brands Every Design Lover Should Know About (2026)

Source: [ad_main](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/fine-porcelain-brands)

With historic roots in royal courts and centuries-old ateliers, porcelain has a certain gravitas that can make any weeknight meal feel like an occasion. Fired at the highest temperatures of any ceramic, its thin, delicate build also belies its versatility and durability. Over the past few years, the material has been eclipsed by more affordable, heavier crafted pieces in clay, but the pendulum is swinging back as more young people rediscover heritage names such as Ginori and Wedgwood that might have filled the china cabinets of their grandparents. Below, some of the fine porcelain brands that are bringing the high-gloss finish back into rotation.
Ginori 1735: The Italian Classic
Ginori 1735’s name is synonymous with elegance and heritage, beloved by design lovers and homemakers the world over. Founded in 1735 outside Florence by Marquis Carlo Andrea Ginori, the house set out to make Italy’s finest porcelain, working what Ginori called “white gold.” Its most recognizable pattern, Oriente Italiano, dates back to when Gio Ponti was artistic director and features a Chinoiserie-inspired floral design in saturated tones and rimmed in gold.
It’s still in production almost a century later, but is now rendered in even more vibrant iterations thanks to a period during which former Gucci creative director Alessandro Michele steered it in a more youthful, colorful direction. Of course, Ginori 1735’s offering now extends beyond dinnerware and servingware into decor and home fragrances. You can even dine among all things Ginori 1735 in various Café Ginori in cities worldwide.
Villeroy & Boch: Graphic & Grounded
The Germany-based porcelain house Villeroy & Boch got its start in 1748, after founder François Boch took up the family business of industrial pottery from his father. Its range leans in a bolder direction, from floral motifs to more Deco-style designs like the MetroChic collection which incorporates geometric black-and-gold details. Another highlight is the La Boule set originally designed by Helen von Boch in 1971: a set of bowls and plates that stack into a sphere when not in use.
Royal Copenhagen: The Blue-And-White Pick
Royal Copenhagen is Denmark’s oldest porcelain house. It’s known for its signature hand-painted blue floral motif that’s remained mostly the same since 1775, reappearing across teapots, cake stands, sauce boats, and beyond. Known as Blue Fluted, the original design is painted freehand, so no two pieces are alike, and even current variations still carry the number “1” stamped underneath as a nod to where it all started. These pieces are made for daily use, and mixing and matching across other collections is highly encouraged. It’s also worth mentioning Arje Griegst’s delightful Triton line of seashell-inspired dinnerware that is so coveted among collectors, the brand reissued archival pieces from the line this year.
Lenox: An American Staple
Lenox is America’s answer to European porcelain, founded in 1889 as a small art studio producing hand-painted ivory china. At the time, almost all fine porcelain sold in the US was imported, so stateside production was a milestone that helped it become the first American brand tapped to produce official china for the White House. While its wares lean understated and classic (like the 24 Seven line that suggests its daily applications), its scalloped French Perle Scallop shows a more playful side with its ruffled edges: dressy enough for company but tough enough for a Tuesday night.
Wedgwood: The Shape-Shifter
Wedgwood is hard to pin down to just one look, and that’s sort of the point. The English brand has been around since 1759, and is known for pushing the boundaries of fine bone china into more uncharted territory. Mythical Creatures, designed by Kit Kemp of London’s Firmdale Hotels, is a playful take on fine bone china that comes decorated with folk-art birds and beasts, then finished with a gold rim Kemp customizes herself. Gio Gold on the other hand is a collection defined by clean, geometric patterns on white china, banded in gold and minimal enough to disappear into a table setting.
J.L. Coquet: The One Chefs Can’t Get Enough Of
J.L. Coquet is the fine porcelain brand that has become immortalized among Michelin-starred restaurants and beloved by five-star chefs. The house was founded in 1824, near Limoges, France, but it wasn’t until 1963 when it started producing the kinds of collections that put it on the map. Its signature Hémisphère features fine grooves encircling each plate like rings around a planet. The brand also recently partnered with the cognac house LOUIS XIII on its first-ever “Art de la Table” collections, developing 11 new porcelain techniques for the project as proof of its luxury cachet.
Mikasa: The Wedding Registry Staple
Mikasa is a more practical choice among its fine porcelain peers. It started in California in 1948 as a dinnerware importer and built a reputation on producing good-looking dishes without the fuss. Swirl Bone is the most straightforward design in its library with its clean, white design and subtle patterned rim. It’s the kind of set that frequently shows up across wedding registries. Akira Vegan Bone ventures into more color and botanical inspiration, all made from the same traditional bone china material.
Bernardaud: The Collector’s Choice
Bernardaud can oscillate from centuries-old royal reproduction to contemporary designs with ease. This Limoges-based house has spent decades working with artists like Marc Chagall, Jeff Koons, and JR to reinterpret its porcelain, so its collections feels a lot more similar to limited-edition art than purely functional china. But it holds its own when it comes to history and flourish, too. Its Albertine collection incorporates delicate sketches of birds and foliage climbing the porcelain, while its Marie-Antoinette line is an inspired reproduction decorated with the queen’s favorite flower.
Augarten: The Playful, High-End Option
Augarten’s caviar service costs $9,850 for a set, which tells you a lot right off the bat. Founded in Vienna in 1718, Augarten is Europe’s second-oldest porcelain manufactory, and each piece is still made entirely by hand in the same historic building. Its signature style is the delicate Viennese Rose, which offers a romantic floral pattern. It has also collaborated with designer Giambattista Valli in the past on botanical plates and fruit-shaped porcelain boxes that are as much sculpture as dinnerware, bringing joy and color to a well-curated tablescape.
