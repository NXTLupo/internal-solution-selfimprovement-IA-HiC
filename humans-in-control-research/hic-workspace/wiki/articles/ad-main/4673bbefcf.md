# This Upstate New York Home Made of Reflective Sheets of Aluminum Is Designed for Freedom and Flexibility

Source: [ad_main](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/max-worrell-and-jejon-young-hudson-valley-home)

On first approach, one might think the home of Max Worrell and Jejon Yeung isn’t a home at all. Could it be a storage shed? Maybe a shipping container? Perhaps a land art installation? For the couple, founders of Worrell Yeung, that obfuscation is entirely the point. Situated on six acres of woodland, the 1,500-square-foot retreat marks the first house they’ve designed for themselves since launching their celebrated joint practice 11 years ago. Clad in reflective sheets of corrugated aluminum that extend six inches into the ground, the square structure invites curiosity. But it is also an exacting study in mathematics, comprising a series of cubic modules (each one seven feet by seven feet by seven feet). All of the windows—and even the kitchen island and the couple’s bed platform—conform to the same dimensions. Outside, a 7-by-14-foot plunge pool was installed in the yard.
That rigor is contrasted by a free-flowing layout optimized for indoor-outdoor living. The duo dub it their “open cabin” though Yeung jokes there is only “one month a year” when the couple can leave doors and windows open to the elements. Relaxed and inviting, the interiors are organized around a T-shaped volume, clad in ash wood, that contains two skylit bathrooms and storage. “We were doing a lot of these in our city projects,” Yeung says of that functional central core, now a signature of their residential work. “When we started getting ground-up house commissions we realized it’s how we like to design and understand space.”
The living room, kitchen, and dining area effectively occupy one half of the home, the private quarters the other. (The latter include the primary bedroom, a flexible salon with Yeung’s piano and a Murphy bed for guests, and a wood-paneled den.) Spaces flow into one another so that the couple, home alone, can live as if in one big room—a mutable experience heightened by the lack of window treatments. Vintage pieces sourced from auctions, including designs by Le Corbusier, Mies van der Rohe, Gae Aulenti, and Piero Castiglioni, add a sense of lived-in ease.
Designing for freedom and flexibility was partly in response to the couple’s “normative” (as Worrell puts it) residence in Brooklyn: a cookie-cutter co-op in a neighborhood increasingly known more for strollers than bucking the status quo. The couple also took inspiration from the steel-and mirror environmental installations of American artist Dan Graham. Evident too are the gay architectural legacies of Horace Gifford, who designed many of the flat-roofed modernist vacation homes in Fire Island Pines; Philip Johnson, who shared his own box-shaped Glass House with his partner, David Whitney; and Paul Rudolph, the former chairman of Yale School of Architecture, where Worrell and Yeung first met. Hence the paprika rug, a nod to Yale’s iconic carpeting.
Exposure and retreat, two queered modes of moving through the world, create an underlying tension. “We play with what is concealed and what is revealed,” says Yeung. “There are a lot of diagonal views into other rooms and spaces. Nothing is straightforward.” Certain elements, like the square kitchen island, were also, he says, “tests” for ideas that were presented to clients but never executed. “Now clients can come to us and see what works,” says Worrell. “They realize what’s possible.”
