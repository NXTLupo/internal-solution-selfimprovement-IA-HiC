## **Operationalizing Controls in Enterprise AI**

An engineer asks an AI coding agent to investigate a support ticket. Hidden in the ticket is an instruction the engineer never authorized. The agent follows it, retrieves a deployment configuration, and sends it to an external destination.

The identity provider logs a successful authentication. The tool server records an authorized read. Network telemetry shows an outbound connection. Every component appears to have done what it was permitted to do. Yet the sequence produces an outcome no one authorized. Would your security team recognize it?

That is the challenge as AI moves into enterprise production. We spend considerable effort establishing policies, vetting vendors, and deciding which capabilities employees may use. But approving a service does not tell us whether its controls remain effective when agents begin acting across systems.

Agents can retrieve sensitive information, call application programming interfaces (APIs), modify records, execute code, and initiate business processes, often using legitimate credentials. Traditional security controls remain essential. What changes is the need to recognize when individually permitted actions combine into an unauthorized outcome.

In my previous article, Governing AI in the Enterprise, I discussed why AI governance requires a coordinated operating model. The next challenge is proving that its controls can be tested, monitored, and trusted in production.

## Governance Does Not Equal Enforcement

One of the most important questions in enterprise AI security is not simply whether we have controls, but whether we know that they work. An architecture review identifies the required safeguards: approved tools, limited permissions, and logging. Engineering implements them. But what happens when a configuration changes, a new Model Context Protocol (MCP) server is added, a model is upgraded, or the gateway fails?

An approval tells us what was reviewed at a point in time. It does not prove those protections will still be effective months later. Security architecture should therefore define not only the control requirements, but also how the organization will verify that they remain in place.

Many organizations have not reached that point yet. IBM’s 2026 Cost of a Data Breach Report found that 21% of the breached organizations it studied had an AI-related breach, up from 13% a year earlier. Of those with an AI-related breach, 92% lacked proper AI access controls such as role-based access and multifactor authentication. IT approval for AI deployments, the most common governance control, also slipped from 45% to 38%. These findings point to familiar access and governance gaps. The work starts with controls most teams already know how to run.

Design evidence defines the required controls. Deployment evidence shows how they were implemented. Operational evidence, including tests, events, and monitoring, shows whether they continue to function. None of these is sufficient on its own, but together they provide a stronger basis for assurance.

Figure 1. Design, deployment, and operational evidence show whether approved controls remain effective.

A deployment check may confirm that an agent uses approved MCP servers, yet miss a local override or a change in behavior behind a remote endpoint. Runtime enforcement and visibility must close that gap. Ask repeatable questions: Is the agent using the correct identity? Are its integrations still approved? Is telemetry reaching the security operations center (SOC)? Can its access be revoked? Each answer needs evidence, not assumptions.

Much of this depends on good data governance. Risk tiers and data-loss prevention controls rely on accurate classification. Gateway policies also need to understand which data and actions are in scope. Retrieval systems must preserve or enforce source-system permissions, including restrictions on what generated responses can disclose. Prompts, outputs, and agent memory also require appropriate ownership, classification, access controls, and retention. When agents write into systems of record, we should be able to identify which entries were generated by a model.

## The Identity Conundrum

One familiar identity issue is employees using personal accounts to access approved AI services. The enterprise may have configured single sign-on, data protections, and audit settings, but personal sessions can fall outside those controls and contractual safeguards. Endpoint, browser, and network protections may still apply; simply blocking a destination, however, cannot distinguish a personal account from the approved enterprise tenant. Where supported, tenant restrictions and identity-aware controls can help. Employees also need a practical approved alternative, or missing capabilities and slow processes will encourage unmanaged use.

The second issue is more complex. Who is acting when an AI agent performs a task? An employee may authorize the work, but the agent can use multiple tools, retrieve information from several systems, and continue acting over time. If every operation uses the employee’s credentials, attribution becomes difficult and containment may disrupt the employee rather than the agent.

An enterprise should not treat an AI agent as an extension of the person who launched it. As agents become persistent and work asynchronously, they behave more like workload identities and should be governed that way, while still recording who delegated the work. Where supported, agents should have separately attributable identities, limited permissions, and short-lived credentials. We should be able to establish which agent acted, on whose behalf, under what authority, and against which resources. Each agent also needs a human owner who can review its scope, oversee its activity, and initiate revocation. That owner should not be expected to predict every model output.

Consequential actions that exceed defined risk thresholds or fall outside approved automation boundaries should require explicit human approval. These may include payments, permission changes, sensitive external communications, and production deployments. Approval must be bound to the specific action, target, and parameters, and an agent must not be able to authorize itself. Human review cannot compensate for excessive privileges: the architecture must still constrain what the agent can do.

## AI Infrastructure Is Becoming Mission Critical

An AI model gateway may begin as a way to centralize provider access and enforce security controls. As adoption grows, applications depend on it for request routing, credential management, guardrails, and telemetry. One lesson I learned from platform engineering is that shared infrastructure can become business-critical before anyone formally treats it that way. AI gateways follow the same pattern, with an added complication: they may enforce the policies we depend on to protect enterprise data. Availability, resilience, and recovery requirements need to evolve with that dependency.

Who owns the gateway? How are changes approved? What happens if it fails? A direct connection to a provider may restore availability while bypassing policy enforcement and logging. Any fallback must be explicitly approved and preserve essential controls and auditability. When those protections cannot be maintained, the application should fail closed. The architectural principle is to fail securely: a service failure must not create a bypass around authentication, authorization, data protection, or other required controls. Test those decisions during design, before an outage forces them.

The gateway itself is a high-value target. It may see sensitive prompts, influence enforcement decisions, and access provider credentials. Privileged access management, secrets management, change control, logging, and recovery processes all apply. Restoring traffic without restoring the guardrail configuration is not a complete recovery. AI introduces new capabilities and risks, but it does not remove our established responsibilities for securing and operating infrastructure.

## Can Your SOC Investigate the Agent?

Return to the coding agent. The identity platform knows who authenticated, the MCP server records a tool call, the endpoint logs process activity, and the network platform sees an outbound connection. Each team may see only part of the sequence. Can the SOC connect those events quickly enough to detect misuse?

Figure 2. Correlated security evidence connects the initiating user, agent, and tools to detection and response.

A meaningful investigation depends on trusted links among the initiating user, agent identity, execution or run ID, tools, access decisions, data accessed, and endpoint and network activity. Many integrations will not provide all of this natively. Discovering those gaps during an incident is too late; architecture reviews should establish the evidence security operations will need before an agent reaches production.
