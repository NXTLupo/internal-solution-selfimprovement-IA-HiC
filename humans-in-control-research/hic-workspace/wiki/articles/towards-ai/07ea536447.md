# Claude Fable 5 Is Back, and It Now Reroutes Some of Your Requests to Opus 4.8 Automatically

Source: [towards_ai](https://pub.towardsai.net/claude-fable-5-is-back-and-it-now-reroutes-some-of-your-requests-to-opus-4-8-automatically-ec2856f9665e?source=rss----98111c9905da---4)

Claude Fable 5 Is Back, and It Now Reroutes Some of Your Requests to Opus 4.8 Automatically
After roughly three weeks offline, Claude Fable 5 is available again as of July 1, and the version that returned isn’t quite the one that left. The most interesting change is a new automatic delegation system, when the model detects a request that trips its safety classifier, it hands the work off to the smaller Opus 4.8 and tells the user. That’s the headline. The details around it, tighter access economics, honest tradeoffs, and a new industry framework being drafted underneath, all matter for anyone building on Claude. Here’s the clear version.
Claude Fable 5, Anthropic’s most capable widely released model, is generally available again from Wednesday, July 1, 2026. It went offline roughly three weeks ago, and its return has been announced across the entire Claude ecosystem, Claude.ai, the Claude Platform, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork, with the cloud hyperscalers, AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Foundry, being re-enabled as quickly as those partners can bring it live.
The model that returned is the same underlying Fable 5. What changed is the safety layer around it, and that change is the actual story worth understanding, because it produces a genuinely new behavior that developers and everyday users will hit almost immediately, especially anyone who codes.
The new twist, automatic delegation to Opus 4.8
Anthropic trained a new safety classifier and shipped it with the restored Fable 5. The classifier is a small automated system that runs alongside the model during every interaction, watching for prompts and outputs that resemble a specific class of cybersecurity work the company wants to gate more carefully. It targets the exact technique reported by outside researchers, and the company says it now blocks that technique in more than 99 percent of attempts.
The interesting part isn’t the blocking. It’s what happens after the block. When Fable 5’s classifier decides a request shouldn’t be answered by Fable 5, the request is automatically rerouted to the smaller Opus 4.8, and the user is notified that this has happened. In other words, Fable 5 has a built-in delegation to a less capable sibling for tasks it declines to handle. The user still gets an answer, just from a different model, and the response continues in the same conversation.
For most everyday use, most of the time, this is invisible. Anthropic’s own testing places the classifier’s true target as a narrow band of high-risk cybersecurity prompts, and the company acknowledges that many of the specific vulnerability-identification tasks the classifier is designed to catch are things older, less capable models like Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, and various open-source alternatives can already do without any special guardrails, so the safety gain is targeted rather than sweeping. But the tradeoff is real, and it lands hardest on the people most likely to be reading this.
The honest tradeoff, more false positives on routine coding
Anthropic has been unusually direct about the cost of the new classifier, and it deserves to be equally direct here. A safety filter tuned aggressively enough to catch its target 99 percent of the time will also catch a certain rate of benign requests that only look similar, and the company has said openly that developers should expect more of these false positives on routine coding and debugging.
In practical terms, that means anyone using Fable 5 for real engineering work is more likely than before to see a routine prompt get flagged, watch the model back off, and get their answer from Opus 4.8 instead. Sometimes that’ll be fine, Opus 4.8 is a genuinely capable model. Sometimes it’ll feel like a step down from the answer Fable 5 would have given, because Fable 5 was pitched as the more capable model in the first place. And sometimes the notification itself will interrupt a flow of work that would have been smoother without it.
Anthropic has said it’s planning refinements to reduce these false positives, and the classifier will presumably get better tuned over time. But at launch, the honest characterization of the returned Fable 5 is that it’s the same model wrapped in a tighter safety layer, and the visible experience of using it is a little more interruptive than the version people tried three weeks ago. This is worth naming plainly rather than dressed up as a feature.
There’s a slightly cutting way to put this that has been circulating in coverage, that for cybersecurity-adjacent tasks, the returned Fable 5 is functionally close to Opus 4.8 in a wrapper, because those are exactly the tasks that get rerouted. That framing overstates it for most work, since the reroute is targeted, not blanket, but it points at something real. If your workload leans heavily toward the kinds of prompts the classifier is tuned to catch, you’ll see more of Opus 4.8 in your responses than you did in the original launch.
The access economics are tighter than the original launch
The way you actually pay for Fable 5 has also shifted, and this one is easy to miss in the excitement of the return.
At the original launch on June 9, Fable 5 was included for Pro, Max, Team, and select Enterprise plans at full inclusion in weekly usage limits. The return is more conservative. From July 1 through July 7, Fable 5 is included at up to 50 percent of a user’s weekly tier allowance on those same plan tiers. After July 7, that included allowance goes away and Fable 5 shifts to usage-credit billing for those users. Standard Enterprise seats don’t get an included allowance at all, all Fable 5 usage there is billed through credits.

In plain terms, the free-included window is shorter and smaller than the one users had at the first launch, six days at half cap now, against thirteen days at full inclusion at launch, and it lands at a moment when many people who tried Fable 5 the first time already know they want to keep using it. The upshot for a typical Pro or Max user is that if you want steady access to Fable 5 beyond July 7, you should expect to pay per-token through the usage-credit path, not through your subscription.
If you’re on Team or one of the Enterprise tiers that has an included allowance, the same July 7 boundary applies, and it’s worth putting on the calendar. And if you’re on standard Enterprise, this is a usage-credit model from day one and always has been.
If Fable 5 doesn’t show up, try this
One practical note that has already come up for early users. Fable 5 is not the default model on any Claude plan, you have to actively select it. In Claude Code specifically, the command to switch to it is /model fable, and that is also the command to return to Fable 5 after a delegation has moved your session to Opus 4.8. The classifier reroute is sticky, once a request has been rerouted, the session stays on Opus until you switch back. That is easy to miss if you did not see the notice at the moment it happened.
A couple of related details worth knowing. Claude Code needs to be at version 2.1.170 or later to see Fable 5, so if the model is not in your picker, run claude update first. If your workflow uses subagents, you can set the model per subagent in the YAML frontmatter, so a Fable 5 orchestrator can farm work out to Sonnet or Haiku workers with model: sonnet or model: haiku in each subagent's file.
The honest caveat, /model fable will not conjure access that is not there. If Fable 5 is unavailable to you specifically, the likely causes are three, and only the first is fixed by a command. First, the model just was not selected in your session, that is the case /model fable solves. Second, you are already through your included weekly usage allowance for Fable 5, which through July 7 is capped at fifty percent of your tier's total, and you either need to wait for the reset or enable usage credits. Third, you are on a plan or cloud deployment that does not have Fable 5 enabled yet, standard Enterprise seats without credits, or an AWS, Google Cloud, or Foundry endpoint that is still being restored after the pause. For the last two, the command does not help, that is a billing or provisioning matter, not a selection one.
The industry moves happening underneath
Two things happening around the return, both worth knowing about even if they aren’t features you touch directly.
Anthropic is now proposing a shared framework, being drafted with Amazon, Microsoft, and Google, to grade how dangerous a specific jailbreak actually is, scoring each finding on capability gain, breadth of impact, ease of weaponization, and how discoverable the technique is. This is a first attempt at getting the frontier labs on a common scale rather than reacting case by case, and if it lands, it changes how future issues get triaged. The practical effect for developers is that the frantic pace of disruption around this specific event should be less likely to repeat in the same shape, because the labs will have a shared vocabulary for what counts as serious.
Alongside that, Anthropic opened a HackerOne program for researchers to report new Fable 5 bypass techniques directly and pledged external auditors earlier access to test future frontier models before they release publicly. This is a governance shift, not a product feature, but it’s the kind of shift that shapes how the next few model releases actually reach users.
