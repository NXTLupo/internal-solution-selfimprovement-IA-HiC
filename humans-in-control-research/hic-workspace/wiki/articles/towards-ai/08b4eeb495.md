In the span of nine months, three of the best-known names in LLM observability stopped being independent companies. ClickHouse bought Langfuse in January. Cisco folded Galileo into Splunk by the middle of the year. Dynatrace closed its $915 million purchase of Arize in October. If you picked a tracing or evaluation tool for your agent stack any time in the last two years, there’s a real chance it now reports to someone else’s product roadmap.

## Three acquisitions in nine months

Start with the deals themselves, because the pattern only becomes obvious once you line them up. ClickHouse announced it had acquired Langfuse on January 16, 2026, describing it as “the leading open-source platform for LLM observability, evaluations, and prompt management” [1]. Langfuse’s tracing, evaluation, and prompt-management stack already ran entirely on ClickHouse under the hood, so the acquisition mostly formalized a dependency that already existed. ClickHouse committed to keeping Langfuse’s core under its MIT license and running Langfuse Cloud as a standalone service, at least for now [1].

Cisco moved next, though its timeline is messier. Cisco’s Splunk division announced its intent to acquire Galileo, an AI agent evaluation and observability startup, on April 9, 2026, saying the deal was expected to close in the fourth quarter of Cisco’s fiscal year [2]. An update to that same post confirmed the acquisition had completed by May 22, 2026. Galileo didn’t survive as a brand: Splunk’s own acquisitions page now describes the product simply as “Splunk Agent Observability” [3].

Dynatrace closed the biggest of the three. Goodwin, the law firm that advised Dynatrace on the deal, announced on August 14, 2026, that the acquisition was “a cash and stock transaction valued at $915 million” [4]. Dynatrace’s own press release doesn’t repeat that figure, but it confirms the deal closed on October 1, 2026 [5]. Arize was not a struggling startup looking for an exit. It had raised a $70 million Series C less than two years earlier, in a round that included strategic money from Datadog and PagerDuty, two other observability vendors [6]. Getting bought anyway is the part worth sitting with.

## The strategic logic behind the buying spree

None of these acquirers sell the same thing. ClickHouse sells a real-time analytics database. Cisco, through Splunk, sells infrastructure and security monitoring. Dynatrace sells application performance monitoring. What they share is a product gap: none of them had a native way to answer whether an LLM’s output was actually good, as opposed to whether the service that produced it was up and responding.

Dynatrace was explicit about this in its own announcement, framing the deal around the gap between pre-launch evaluation and production monitoring: “evaluation (testing AI before launch) and observability (monitoring it in production) are often separate,” and combining them lets teams “catch issues earlier and fix them faster, from development through production” [5]. Splunk made a similar pitch for Galileo, describing it as a way to instrument “every stage of the ADLC,” from prompt optimization and model selection through evaluation, monitoring, and guardrails [2]. Read the two statements side by side and they’re nearly interchangeable. This is a shared industry thesis, not a coincidence.

If you’ve been reading this blog, this framing should sound familiar. I’ve written before about [why it sounds right isn’t good enough as an evaluation bar](https://medium.com/gitconnected/evaluating-ai-output-why-it-sounds-right-isnt-good-enough-f9edd21f7346), and the acquirers here are making the same argument with their wallets: evaluation and observability are converging into one buying decision, not two separate tool categories.

## What you actually risk when your vendor gets rolled up

The acquisition itself isn’t the risk. Being acquired by a platform vendor with deeper pockets can genuinely mean better infrastructure and a longer runway. The risk is what tends to follow a rebrand: roadmap drift toward the parent company’s existing customers, pricing tied to a platform-wide contract instead of a usage tier you can reason about, and feature development that slows while the acquirer spends a year or two on integration plumbing instead of new capabilities.

Galileo is the clearest example available right now, because its public identity is already gone. The product exists, customers presumably still use it, but it’s “Splunk Agent Observability” now, not Galileo [3]. If your evaluation pipeline, your dashboards, or your alerting rules were built around Galileo-specific terminology or API shapes, you inherited a migration project you didn’t ask for.

This is exactly the kind of failure mode I covered in [what to log before your agent fails in production](https://medium.com/gitconnected/what-to-log-before-your-agent-fails-in-production-aa594d6d134d): the logging and tracing layer is supposed to be the stable part of your stack, the thing you don’t have to rethink every quarter. When the vendor providing that layer changes ownership, that assumption breaks, even if nothing about the product’s day-to-day behavior changes immediately.

## OpenTelemetry’s GenAI conventions: insurance, not armor

The practical response to vendor consolidation isn’t to avoid observability tools. It’s to instrument against an open standard first and treat any specific vendor’s SDK as a thin, swappable layer on top. That’s the argument behind OpenTelemetry’s GenAI semantic conventions, a shared vocabulary of span and attribute names (`gen_ai.*`) for describing LLM calls, agent steps, and tool use, which multiple vendors, including the ones discussed above, already support.

There’s a catch worth stating plainly: as of a knowledge-base update dated September 15, 2026, every single `gen_ai.*` span, metric, and attribute in the OpenTelemetry registry sits at the "Development" stability level, the lowest of three tiers. The only attributes in that part of the spec that have reached "Stable" are generic ones inherited from core OpenTelemetry: `error.type`, `server.address`, and `server.port` [7]. Development status means no compatibility guarantees. Names can be renamed and span shapes restructured without a deprecation window. I wrote more about what these spans actually capture, and where their current limits are, in [tracing AI agents: what OTel's GenAI spans actually capture](https://medium.com/towards-artificial-intelligence/tracing-ai-agents-what-otels-genai-spans-actually-capture-560fafc96de2), and that instability is the main reason I called OTel insurance rather than armor. It reduces vendor lock-in; it does not eliminate churn.

_The standard exists. It just isn’t finished yet._

Practically, that means pinning your instrumentation library version and recording which schema version it targets, rather than assuming attribute names will hold steady across upgrades. A minimal example of emitting a `gen_ai.*` span manually, without relying on a vendor SDK to decide the attribute names for you, looks like this:

from opentelemetry import trace  
tracer = trace.get_tracer("my-agent")  
with tracer.start_as_current_span("gen_ai.chat") as span:  
 span.set_attribute("gen_ai.system", "anthropic")  
 span.set_attribute("gen_ai.request.model", "claude-opus-4-6")  
 span.set_attribute("gen_ai.usage.input_tokens", 182)  
 span.set_attribute("gen_ai.usage.output_tokens", 47)  
 # ... make the actual API call here

Any observability backend that speaks OpenTelemetry, acquired or not, can ingest spans built this way. That’s the whole point: the vendor becomes a place you send data, not a format you’re locked into.

## A checklist for choosing, or keeping, an observability tool

None of this means every tool in the space is about to get bought. LangChain, which owns the LangSmith observability and evaluation product, raised a $125 million Series B in October 2025 at a $1.25 billion valuation, led by IVP, and stayed independent through all three of the deals above [8]. Being well-funded and popular doesn’t guarantee independence either, as Arize’s own history shows, but it’s evidence that not every serious player is consolidating at once.
