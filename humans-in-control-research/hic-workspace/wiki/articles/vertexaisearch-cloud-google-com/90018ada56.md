3 : ISF Advisors, Beyond the Frontier: Decoding Viability in Smallholder Finance – Rural and Agricultural Finance State of the Sector Report.Key message #1: Relative to other sectors, identifying, 
quantifying, pricing and managing risk is one of the 
most challenging aspects of smallholder agrifinance.
Quantifying risk in agriculture is inherently difficult 
because of diversity, volatility and limited data 
availability in the sector. Agriculture spans a wide range 
of crops, geographies, climates and production systems, 
each with distinct risk profiles. It is also highly exposed 
to issues beyond farmers’ control, such as market 
fluctuations and global events that affect input costs and 
commodity prices. These risks are compounded by rural, 
data-poor environments in which high-quality and reliable 
information is scarce and costly to collect. As a result, 
risks tend to be interconnected, making it challenging for 
lenders to isolate and accurately assess individual risks 
and specific drivers of loss.
Climate change magnifies this complexity. Over the 
past three decades, climate-related hazards have caused 
an estimated US$3.8 trillion in agricultural losses. Today, 
more than half the world is affected by drought, and 
nearly 2 billion people face a rising risk of frequent 
and severe flooding. 1 As highlighted in ISF Advisor’s 
2025 Agricultural Finance State of the Sector (SOS) 
report,2 climate shocks can push the cost of lending to 
smallholder farmers to unsustainable levels, driving many 
business models built on engaging with smallholder 
farmers beyond the viability frontier. 
Key message #2: A more holistic approach to identifying 
and managing different types of risk is needed.
The diversity and complexity of risk in smallholder 
finance means no single risk management instrument 
can unlock finance at scale. There is an opportunity – 
and a need – to build a more nuanced understanding 
and more comprehensive view of risk that spans the 
entire agricultural value chain, from primary production 
to markets to currency and policy exposure. This requires 
quantifying risks more accurately and reassessing where 
different elements of the risk stacks should sit across 
actors and layers of the system.In certain circumstances, portfolio-level risk transfer 
approaches, such as guarantees or meso-level 
insurance, may be appropriate. More sustainably, 
however, a layered approach is required, including:  
● Identifying, pricing and managing different aspects of 
the risk stack
● Transferring parts of the risk stack to insurers or value 
chain actors (VCA)
● Linking to macroinsurance schemes
● Accessing de-risking services and other risk-sharing 
mechanisms 
● Using more climate-adaptive technologies and farming 
practices that reduce the underlying exposure.
In this broader framing, agricultural insurance functions 
as both a financial instrument and an industry capability 
that can catalyse a shift from single-actor, single-solution 
approaches toward more coordinated, multi-stakeholder 
risk management.  
At its core, agricultural insurance lowers actual risk by 
protecting balance sheets against the portfolio losses 
that typically follow climate shocks. Perhaps even more 
importantly, agricultural insurance can play a critical role 
in reducing uncertainty and lowering perceived risk. 
Recent research by ISF Advisors3 shows that perceived 
risk in the agricultural sector often exceeds actual risk, 
particularly among formal financial institutions and 
offtakers, now the largest lenders to smallholder farmers. 
These actors have strong incentives to lend to secure 
supply, but many operate with thin balance sheets that 
are often unable to absorb a single climate shock. 
Beyond agricultural insurance’s function as a financial 
product, the agricultural insurance industry itself can 
serve as a key enabler of smarter risk management 
across the agricultural finance ecosystem. The industry 
can effectively act as the sector’s risk intelligence 
infrastructure. Through the data, models and analytical 
capabilities developed for their work, agricultural 
insurance providers are able to generate highly granular 
risk insights across crops, geographies, production 
systems and business models. 
These capabilties help codify, quantify and price

risk from something perceived and unmanageable

Several factors explain the limited integration of 
agrifinance and agricultural insurance:
● Policy fragmentation. In most countries, agricultural 
insurance and agrifinance are governed by separate 
ministries or regulatory bodies, resulting in fragmented 
policy ownership and limited coordination.
● Limited public investment in agricultural insurance. 
Many governments prioritize investing in agrifinance through 
large public lending programmes, such as Bangladesh’s 
credit ecosystem, driven by microfinance institutions (MFIs), 
and Ethiopia’s Agricultural Transformation Institute public 
fertilizer programme. However, agricultural insurance rarely 
receives equivalent funding, technical resources, financial 
or political attention. 
● Low insurer capability. Agricultural insurance is rarely 
mandatory, unlike motor or health insurance, and remains 
underfunded, leaving insurers with limited agricultural 
expertise, weak data systems and little incentive to develop 
specialized teams.
● Weak business case for lenders. Financial institutions and 
VCAs often do not understand the benefits of integrating 
insurance or cannot absorb the additional premium costs 
without external support.
● Farmer-level barriers. Low financial literacy, limited trust 
and poor experience with past products may suppress 
demand. 
● Gaps in enabling regulations and market incentives.  
Few countries have frameworks that attract or retain private 
sector participation at scale. 
As a result, agrifinance and agricultural insurance markets 
continue to evolve in parallel rather than as complementary 
mechanisms addressing the same risk stack.
However, climate change has heightened the urgency 
of integration, as rising climate volatility increases 
lenders’ exposure and puts upward pressure on 
insurers’ pricing models. Insurance can protect lenders’ 
balance sheets and de-risk investment, but finance is 
equally critical to enable farmers and agri-SMEs to adopt 
climate-smart technologies and infrastructure, such as 
improved inputs, irrigation, storage or processing, that 
can reduce the underlying production risks insurers are 
pricing.
Without parallel investment in these underlying 
technologies, insurers are likely to retreat from serving 
the most exposed segments, further constraining access 
to finance and reinforcing a cycle of underinvestment 
and vulnerability.

102. The risk management benefits of agricultural insurance 
for lenders and value chain actors
Key message #1: Integrating agricultural insurance at 
the farmer or portfolio level delivers tangible benefits 
for lenders, particularly by reducing loan defaults and 
by lowering banks’ risk-weighted asset ratios, improving 
overall capital adequacy.
Agricultural insurance strengthens the financial 
stability and resilience of agricultural lending portfolios. 
Evidence shows that integrating insurance into agrifinance 
can reduce loan defaults, expand credit access and 
enhance the sustainability of lending institutions.5 The 
United States Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation 
(FDIC) found that an increase of one standard deviation 
in crop insurance payouts reduces past-due farm loans 
by around 60 basis points (bps) – which is equivalent to 
roughly 20 percent of typical delinquency fluctuations.6 
Agricultural insurance can also strengthen banks’ 
capital positions by reducing portfolio risk and 
potentially freeing up liquidity. Research shows that 
agricultural insurance is an effective and affordable way 
to address systemic risks inherent in agriculture.7 By 
lowering the effective risk weight of agricultural loans, 
agricultural insurance can reduce lenders’ risk-weighted 
asset ratios, even when facing covariant shocks – 
situations where many borrowers default simultaneously 
following a disaster.8
Key message #2: Agricultural insurance can provide 
lenders with evidence-based references for quantifying 
and pricing systemic risk, creating the potential for 
better lending terms and increased risk appetite. 
Financial institutions often price smallholder 
lending for a worst-case scenario, applying 
conservative assumptions that overlook the 
differences across farmer profiles, value chains and 
production systems.But, as highlighted in the 2025 Agricultural Finance SOS 
report,9 risk varies widely, even within the same region 
or value chain, depending on climate exposure and 
sensitivity, commercialization levels and the adoption of 
climate-smart practices.
 
Agricultural insurance data, including actuarial data 
sets and satellite-based risk data, can help lenders 
better understand and price agricultural risk. When 
combined with emerging digital traceability systems, 
these data offer a more granular view of how risk varies 
across geographies, commodity markets and farmer 
segments. This deeper insight can help to inform more 
accurate, data-driven decisions on lending eligibility, 
loan sizing and pricing.
In most cases, such evidence emerges from contexts 
where insurance is formally integrated into loan 
products or portfolio-level arrangements. However, 
as highlighted in section 1, a larger opportunity lies 
in enabling lenders to use agricultural insurance 
data and risk intelligence capabilities even when 
they have not formally integrated insurance into their 
portfolio. This can help lenders better understand 
and price the risk of lending to smallholder farmers.  
Despite the risk mitigation benefits of agricultural 
insurance, interviews across markets indicate these 
gains are rarely passed on to borrowers. Lenders 
often do not expand their risk appetite or reduce loan 
interest rates (pricing); in fact, to cover premium costs, 
pricing may increase when insurance is bundled with 
loans. Reasons may include delayed recognition of risk 
reduction, already low-risk products (e.g., MFI group 
lending) and a lack of robust evidence demonstrating 
the benefits of integrating insurance for lenders.

3. Improving policy environments to build connected 
agricultural insurance and agrifinance markets
Key message #1: Although agrifinance and 
agricultural insurance are natural complements, 
they are often developed and governed separately. 
Agricultural insurance and agrifinance share similar 
objectives, stakeholders and incentives. Both aim to 
strengthen resilience in agriculture, expand access to 
capital and reduce vulnerability to climate and market 
shocks. Both require coordinated engagement from 
governments, financial institutions, producers and VCAs.  
Despite their natural synergies, the two markets are 
often treated as separate policy domains, with distinct 
programmes and agendas. Many governments across 
East Africa, for example, have developed agrifinance 
frameworks, but only Rwanda, Uganda and (in draft 
form) Kenya have introduced dedicated frameworks 
for agricultural insurance. This separation leads to 
fragmented programmes, duplicated efforts and missed 
opportunities to build mutually reinforcing markets. 
Key message #2: Strong government leadership is 
essential to align agrifinance and agricultural insurance 
and build markets that work together .
To realize the benefits of linking these markets, 
governments need to:
1. Take a broad view of the market, while addressing 
agriculture’s unique risk profile. 
 
A clear government strategy should position agricultural 
insurance within the wider financial and insurance system, 
while responding to the sector-specific challenges of 
agricultural markets.10 India provides a useful example. 
Until 2019, banks were required to integrate agricultural 
insurance with farmer credit, enabling early scale and 
strengthening linkages between agricultural insurance 
and agrifinance. Even though a voluntary model was 
adopted in 2019, under which farmers are allowed to 
opt out of agricultural insurance, banks are still required 
to offer integrated products. Interviews suggest uptake 
remains strong even under this voluntary regime, 
indicating a shift in farmer behaviour.2. Establish a centralized coordination body with a clear 
mandate.
 
Governance fragmentation remains one of the biggest 
barriers to scale. To address it, high-level government 
body should be given a clear mandate to implement 
the agricultural insurance agenda, as well as authority 
to coordinate across the private sector, central banks, 
regulatory bodies, ministries and other key stakeholders.11 
Ethiopia’s Rural Finance Service Unit (RFSU), established in 
2025, provides a recent example. Tasked with expanding 
access to agrifinance and agricultural insurance, RFSU 
serves as a central coordination hub across government 
agencies, including the Ministry of Agriculture, the Ministry 
of Finance and the National Bank of Ethiopia, and partners 
with insurers, banks and development organizations.12  
3. Mobilize and align financial resources to ensure long-
term sustainability.
  
To ensure long-term sustainability, governments should 
draw on renewed public funding and donor support 
for agrifinance and address fiscal constraints through 
innovative financing. Public funding can be strategically 
leveraged to crowd in private investment, using 
instruments such as blended finance mechanisms or 
green bonds.13

164. Unlocking farming investment through linking

Key message #1: Agricultural insurance has been proven 
to protect farmers from climate shocks and increase 
their willingness to invest in their farms.
 
Agricultural insurance helps stabilize farmers’ incomes 
and livelihoods by providing a financial safety net against 
shocks like weather disruptions and crop losses, enabling 
farmers to recover more quickly when such events occur. 
When insurance payouts arrive after a shock, farmers 
avoid depleting their savings or selling productive assets 
like livestock, which helps them to maintain herd sizes 
and sustain their operations during difficult periods.17
When protected by insurance, farmers are more likely to 
invest in climate adaptation and on-farm infrastructure, 
driving productivity and resilience. By reducing risk exposure, 
insurance encourages farmers to invest more confidently 
in climate adaptation investments, as well as in productive 
inputs such as improved seeds and fertilizers. Studies show 
that insured smallholder farmers increase investments 
and technology use by 25–35 percent, resulting in higher 
productivity and incomes (Farrin & Miranda, 2013; Karlan 
et al., 2014; Carter et al., 2016).18 In Kenya, for example, 
livestock insurance changed production strategies by 
inducing an 83 percent reduction in the share of small 
animals (mainly goats) herded relative to large animal and 
enabled the share of household members who completed 
age appropriate education.19
Agricultural insurance drives demand for agrifinance, 
creating a virtuous cycle of investment, resilience and 
growth. When farmers are protected against shocks, 
they gain the confidence to invest more in their farms 
and in on-farm infrastructure. This increased willingness 
to invest boosts demand for financing, which in turn 
strengthens the case for broader insurance coverage. 
As access to both finance and insurance grows, the two 
reinforce each other, driving sustained improvements in 
productivity, resilience and rural livelihoods.

the bankability of smallholder farmers. Microinsured

Key message #3: Agricultural insurance at scale 
can fundamentally strengthen the broader enabling 
conditions for finance through systemic and 
behavioural change. 
Beyond its direct role in improving the performance and 
viability of smallholder finance, agricultural insurance also 
strengthens the broader enabling environment. Whether 
through formal coverage at the farmer or portfolio level, 
or through the use of data-driven risk assessment tools, 
agricultural insurance can enhance how agricultural risks 
are identified, priced and managed across the system.  
By protecting farmers, offtakers and processors from 
climate and market shocks, insurance helps stabilize 
cash flows and sustain liquidity across value chains. 
This, in turn, builds and preserves lenders’ confidence in 
agricultural markets.
Insurance also encourages greater investment along the 
value chain, not only on-farm, but also in aggregation, 
storage or processing infrastructure. When farmers and 
agri-SMEs know they are protected against major losses, 
they are more willing to invest (whether through using their 
own capital or taking on credit) in resilience-enhancing 
improvements such as irrigation, improved inputs or post-
harvest storage. These investments reduce underlying 
production risks, strengthen value chain reliability and 
create higher-value borrowers and commodities. As a 
result, the underlying market becomes more attractive 
for lending and farmers become more “financeable”. 
Insurance, therefore, helps to unlock a virtuous cycle, 
in which insurance enables agricultural investments, 
investment reduces risk and cost-to-serve, larger and 
more productive farms increase revenue potential and 
more finance is able to flow. Over time, as the insurance industry builds deeper 
data sets and stronger analytical capacity around value 
chains and markets, it will be able to differentiate and 
price risk more accurately. This creates opportunities 
to distribute risks across multiple actors rather than 
concentrating them solely at the farmer or lender level. 
For example, climate and yield risks may remain with 
insurers, while trade, market or currency risks could be 
covered by other financial instruments. As investment 
flows into primary production and resilience-building 
technologies, supported by both finance and insurance, 
the underlying risk in the system should gradually decline. 
In this reinforcing cycle, better risk data enable smarter 
pricing of risk (premiums); smarter pricing attracts 
more finance; increased investment strengthens the 
resilience of farmers and agri-SMEs; and, ultimately, the 
true cost of premiums falls across the system.
Key message #4: Better integration between agri-
lending and agricultural insurance at the farmer and 
agri-SME level can help overcome the adoption barriers 
that prevent agricultural insurance from reaching scale. 
Integrating lending and insurance can increase 
awareness and accessibility among smallholder 
farmers. Stakeholders note that smallholder farmers often 
lack awareness of the benefits and practical applications 
of agricultural insurance, leading to limited uptake. When 
insurance is embedded within financing products, farmers 
gain a clearer understanding of its value and can link 
insurance directly to the tangible outcome of securing credit.  
The high cost of premiums, one of the most persistent 
barriers, can also be mitigated through integration. 
Financial institutions and value chain actors are 
well placed to assess and aggregate the demand 
for agricultural insurance. By acting as a demand 
aggregator and distribution channel for agricultural 
insurance, these actors can reduce the operational 
costs of delivering insurance and provide the 
scale needed to deploy insurance more affordably.  
 
Integrating insurance with loans also allows 
premiums to be incorporated into interest 
payments and shared among the stakeholders who 
benefit most from reduced portfolio risk: farmers, 
lenders, offtakers and, in some cases, government 
programmes. This approach makes coverage more 
affordable while aligning incentives across the value 
chain.

them to identify, quantify and price agricultural risks with 
far greater accuracy. Financial institutions, investors and 
offtakers can then use these insights to design stronger 
risk-sharing mechanisms and lending products. Thus, 
insurers’ capabilities form the core market foundations 
needed for deeper integration between agricultural 
insurance and agrifinance.
As highlighted in section 1, the data sets and analytical tools developed by insurers and reinsurers can 
meaningfully inform credit risk assessment and portfolio 
management across the financial sector. When shared 
responsibly, these data-driven insights can help financiers 
differentiate between systemic and idiosyncratic risks, 
set more appropriate pricing and allocate capital more 
efficiently. In this way, insurers operate not only as 
risk-takers, but as risk intelligence partners within the 
agrifinance ecosystem.
Over the longer term, insurers and reinsurers can also 
act as catalytic capital providers, deploying portions of 
their reserves or investment portfolios into agri-lending 
facilities, blended finance structures or guarantee 
schemes that expand financing for smallholder farmers 
and agri-SMEs.

20Key message #2: Insurance and reinsurance companies 
are key underwriters of innovation, co-creating new 
products and models that address agriculture-specific 
risks and contexts.
 
Through partnerships with banks, MFIs, AgFinTechs and 
value chain lenders, insurers can co-develop integrated 
insurance-finance products that protect both borrowers 
and lenders. Reinsurers, in particular, can deepen this 
impact by absorbing systemic or catastrophic risks that 
individual insurers or local lenders cannot carry alone, 
allowing more credit to flow to higher-risk segments.
Past examples of innovation include the development of 
parametric insurance; integrating insurance with credit and 
other market services such as input provision and extension; 
mutual insurance models that pool groups of farmers under 
one single policy (i.e., risk pooling); and bundling different 
types of insurance (e.g., crop and life insurance) to provide 
more comprehensive protection.
A leading example is One Acre Fund’s partnership with 
RiskShield, which provides farmers in Rwanda with an 
integrated set of products, including inputs, credit, crop 
insurance and a credit life insurance benefit that waives 
clients’ outstanding debts in the event of death, in addition 
to providing farmer training and market facilitation.21
Key message #3: Regulatory frameworks and market 
incentives that encourage insurer and reinsurer 
participation are critical to de-risking agricultural investment.  
Despite the potential, most insurers and reinsurers 
in emerging markets have limited engagement 
in agriculture. A core reason is that agricultural 
insurance is rarely mandatory, unlike motor or health 
insurance, giving companies little incentive to invest 
in specialized agricultural expertise or strong data 
systems. In voluntary and low-margin contexts, 
insurers naturally prioritize higher-return product 
lines, leaving the agricultural sector underdeveloped. 
Stronger regulatory incentives and enabling policies 
can help shift this dynamic. Measures that can encourage 
insurers to expand their agricultural capabilities include 
requiring mandatory coverage for certain loan products, 
offering premium subsidies linked to lending programmes 
and establishing public reinsurance mechanisms.Likewise, clearer risk-sharing frameworks and improved 
coordination between insurance and financial regulators 
– for example, recognizing insured agricultural portfolios 
as lower-risk assets for capital adequacy calculations 
– can support greater collaboration between insurers, 
banks and other agrifinance actors. 
Governments and development partners can also 
encourage participation by creating joint innovation 
platforms in which insurers, financiers and technology 
providers can co-develop new solutions. These platforms 
can support innovations such as meso-level products for 
value chain actors or index-based portfolio products for 
financial institutions.
A practical example comes from the Philippines, where 
the central bank (Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, BSP), allows 
banks to treat insured agri-portfolios as lower-risk assets 
for capital adequacy ratio purposes, provided the product 
is approved by the country’s Insurance Commission. 
This type of alignment demonstrates how regulation 
can unlock stronger linkages between agrifinance and 
agricultural insurance.

226. The role of technology and innovation in 
enabling alignment
Key message #1: Technology is lowering the transaction 
costs that historically kept insurance and finance 
markets separate, enabling bundled and data-driven 
delivery models.
Digital platforms are transforming how farmers, agri-SMEs 
and value chain actors access both credit and insurance. 
By integrating different services such as digital credit 
scoring, e-wallets and distribution networks, technology 
is making it feasible to deliver insurance alongside 
lending at scale.
Advances in hyperlocal weather data, remote sensing and 
mobile payments have dramatically reduced the cost of 
underwriting, premium collection and claims processing. 
These tools make bundled products more viable for 
lenders and insurers alike, allowing, for instance, instant 
premium deductions from loan repayments or automated 
payouts directly to farmers’ mobile wallets after verified 
weather events.
Above all, Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI), or national-
level infrastructure for digital identity, digital payments 
and banking, and data exchange, is reshaping how 
new technologies can be deployed and scaled, while 
protecting data privacy. This is especially true in agriculture, 
where the World Bank estimates that by 2050, a single 
farm could generate 4.1 million data points per day.22 
 
AgTechs and digital platforms are emerging to harness 
this data, but they depend on strong DPI to enable 
seamless, secure and cost-efficient data-sharing across 
institutions. Robust DPI also promotes financial inclusion 
by bringing more farmers into the formal financial system, 
where “integrated” financial-insurance products can be 
accessed. In India, for example, the Ministry of Electronics 
and Information Technology estimates that DPI alone 
could bring 40–60 percent of the farming population into 
formal credit channels.23
Key message #2: Technology is building a shared risk 
intelligence infrastructure that enables both insurers 
and financiers to quantify and price agricultural risk 
more accurately.Innovations in data analytics, satellite monitoring and 
artificial intelligence-driven modelling are helping convert 
raw climate and yield data into actionable insights for 
insurers, and increasingly for lenders too. 
For insurers, these tools support more accurate product 
design and faster, more reliable loss estimation. For 
lenders, these data sets and systems are making 
agricultural risk measurable, reducing the information 
asymmetry that has traditionally led financial institutions 
to price smallholder lending for worst-case scenarios. 
With better data, lenders can conduct dynamic risk 
pricing and portfolio stress testing, and can allocate 
capital in ways that reflect actual rather than perceived 
risk. 
Over time, these shared data sets and interoperable 
systems will allow insurers, financiers and investors 
to co-create blended instruments that distribute 
risk more efficiently across the system, rather 
than concentrating it at the farmer or lender level. 
As mentioned above, governments and donors can play 
a catalytic role by supporting open data platforms and 
DPI that allow public repositories to become accessible, 
as well as by enabling responsible data-sharing between 
insurance providers, lenders and regulators, while 
ensuring farmer privacy is protected. This is especially 
critical in emerging markets, where the lack of reliable, 
interoperable data has historically limited both agricultural 
lending and insurance uptake.
An example of an innovative data platform is Cropin, 
created for the agriculture industry, which collates 
weather updates, soil health data, market prices, 
expert advisories and artificial intelligence-driven 
analytics to support data-driven decisions for farmers, 
governments, banks and insurers. To date, Cropin 
has digitized over 30 million acres of farmland across 
more than 100 countries, and has collected data on 
thousands of crop varieties.24

7. Recommendations
Recommendation #1: Reframe conversations 
with governments around building markets 
together, in mutually reinforcing ways, using 
a more sophisticated “industry model”.  
Governments increasingly recognize the synergies 
and interdependencies between agricultural insurance 
and agrifinance, but their understanding of how these 
markets interact remains relatively simplistic, often 
focusing narrowly on insurance as a distribution channel. 
A more sophisticated “industry model” is needed, 
one that reflects the complex dynamics between 
capital providers, financial institutions, insurance and 
reinsurance companies and intermediaries/aggregators. 
Using this lens, policymakers can design regulations and 
incentives that encourage collaboration and innovation 
across the two markets, enabling data and capabilities 
to be shared, as well as supporting the creation of new 
products that mitigate risk, reduce costs and encourage 
investment, especially in climate resilience.
Recommendation #2: Place value chain actors at 
the centre of the agricultural insurance agenda. 
As shown throughout this report, VCAs provide over 40 
percent of total smallholder finance, yet they are rarely 
prioritized within the agricultural insurance agenda.
 
Placing VCAs at the centre of the agricultural insurance 
agenda allows governments to reach this large segment 
of the agrifinance market, which currently sits in a kind of 
regulatory no man’s land. A focus on VCAs can also:  
● Unlock more lending through VCAs by reducing their 
exposure to farm- and portfolio-level shocks, which their 
balance sheets alone cannot absorb 
● Strengthen entire value chains by reducing their 
exposure to supply shocks, which would improve the 
resilience of VCAs, their farmer networks and other 
downstream agribusinesses.
To do this effectively, governments and stakeholders 
must actively engage VCAs and their industry groups 
so that their perspectives and needs shape the national 
agricultural insurance agenda.Recommendation #3: Build stronger evidence to drive 
convergence between agrifinance and agricultural 
insurance. 
More rigorous evidence is needed to demonstrate how 
better integrated agricultural insurance and agrifinance 
markets benefit the agriculture sector at the macro, meso 
and micro levels. Priority gaps include:
● Showing the importance of farmer awareness and 
understanding of agricultural insurance (micro)
● Deepening evidence on the impact of agricultural 
insurance on agricultural investment, especially in relation 
to climate resilience (micro and meso)
● Demonstrating how agricultural insurance can benefit 
value chain actors and strengthen the resilience of value 
chains (meso)
● Building evidence on how agricultural insurance 
supports food security (macro)
●  Generating clearer guidance on how premium subsi-
dies can be used most efficiently (macro).
