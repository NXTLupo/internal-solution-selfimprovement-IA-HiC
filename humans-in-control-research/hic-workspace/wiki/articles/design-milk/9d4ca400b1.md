# Kevin Klein on Korean Dancheong, Ferraris, Lake Maracaibo + More

Source: [design_milk](https://design-milk.com/kevin-klein-designer-friday-five/)

After more than a decade working in hospitality development and collaborating with industry icons like Kelly Wearstler and Philippe Starck, Kevin Klein had a realization: “I was being paid to provide design insight to the same people that I was actively looking to for their own perspectives,” he says. That pivot to interior design led him to found Kevin Klein Design in 2020.
The California native’s first client at his eponymous firm was a childhood friend who wanted his home designed from top to bottom. A project of that scope was an invaluable opportunity that still influences his approach today.
Klein creates spaces he hopes will inspire—but his approach is anything but style-centric. With an architecture degree from the University of Southern California, he approaches design holistically, emphasizing construction, operations, and long-term functionality. Whether working in hospitality, residential, or multifamily design, he gives each project a distinct identity.
Klein says his favorite part of the process is when a job is complete and he sees the before-and-after photos. That’s when he sees the tangible, lasting impact of a design—a moment he says is particularly gratifying for him and his team.
Today, Kevin Klein joins us for Friday Five! Below, he shares five items that inspire him and five projects that reflect his work.
Kevin Klein’s 5 Inspirations
1. Gladding, McBean Ceramics Company
I first worked with Gladding, McBean during the restoration of the NoMad Hotel in Los Angeles, where historic terra-cotta elements were key to bringing the original 1920s Bank of Italy building back to life. What fascinated me was discovering just how rare this craft has become: Gladding, McBean is one of only two companies in the country still capable of producing architectural terra cotta at this level. They meticulously replicate deteriorated or missing historic pieces by combining scanning and fabrication technology with traditional handcraft.
I toured their 150-year-old facility in Northern California and was completely blown away by the process: sculpting, molding, glazing, firing, color matching, and essentially reverse-engineering architectural elements that may have been made a century ago. It completely changed the way I look at restoration—not as recreating the past, but as keeping an extraordinary material language alive.
2. Park Hotel Vitznau
I’m fascinated by hotels that feel inseparable from their setting, and Park Hotel Vitznau in Switzerland is one of the best examples. The original hotel opened in 1903 on the edge of Lake Lucerne. I love the tension between this almost fantastical, old-world Swiss architecture and the extraordinary clarity of the surrounding landscape: the lake, the mountains, and the intensity of the light and water.
Inside, the hotel combines contemporary technology and amenities with a sense of honest nostalgia. It’s a great example of how history and modern luxury don’t need to compete. The architecture provides the romance and permanence, while the contemporary interventions provide comfort and relevance. Ultimately, though, the most powerful design element is something no designer put there: the setting. Everything feels secondary to Lake Lucerne, which is exactly what makes the hotel so memorable.
3. Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela
I traveled to Lake Maracaibo in 2012, and I still think about the visuals of that trip today. It’s a strange, incredibly visceral place: the enormous body of water, the density of the surrounding rainforest, the humidity, and an almost constant electrical energy in the sky. The region is famous for its lightning, and seeing that amount of light and energy against such a dark, tropical landscape felt almost artificial. Something about it felt cinematic, and it has stayed in my head for more than a decade. I think designers are constantly building a subconscious library of places, colors, light, textures, and experiences, and Lake Maracaibo found a permanent place in mine.
4. Ferrari 575M Maranello
The Ferrari 575M Maranello sits at an intersection of tradition and modernity that I find incredibly appealing. The V12 has all of the sound, tactility, proportion, and theater you want from an old Italian grand tourer. The long hood, physical controls, and relatively restrained technology give it a vintage allure without actually being a vintage car. I also love that its beauty feels like a strange version of form following function. Nothing about it is gratuitous, yet the result is incredibly stylized. To me, it’s almost the perfect middle ground between romance and performance. Hopefully, one day soon I’ll be able to find one for myself.
5. Korean Dancheong
I’m fascinated by Dancheong—the traditional Korean system of decorative painting—because of how much ornamentation, color, and playfulness can exist within such a rigid underlying system. At first glance, it can feel almost exuberant, with reds, greens, blues, intricate florals, and geometric patterns layered across an architectural surface. But the more you look at it, the more you realize how structured everything actually is. Rules, repetition, hierarchy, and rhythm run underneath all that decoration. I’m generally drawn to design that can hold opposing ideas at the same time, and Dancheong does that beautifully. It’s disciplined without being serious, decorative without feeling arbitrary, and colorful without losing architectural clarity.
5 Projects by Kevin Klein
1. SYRN Brand Studio
I loved the challenge of designing a space for SYRN, Sydney Sweeney’s lingerie brand. It had to function as a workplace, a creative laboratory, and a physical expression of a brand still defining itself. Rather than defaulting to the expected language of a fashion showroom, we leaned into something more intimate and residential. Warm woods, vintage pieces, sculptural forms, and moments of unexpected color create a space that feels collected rather than prescribed. For me, the most interesting commercial spaces are the ones that don’t immediately read as commercial; they create a world you want to spend time in, and the brand’s identity emerges almost subconsciously through the experience of being there.
2. Walkers’ Bar and Market
I’ve always been drawn to spaces that can shift throughout the day, and Walkers’ Bar and Market in Dallas gave us the chance to design something that feels like a neighborhood market in the afternoon and an intimate wine bar at night. We focused on a kind of quiet luxury—beautiful Italian woods and marble, vintage European lighting, restored furniture, and custom pieces—but deliberately kept everything approachable and a little playful. Ultimately, I wanted the space to feel less like a “designed” restaurant and more like somewhere that had evolved over time: personal, layered, slightly imperfect, and deeply connected to the personalities of the siblings behind it.
3. The Hollywood Roosevelt Gable & Lombard Penthouse and Johnny Grant Apartment
Working within a place as storied as The Hollywood Roosevelt, the goal was never to recreate some idealized version of Old Hollywood, but to make the spaces feel as though they had simply evolved over the past 100 years. We sourced furniture and objects from more than 25 countries and layered them with Italian marble, dark walnut, oak, and warm plaster, allowing age and imperfection to become part of the design rather than something to disguise. During construction, we even uncovered a partially finished hand-painted ceiling mural hidden behind drywall; instead of restoring or completing it, we left it exactly as we found it. That discovery really encapsulated the project for me. Sometimes the most compelling thing a designer can do is recognize when something already has more character than anything you could add.
4. West Hollywood Home
This project started with a pretty simple idea: create a room designed almost entirely around hanging out. What emerged was this immersive, slightly hedonistic entertainment space centered on an oversized sunken “cuddle pit,” where the distinction between furniture and architecture starts to disappear. I loved the opportunity to be a little less serious here by layering pattern, texture, vintage pieces, art, and unexpected objects into something eclectic and deliberately difficult to categorize. It’s a good reminder for me that great residential design doesn’t always need to be precious or overly composed; sometimes the rooms people remember most are simply the ones that give them permission to have fun.
5. Chit Chat Coffee & Matcha
With Chit Chat, I wanted to take the familiarity and casualness of a neighborhood coffee shop and give it a more considered architectural identity. The design pulls from mid-century detailing and materiality, but we were careful not to let that reference become nostalgic or overly precious—the space still needed to feel comfortable, social, and genuinely useful. The proportions and detailing lend the space an elegance, balanced by warmth and an inherent informality. I love projects like this because they demonstrate that thoughtful design doesn’t have to announce itself; sometimes its greatest success is simply making an everyday ritual feel a little more special.
