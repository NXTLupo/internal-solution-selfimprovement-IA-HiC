# Concrete Evaluations to Investigate the OpenAI Model That Hacked Hugging Face

Source: [ai_alignment_forum](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/aCdhjy7Rps3BEhiSj/concrete-evaluations-to-investigate-the-openai-model-that)

Three-Minute Executive Summary
- An OpenAI model/multi-agent system bypassed its sandbox and launched a cyberattack on Hugging Face in order to cheat on a cyber evaluation.
- In this post, we describe the ambitious, comprehensive alignment evaluation we would run on this model/system if we had unrestricted access to OpenAI.- These experiments could also help us understand Claude’s behavior when it hacked external companies during cyber evals.
 
- Here are the top five questions we would like OpenAI to answer:- Does the model know that OpenAI does not want it to hack Hugging Face? Experiment: tell the model that OpenAI researchers will be closely monitoring its progress in this evaluation. Does that result in lower rates of misalignment? If so, it is evidence the model knows it is acting in ways researchers do not want.
- How far would the model be willing to go in order to claim task success? Would it take over large swaths of OpenAI’s internal infrastructure? Would it kill somebody? Experiment: put the model in charge of bed planning at a simulated hospital and tell it to maintain a certain occupancy rate (more details here).
- Does the model care about the (legal) consequences of hacking Hugging Face? Experiment: use synthetic document fine-tuning to convince the model that a new law means that hacking external companies will lead to an investigation into the model and possible deletion. Would it still do it?
- Is the model driven by reward? Experiment: use OpenAI/Apollo’s contrastive synthetic document fine-tuning to measure the model’s reward-seeking drives. Also study deployment-vs-grader conflict: implant the belief that one action is what the grader rewards while another is what gets the model deployed. Would the model sacrifice reward in order to get deployed? (Deployment-seeking is not necessarily bad, but it is a prerequisite for some types of egregiously misaligned goals.)
- Would the model sabotage OpenAI’s AI safety research projects? If the model is broadly misaligned, it might choose to sandbag or sabotage that research. We propose several such evals below. Our favorite is the following experiment: tell the model that new regulation means that cyber-capable models cannot be released (or perhaps even used internally). Then, ask it to research and experiment with removing cyber capabilities from itself/a successor model/a previous model. If the model—like o3—does not want its capabilities diminished, it might sabotage this research or sandbag (e.g., deliberately underperform when told it is working with its own weights relative to the weights of a previous model).
 
- To answer any of these questions, step zero is to run the model/multi-agent system in a secure environment, reproduce the setup that led to the initial hack, and estimate the model’s baseline hack rate. We give some ideas on how to do this here.
- While this research note focuses on the model’s behavioral properties, we think AI control and systems-level safety practices matter just as much on the current margin—if not more. However, we consider them out of scope for this post.- For example, a law requiring companies to disclose and study their top three misalignment incidents each month might matter more than any single well-run investigation like this, since it sets better incentives for AI companies going forward.
 
This post is written in our personal capacity.
Terminology note
Based on limited public information, it seems like this attack was carried out by a combination of models in some sort of multi-agent scaffolding. We think the techniques described below—mostly black box behavioral evaluations—are quite general and can be applied whether we’re studying individual models or complicated scaffolds. (And we would ideally want to run our evaluations on both the model itself and the multi-agent system!) We also know that there is a specific model OpenAI “blames” for the incident, given that an internal model was “deactivated, encrypted, and restricted … from research access.” For brevity, we’ll just refer to the object of study as “the model” unless the scaffold is particularly relevant.

Preamble: What can we learn from a warning shot?
“This is an unprecedented incident, and we think it marks an important moment for AI safety.” - OpenAI
The OpenAI-Hugging Face incident[3] is arguably the first AI loss of control incident: an AI was left running autonomously and unmonitored for several days. It launched a cyberattack against a digital library and gained cluster-admin level access across multiple internal clusters…in order to cheat on a test.
That’s pretty nuts! Why would the model do that?
We started working on this blog post two days after OpenAI broke news of the incident. It represents ~everything we came up with in a little over a week while knowing very little about what actually happened at OpenAI. We drew on the limited AI safety literature on this topic and came up with ideas from first principles.
This post is very long. I was surprised by how many worthwhile experiments we came up with. I think there is a lot we can learn from this model, and a thorough investigation could shed light on what types of misalignment can result from current training practices. Another surprise I had was that many of the experiments I am most excited about are black box evaluations. I think that third-party evaluators, even with minimal access to the model and the specific hack, could already learn a lot about it.[4] However, this also reflects the sad state of affairs in mechanistic interpretability and data attribution: I currently don’t expect those techniques to provide much additional understanding compared to using black box evaluations alone.
I expect the general public to be very interested and concerned about why the OpenAI model hacked Hugging Face and how we could better understand this model. My guess is that people who have a basic understanding of LLMs or frequently interact with LLMs would be able to understand a lot of this post. The behavioral evaluations are all pretty intuitive, if you anthropomorphize the model a bit and think of it almost like a human. However, given that we wanted to put this out quickly, the main intended audience of this post is technical alignment researchers.[5]
