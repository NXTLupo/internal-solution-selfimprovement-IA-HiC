A MESSAGE FROM 
THE CHANCELLOR
Dear CSU Community,
In February 2025, the California State University took bold action to shape the future of higher education innovation 
with the launch of our AI-Empowered University initiative. We acted with urgency, to address inequities emerging across the CSU’s 22-university system regarding uneven access to AI-enabled tools. Instead of continuing with a fragmented approach, we leveraged the power of the CSU system to ensure equitable access to state-of-the-art AI tools and trainings for every student, faculty and staff member. And as the nation’s largest and most diverse public university system, we have an obligation to ensure that this extraordinary technology expands opportunity, supports faculty and staff excellence, advances social mobility for the students we serve, strengthens the California workforce, and is implemented in a manner that reflects the CSU’s core values.
As a critical component of this work and to help ensure its sustained success, in fall 2025, we conducted the most 
expansive survey to date on the use of artificial intelligence in higher education – with more than 94,000 respondents, representing students, faculty and staff across our 22 universities. By listening closely to the experiences and perspectives of our diverse community, we can better understand how AI is being used across the CSU, where support is needed, and how we can move forward together with care and intention.
The findings presented here will help inform ongoing planning and decision-making, including efforts to support 
our faculty and staff in their teaching and professional development, strengthen institutional capacity, and prepare graduates with the knowledge and skills required to thrive in an evolving economy. As you read the report, I invite you to view it not simply as a measure of current attitudes, but as a call to action. The CSU has an opportunity to lead higher education by shaping how AI can be incorporated thoughtfully, equitably and responsibly. And we will answer that call.
I extend my appreciation to all those who participated in this effort and offer my sincere gratitude to the team at San 
Diego State University for designing and conducting this survey on behalf of the CSU system. This important work will ensure that the CSU’s path forward is guided by broad engagement, careful reflection, and a shared commitment to innovation, scholarship and opportunity.
Warmly,
Mildred García, Ed.D.

More than half of faculty and two-thirds of staff access it through  college-provided accounts.2

About 82% of staff respondents, 78% of faculty 
respondents and 69% of student respondents believe AI will become an essential part of most professions. When asked about their own careers, the numbers remain strong: 72% of staff, 69% of faculty and 58% of students agree. Strong Majorities Believe AI Enhances 
Creativity, Even as They Worry About Its  Long-Term Impact
About 78% of staff respondents, 66% of faculty 
respondents and 60% of student respondents agree that AI technology can enhance creativity and innovation, a notably high finding given the common concern that AI produces generic content. At the same time, about eight in 10 faculty, eight in 10 students and eight in 10 staff worry that AI will negatively affect human creativity over time. The CSU community holds both views simultaneously: AI is a creative tool today and a potential creative challenge tomorrow.9
Nearly Everyone Agrees:  
AI Output Must Be Verified
Ninety-seven percent of faculty respondents, 94% of 
staff respondents and 88% of student respondents say it is necessary to verify the accuracy of AI-generated content. This near-universal agreement holds across job types and demographic groups. The CSU community uses AI actively and refuses to accept its  outputs uncritically.
The Demand for AI Training Is Real, and the 
Students Who Want It Most Are Those the CSU Is Built to Serve
More than eight in 10 staff respondents and roughly 
seven in 10 faculty respondents want formal AI training. Among student respondents, about half express the same interest, but first-generation students lead at 53%, compared with 45% of non-first-generation students. Black and Hispanic and Latino students also express strong interest. 10

More than 94,000 students, faculty and staff from 22 CSU universities* and the Chancellor’s Office 
participated in the survey, making it the most comprehensive study of AI perceptions and usage in higher education. The sample includes 80,626 students, 6,085 faculty and 7,349 staff.
FACULTY
6,085 faculty from across all 22 universities and the Chancellor’s Office completed the survey. Respondents 
represented a wide range of disciplines, academic ranks and appointment types, including tenured, tenure-track and contingent faculty.
STAFF
7,349 staff from across all 22 universities and the Chancellor’s Office completed the survey, representing a wide range of administrative, operational and professional roles.STUDENTS
Undergraduates made up 85% of the student sample, with graduate students accounting for the remaining 15%. The largest share of respondents were in their first year of study, followed by third-year, fourth-year, second-year and fifth-year or beyond.
Women represented 61% of student respondents, 
men 36% and those identifying as another gender 3%. The median age was 21, with about three-quarters of respondents between the ages of 18 and 24.
The student sample reflects the CSU’s role as the 
nation’s most diverse four-year public university system. Hispanic and Latino students made up 48% of respondents, followed by white students (24%), Asian students (20%) and Black students (7%). All racial and ethnic characteristics are self-reported. More than half of student respondents identified as  first-generation college students. The self-reported  first-generation rate among survey respondents (55%) is higher than the CSU’s institutionally reported figure. This may reflect differences in how respondents interpreted the term or in which students chose to participate. The vast majority were California residents; most lived off campus and nearly nine in 10 were enrolled full time.
Respondents represent a wide range of academic 
disciplines. The most common majors included psychology, computer science, biology, business administration and nursing, with strong representation from colleges of social and behavioral sciences, engineering, health and human services, education and business. This breadth ensures that the findings reflect perspectives from across the academic spectrum, not solely from technology-focused fields.

THE STUDENT PERSPECTIVE
While many faculty express skepticism about AI’s 
role in academic work, students report varied levels of encouragement to use it. Thirty-nine percent of students say their professors encourage the use of AI, and 33% say their professors teach them how to use AI tools effectively. These findings are consistent with the faculty data showing that encouragement and requirement together account for about 25% of classroom policies.
About half of students (51%) believe that students who 
use AI for coursework have an academic advantage. At the same time, nearly two-thirds (65%) express skepticism about whether AI is benefiting education overall. These two findings are not in tension. Students can recognize that AI offers a practical edge while still questioning whether that edge is improving the quality of their learning experience.

The CSU community approaches AI with high expectations for accuracy. About 38% of students, 30% of faculty and 
46% of staff agree that they trust AI algorithms to provide accurate information. Faculty express the least trust of any group, with nearly half strongly disagreeing or disagreeing outright. Staff are the most willing to express trust, though still fewer than half do so.
This finding pairs naturally with one of the strongest results in the entire survey: Nearly all faculty (97%), more than 
nine in 10 staff and nearly nine in 10 students say it is necessary to verify the accuracy of AI-generated content. The commitment to verification holds across job types among faculty, with tenured and contingent faculty expressing nearly identical rates of agreement.
The combination of critical engagement and near-universal commitment to verification is one of 
the most defining features of the CSU community’s relationship with AI. This is a community that uses AI actively but refuses to accept its outputs uncritically.

CSU - Ahead of The CurvePRIVACY , REGULATION AND  
LONG-TERM IMPACT
Concerns about privacy, regulation and the long-term 
trajectory of AI are widespread and deeply held across the CSU community. More than eight in 10 respondents in every group worry about AI’s impact on personal privacy, with faculty expressing the strongest concern  at about 90%.
The strongest agreement in the entire survey centers 
on the risks of unregulated AI development. About 96% of faculty, 94% of staff and 90% of students agree that unregulated AI development may lead to unforeseen risks. These rates are among the highest in  the entire survey.
Concerns about AI’s long-term societal impact follow  
a similar pattern: About 89% of faculty, 86% of staff and 85% of students express concern. The CSU community is thinking not just about how AI affects them today, but about AI’s lasting impact on the world beyond campus.CREATIVITY AND THE ENVIRONMENT
Two additional concerns round out the picture. About eight in 10 faculty worry about AI negatively affecting human creativity, with about eight in 10 students and nearly eight in 10 staff expressing the same concern. This finding, explored in the previous section in the context of job security and professional identity, also carries implications for how the CSU thinks about AI’s role in the arts, humanities and creative disciplines.
Environmental concern is also notable. More than eight 
in 10 faculty express concern about the environmental impacts of AI, as do about eight in 10 students, while roughly three in four staff say the same. Faculty lead on this measure, consistent with a broader pattern in the data of faculty expressing more caution across  AI-related concerns.
The CSU community’s relationship with AI is defined not 
by resistance but by high expectations. Caution is high, but so is engagement. The demand for transparency, ethical use and responsible regulation is nearly universal. These findings reflect a community with high expectations for how AI should be governed.The CSU community has a clear expectation that AI systems should be more open about how they work. More than nine in 10 faculty, about 89% of staff and 85% of students agree that AI algorithms should be more transparent. These are among the highest agreement rates in the entire survey, and the consistency across groups underscores a shared value: The CSU community wants to understand the tools it is using.
The ethical use of AI registers as a major concern across the CSU, with faculty expressing the strongest sentiment. 
About 90% of faculty, 81% of staff and 76% of students say the ethical use of AI is a major concern for them. Faculty consistently rate ethical considerations higher than the other two groups.
When asked whether AI has the potential to reduce human biases, the community is less certain. About 43% of 
students, 39% of staff and 29% of faculty agree. Faculty are the most skeptical on this point, with about seven in 10 disagreeing. The CSU community supports human oversight of AI-generated outcomes.

Interest in AI training is one of the strongest and most 
consistent findings in the survey, but it is not uniform. Staff express the greatest enthusiasm, with 82% wanting formal AI training. Faculty follow at 72%. Among students, about half (49%) express the same interest, but a closer look at the data reveals important patterns.
First-generation college students want AI training 
at higher rates than their peers: 53% compared with 45% of non-first-generation students, an eight-point difference. Black students express the highest interest of any racial or ethnic group at 52%, followed by Hispanic and Latino students at 50%. White students report the lowest interest at 44%. Graduate students are considerably more interested than undergraduates, at 60% compared with 47%.
First-generation students are also more likely to say 
their curriculum lacks adequate exposure to AI (39% compared with 35%) and to report actively seeking AI opportunities (39% compared with 35%). 
The demand for AI training is real, and the students who 
want it most are those the CSU is built to serve.

ATTITUDES DIFFER MORE  
THAN BEHAVIOR
While AI adoption is nearly uniform across 
demographics, attitudes show more variation.  First-generation students are less likely to say verifying AI accuracy is necessary (86% compared with 91% for non-first-generation students). They are also the most eager for AI training (53% compared with 45% of  non-first-generation peers). Interest is strongest among the students the CSU is built to serve.
Academic level also shapes attitudes. Graduate 
students are considerably more interested in formal AI training than undergraduates, at 60% compared with 47%. Those closer to the workforce appear more motivated to build AI skills before they need them.FIELD OF STUDY SHAPES AI ATTITUDES 
MORE THAN BEHAVIOR
Regular AI use among students ranges from 45% in 
arts and humanities to 69% in business, a spread wider than any demographic gap in the survey. Attitudinal differences are sharper still. Business students express the highest trust in AI accuracy at 50%; arts and humanities students the lowest at 32%. Arts and humanities students lead on ethical concern at 81% and skepticism about AI in education at 71%. Business students lead on optimism. Arts and humanities students lead on caution.
About 63% of arts and humanities students say they 
would feel embarrassed if someone found out they used AI for schoolwork, compared with 45% of business students. Arts and humanities students also report the lowest interest in formal AI training at 42%, compared with 63% of business students. Field of study shapes not just how students use AI but how they feel  about using it.AI ADOPTION IS BROAD-BASED ACROSS DEMOGRAPHICS
Unlike many emerging technologies, AI adoption across the CSU does not track closely with demographic lines. ChatGPT usage is consistent across racial and ethnic groups: 86% of Asian students, 84% of white students, 83% of Hispanic and Latino students and 82% of Black students report using ChatGPT. The spread between the highest and lowest groups is just four percentage points.
Regular AI use is similarly even. About 55% of Asian students, 54% of Black students, 51% of Hispanic and Latino 
students and 50% of white students report regular AI use. First-generation and non-first-generation students report virtually identical rates at 54% and 53% respectively.
Students across backgrounds are finding and using these tools at comparable rates. The data make clear that the path 
forward is not about expanding access to AI tools; it is about providing the structured training, ethical guidance and curricular support to help all students use them effectively and responsibly.

THE ROAD AHEAD
In 2025, the California State University launched CSU Forward, a systemwide strategic plan built around 
a single conviction: that public higher education must not simply adapt to change but shape it. The plan establishes four performance objectives—economic mobility, sustainable affordability, becoming an employer of choice and expanding statewide impact—supported by six essential system capacities: academic program development, operational and resource management, change leadership, technology and infrastructure, external partnerships, and policy and regulatory capacity. Together they define how the CSU intends to serve students, strengthen its universities and deliver on its promise to California. This survey is one measure of how that work is taking shape.
PREPARING STUDENTS FOR CAREER-READY FUTURES
CSU Forward’s economic mobility objective, supported by academic program development and external partnership 
capacity, calls on the university to create flexible, career-integrated learning pathways that prepare students for  a rapidly changing workforce. The survey data connect directly to this agenda. Strong majorities across all three groups believe AI will become essential to most professions — and strong majorities also worry about what that means for their own careers. Meeting this moment means treating AI fluency not as a technology elective but as a core component of career preparation across every discipline and every campus.

CSU - Ahead of The CurveWHY THIS STUDY
Artificial intelligence is transforming how people 
learn, teach, work and create. For the California State University, which serves more than 470,000 students and is the most diverse four-year public university system in the nation, understanding this transformation is essential. Researchers at San Diego State University, supported by the CSU Chancellor’s Office, designed this survey to provide a comprehensive, data-driven foundation for understanding how the CSU community uses AI, how it perceives it and what support is needed to ensure that the benefits of AI reach every corner  of the university.DESIGN AND INSTRUMENT
The survey was developed by researchers at San Diego State University supported by the CSU Chancellor’s Office. The study was reviewed and approved by the San Diego State University Institutional Review Board. It was administered as an anonymous online questionnaire with two parallel tracks, one for students and one for faculty and staff. Both tracks included five sections of Likert-scale items measured on a six-point scale ranging from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to “Strongly Agree” (6), along with questions about specific AI tools and three open-ended response prompts. The faculty and staff track included an additional section on AI classroom policies and practices.
The five core sections assessed the following areas: 
1. Awareness and understanding of AI 
2. Experience and usage of AI
3. Perceptions and attitudes toward AI
4. Skills, education and training in AI
5. Future expectations of AI
Respondents were also asked to identify which AI tools 
they use, rank their top three and provide details on version type (free, paid or college-provided), frequency of use and primary use cases. The 21 AI tools included in the survey were: Adobe Express, Brainly, Canva, CapCut, ChatGPT, Claude, Cursor, DALL-E, DeepL, DeepSeek, GitHub Copilot, Google Gemini, Grammarly, Grok, Midjourney, Microsoft Copilot, NotebookLM, Perplexity, QuillBot, Stable Diffusion and Zoom AI Companion. The full survey instrument is available at aisurvey.sdsu.eduPURPOSE
The purpose of this study is to understand how students, faculty and staff across the California State University use AI, how they perceive it and what they need to engage with it effectively. The findings are intended to advance three goals: supporting thoughtful AI adoption across the university; informing the development of guidance, resources and policies; and ensuring that the CSU’s approach to AI reflects its longstanding commitment to inclusivity, equity  and student success.
SCOPE
With more than 94,000 respondents from 22 CSU universities* and the Chancellor’s Office, this is, to the best of our knowledge, the most comprehensive survey of AI perceptions, usage and needs ever conducted at a single college or university system.

A total of 94,060 respondents (80,626 students, 6,085 faculty and 7,349 staff) participated in the survey. As reflected 
in the institutional dashboard, the number of responses varies by question, as not all respondents completed every section of the survey. All percentages in this report are based on valid responses to each item, consistent with the response counts published on the dashboard. 
Throughout this report, findings from the Likert-scale items are reported as the percentage of respondents who 
selected “Somewhat Agree”, “Agree” or “Strongly Agree” (values 4, 5 or 6 on the six-point scale), referred to collectively as the agreement rate. Tool usage rates represent the share of respondents who reached the tool section of the survey and reported using a given tool. Use case and version percentages are calculated among respondents who ranked a tool in their top three and answered the respective question, consistent with the institutional dashboard methodology. 
Demographic percentages are based on respondents who completed the survey (75,620 students, 5,850 faculty and 
7,095 staff). Demographic characteristics have not been weighted to match the CSU’s overall enrollment or workforce profile and may differ from institutional totals.
The report presents findings for three groups: students, faculty and staff. Where the survey language differed slightly 
between tracks to reflect the distinct contexts of each group, those differences are noted. Classroom policy questions were asked only of faculty. ADMINISTRATION AND TIMELINE
The survey was distributed via email to students, faculty and staff at 22 CSU universities* and the Chancellor’s Office in fall 2025, with campus coordinators supporting outreach at each site. Participation was voluntary, and respondents could skip any question or discontinue at any time without penalty. The survey took less than 10 minutes to complete. All survey responses  were anonymous.A NOTE ON PARTICIPATION
Participation rates varied across the 22 universities. This is expected in a voluntary survey of this scale. All findings reflect the perspectives of those who chose to respond; participation was voluntary and results may not be representative of the full CSU population. Readers should exercise caution when drawing campus-level conclusions. Cal Poly San Luis Obispo and Cal Maritime are in the process of integrating into a single university. The transition is expected to be official in fall 2026. This survey reflects all 22 universities and the Chancellor’s Office as they were constituted in fall 2025.
