# Argentina Just Opened a Striking New Sculpture Museum at the Foot of the Andes

Source: [elledecor](https://www.elledecor.com/life-culture/travel/a73407005/cesar-pelli-argentina-museum-lola-mora/)

During his six-decade career, the influential architect César Pelli helped to shape skylines from New York to Kuala Lumpur, crafting bold skyscrapers with distinctive silhouettes that were never shy about announcing their presence. When the founder of Pelli Clarke & Partners died in July 2019, his final design was an extraordinary yet intimate art museum—and the firm’s first cultural project in his native Argentina. Now, seven years after Pelli’s passing, that vision has materialized with the unveiling of the Lola Mora Cultural Center in the city of San Salvador de Jujuy.
Set at the foot of the Andes, the new museum opened its doors in early July and honors the trailblazing Argentinean artist Lola Mora, who is considered one of Latin America’s leading sculptors of the early 20th century. Mora achieved widespread fame with a series of six Carrara marble neoclassical statues commissioned for the Palace of the Argentine National Congress in Buenos Aires. Installed in 1906, the allegorical figures (some nude) caused a stir throughout Argentina. The works were ultimately removed, donated to Jujuy, and installed outdoors.
Newly restored, those six sculptures now anchor the 32,000-square-foot cultural center, displayed together for the first time in over a century. Referencing Mora’s painstaking creative process, Pelli conceived the two-story building’s layout to evoke a sculptor’s chisel from above, with visitors entering through a long corridor that represents the handle. At the end of the bridge-like hall, the structure fans out like a blade, with a curving gallery wrapped in floor-to-ceiling glass that overlooks a lush subtropical forest and mountains beyond.
There, the six sculptures—The Lions, Liberty, Progress, Work, Peace, and Justice—enjoy pride of place, divided into two groupings at either end of the space. The arrangement allows visitors to walk around the works for a 360-degree view. To avoid distracting from the art, Pelli selected a pared-down material palette comprising iron-free glass, native palo blanco hardwood, and hand-chiseled local stone, all of which serve to make the center feel integrated into the natural setting.
Besides the main exhibition gallery, the complex includes a library, restaurant, educational center, shop, and a multipurpose atelier for visiting artists and rotating shows.
The gardens, meanwhile, were designed by Balmori Associates, the landscape firm founded by Pelli’s late wife, Diana Balmori. From the outset, the goal was to preserve the existing ecosystem, relocating trees only when absolutely necessary. Because the 3.2-acre site was formerly a botanical garden, the team was able to work with an array of established flora to create a lyrical outdoor environment with minimal intervention.
Aiming for LEED Gold certification, the building also incorporates an array of sustainable technologies and generates 100 percent of its own power. Features include a bell tower-like wind turbine, photovoltaic roof panels, and a rainwater harvesting system.
The result is a sculptural building that feels both poetic and restrained. Not only is it a graceful tribute to an important yet underappreciated artist, but also a fitting final act for a generational talent like Pelli.
