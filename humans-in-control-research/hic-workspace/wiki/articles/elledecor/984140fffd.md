# 6 Fall 2026 Interior Trends to Watch, According to Experts

Source: [elledecor](https://www.elledecor.com/design-decorate/trends/a73490430/fall-2026-interior-trends/)

If summer is the season of raw aesthetic inspiration, fall is its executive counterpart. The most exciting 2026 fall interior design trends are living proof: From timeless palette revivals to innovative surface materials, designers are translating decor dreams into routines, rebrands, and blueprints.
This fall, moody vibes are on the menu—but not in the predictable iterations you might expect. From rich wood stains and liquid-like surfaces to homes borrowing from hotel motifs, expect plenty of playful tension. (And where there’s tension, it’s good to know that patina is welcome.)
Although the concepts ahead are buzzy enough to land on our fall trend list, skeptical investors can take comfort in the fact that longevity decor is en vogue. “Clients are designing for the now, but also the future,” says Noa Santos, principal designer and CEO of design firm NAINOA. “It changes the questions we ask, but also how we start thinking about how a room functions today and how it could be repurposed later on.”
Read on for designers’ insider takes on the best interior design trends for the months ahead.
Dark Wood Revival
Wood tones are trending dark and sultry this fall. “It’s refreshing that after a decade of bleached wood, we’re seeing a return to medium and darker tones like walnut, espresso oak, and wenge,” Santos says. “They can read as more serious, but also pair well with the plaster and stone we’re leaning into.”
As more designers integrate dark wood into diverse spaces, we’re reminded of its versatility: “When we pair these deep timber tones with light stone materials, we achieve a crisp, tailored contrast, whereas pairing them with dark stones lets us create an intimate, moody atmosphere perfect for custom bars or lounge areas,” Monica Santayana and Ronald Alvarez of design firm Moniomi tell ELLE Decor.
Dublin-based designer Róisín Lafferty expects these moody tones to bleed into other surfaces as well. And although they’re likely to spike in popularity, darker drenches have a timeless payoff that even the most trend-averse can get behind. “Tobacco browns, mineral greens, and earthy clay tones all have a connection to the natural world, giving them a longevity that doesn’t feel trend-led,” she says. As for dark wood palette pairings, Lafferty favors oxblood and burgundy with rich walnut—plus a few brass accents for muted brightness.
Big Entrances
As summer holidays start to wind down, hospitality-inspired trends are also trickling into home decor—at least among Santos’s clients. He reports design elements commonly found in boutique hotels, most notably the well-curated, tone-setting entryway, are becoming more in-demand for residential spaces. “It becomes an arrival instead of just another pass-through foyer,” he says. “Layered lighting scenes instead of an overhead fixture [give the space] a moodier touch.”
Wellness Aesthetic (and Investment)
And what’s a hotel-coded home without a spa-like touch? “Wellness spaces and dedicated suites devoted to promoting longevity isn’t a new trend, but it is evolving,” Santos says, noting that clients are building spaces around cold plunges and saunas—mechanical and plumbing considerations included—rather than adding them onto existing blueprints for flair. Wellness splurges may be trending, but they’re manifesting in long-term investments.
Reflective Surfaces
Mirror, mirror, off the wall: This fall, Lafferty is excited about unexpected reflections in design. “I’m fascinated by reflection as a material in itself and the way it can alter our perception of a space,” she says, noting that unconventionally placed bronze mirrors and smoked or tinted reflective glass can dramatically shift a room’s energy because of how they manipulate light differently.
“I love using these finishes architecturally,” Lafferty adds. “A bronze mirrored threshold can blur where one space ends and another begins, while a mirrored ceiling can change your perception of height.” To keep things grounded, she recommends balancing highly polished surfaces with more tactile elements, like timber, natural stone, or soft upholstery.
The reflective surface effect can also be accomplished more subtly, without glass and mirror: The Moniomi team says that they utilize high-gloss paint, hand-poured lacquer, and polished metallics “as dynamic architectural tools rather than mere finishes.” Their pro tip: Extend a glossy paint job to the ceiling to make enclosed areas feel more expansive. “Using these sleek, liquid-like reflections brings a sense of tailored polish, turning static surfaces into active design features,” says Alvarez.
Material Tension
The best way to make a decor element pop right now? Sandwich it between unlike objects or surfaces. “I’m much more interested in creating tension between materials than in having everything sit too comfortably together,” says Lafferty. In practice, she recommends contrasting raw materials against highly polished; natural stone against lacquer; or traditional craft items within contemporary architecture.
The Lived-In Look
In recent design trends, Santos has observed a phenomenon he calls “the anti-flip house.” After a period of design trends with so-called ‘universal’ buyers’ appeal—think: open floor plans, white oak, brass fixtures—he says clients are favoring more personalized design and architectural quirks.
Berkshires-based designer Jess Cooney has also observed a rejection of blank-slate perfection heading into fall: “I feel that we are going back to beautiful patinas and worn materials again after going through a very restrained period, where clients wanted fully stain-proof and scratch-proof items in their homes and kitchens.” With that shift comes not just acceptance, but attraction toward personality, wear, and patina. “I think as AI continues to take over some industries, unique items will have more and more value—which I am very excited about,” she says.
