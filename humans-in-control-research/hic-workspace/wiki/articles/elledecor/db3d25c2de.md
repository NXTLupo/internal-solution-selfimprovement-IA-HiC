# How <i><em>The Shards</em></i> Built the Ultimate ‘80s L.A. Dream Homes

Source: [elledecor](https://www.elledecor.com/design-decorate/a73405862/the-shards-set-design-80s-homes/)

Money can’t buy happiness, but it can buy a long driveway, a backyard pool, and a bedroom far enough from your parents’ that you can come and go unnoticed. The world of The Shards, Ryan Murphy and Bret Easton Ellis's new FX thriller, is populated with unhappy people making the most of these amenities. Adapted from Ellis's semi-autobiographical 2023 novel, the series follows a fictionalized teenage version of the author (played by Igby Rigney) through his senior year at an elite Los Angeles prep school in 1981. A new classmate enters his tight circle of friends just as a serial killer begins stalking the city's teenagers.
For Emmy-award winning production designer Cat Smith, who previously worked with Murphy on All’s Fair, that world wasn’t difficult to imagine. “I am a local to Los Angeles, I grew up there, and I was alive during that period,” she tells ELLE Decor. “Plus, I went to private school. So I knew what some of these bigger, crazier houses looked like.”
Smith was determined not to lean on the version of the 1980s that has calcified online—a problem she had to navigate almost immediately while doing research. "I ran into so many AI versions of the 80s," she says. "It was a detriment to my research, because you'd have to figure out that it was AI." She repeatedly encountered a movement called VaporCore, built around copious use of neon. "But it just took that one element and made it into a whole room, which is not how architecture or interior design works," she says.
The reality of the era was far more transitional. "This is also not even the 80s, it's like 1981," Smith explains. "So really, for the most part, the interiors you're looking at would be 70s."
The exception is the Bel Air home of Debbie Schaffer, the group's golden girl played by Hayes Warner. Her father, a movie producer, is exactly the kind of man who "would want to have the latest of everything," Smith says. To build this monument to new money, Smith used a hybrid approach. The production found a 1940s-era Bel Air mansion that was sitting completely empty, and they dressed the downstairs to serve as the practical location, adding their own architectural elements, while constructing the upstairs bedrooms on a soundstage.
Because the family bought into "whatever was trendy at the moment," Smith layered their rooms with high-80s glam and shiny surfaces. In an Ellis narrative, surfaces are everything; brand names and expensive objects serve as emotional armor against a deeply hollow reality. In the living room, Smith sought out the era's signature high-low sculpted carpeting, laying down a rug that sweeps through the space with a raised black ribbon shape. Upstairs, Debbie’s mother Liz guards her secrets behind a mirrored closet door, and both women sit at "giant vanity mirrors with their lights," a design choice Smith notes is important “to be able to reflect back what they’re seeing.” Even her father's bedroom ceiling was clad in a found gold mirror with deep grooves.
The status signaling extended to the smallest details. "The 80s were the pinnacle of the landline," Smith says, noting that a specific telephone "was a status symbol of who you were." Debbie naturally gets a giant pink lips phone, while the team's hunt for authentic background props turned up everything from a Garfield phone to a crystal-boxed Gucci model.
Bret’s house required a completely different kind of retrofitting. Located high on Mulholland Drive above the San Fernando Valley, the production secured a mid-century hillside home with a sprawling view of L.A. and the requisite pool, but the real-life owners had recently executed a bright, modern renovation featuring a white kitchen. "I got rid of the open spaces and I made it like late 70s style; a little bit like Laurel Canyon-ish," Smith says. Like Debbie's house, the downstairs is a practical location, but Bret's bedroom and bathroom were constructed on a soundstage.
While the Schaffer house is about projecting wealth, Bret’s bedroom is about projecting an identity. Because Bret is a writer and an unreliable narrator, Smith designed the space as a subjective memory. "You don't know exactly how he's remembering it," she says. "Maybe he's remembering it a little bit more glamorously than actually happened."
The room is a curated teenage ecosystem: a typewriter, a turntable, a cassette collection, and an old marbled-glass mirror that pulls from late-70s trends. His attached bathroom is crowded with hair and skin products, betraying the meticulous effort behind his casual prep-school facade. "He would be the most interested in grooming and putting on a specific face to the world," Smith explains. "That's what Bret's bedroom is about."
Across both homes, Smith relied heavily on reflective surfaces—a nightmare for cinematographers trying to hide their cameras, but a thematic necessity for the story. "There's nowhere to hide with mirrors," Smith notes. The continuous reflections create a dizzying sense of paranoia, a visual echo of the show's title, in which identities and realities are fractured into pieces. The teenagers of 1981 Los Angeles can retreat to their hillside estates or their Bel Air mansions, surround themselves with sculpted carpets and status-symbol telephones, and lock the doors. But in rooms designed to reflect their own vanity right back at them, they are never truly safe.
