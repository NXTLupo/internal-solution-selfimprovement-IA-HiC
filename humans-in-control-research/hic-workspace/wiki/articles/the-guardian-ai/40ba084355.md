# The Guardian view on the AI backlash: American voters want a say in their future | Editorial

Title: The Guardian view on the AI backlash: Silicon Valley’s political power is cracking

URL Source: https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/nov/06/the-guardian-view-on-the-ai-backlash-american-voters-want-a-say-in-their-future

Tech stocks have lost trillions while voters have rejected the industry’s political champions. The billionaire-led boom is facing a democratic reckoning

T his week’s midterms showed that the political bill for the AI boom is falling due. The Democratic sweep intensified fears over runaway spending, mounting debt and resistance to datacentres, sending tech shares tumbling. On Thursday Wall Street suffered its worst day since the pandemic as [$3.2tn was wiped](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/us/news/2026/11/05/us-stocks-nosedive-democrat-victory-sparks-investor-panic/) from markets. Anthropic, developer of the Claude chatbot, is hoping to float within the [next fortnight](https://sg.finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropic-ipo-turmoil-ai-sell-113638328.html) for $1.5tn (£1.1tn), but it may not now get even a trillion dollars. The dominant AI chipmaker [Nvidia saw its value crash](https://www.moneycontrol.com/europe/?url=https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/markets/nvidia-loses-440-billion-as-nasdaq-crashes-5-5-tesla-spacex-and-big-tech-stocks-wipe-out-trillions-14072173.html) by more than $400bn. The world’s richest man, Elon Musk, lost [$113bn in two days](https://www.theguardian.com/business/live/2026/nov/06/global-stocks-tumble-ai-panic-markets-democrats-midterms-success-jobs-oil-dollar-latest-news-updates?CMP=share_btn_url&amp;page=with%3Ablock-6aedb0198f08877db871e9e4#block-6aedb0198f08877db871e9e4).

While a few very rich people may be a little less wealthy, the sell-off may not be a bad thing. It brings a needed dose of reality to an industry whose spending plans and valuations depend on heroic expectations of future profits. That is a view held by many influential figures – from [Goldman Sachs’ research boss](https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/goldman-sachs-exchanges/the-ai-investment-boom-when-will-it-pay-off) to Nobel laureate [Daron Acemoğlu](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/podcasts/how-liberal-democracy-can-survive-age-spiraling-crises). AI may transform the economy. That does not guarantee investors will earn back the sums being poured into it.

Donald Trump is the other big loser. He rejected concerns about AI and the need to impose stricter rules despite [most Americans](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/6133962-voters-artificial-intelligence-federal-government-regulation-poll/) wanting more safeguards. He called concerns about the potential [dangers of AI development](https://abcnews.com/Politics/trump-breaks-republican-midterm-candidates-voters-key-tech/story?id=136540625) a “hoax”. Republicans paid a price for his refusal to listen. Mr Trump argued computing capacity was a national asset that needed to expand rapidly, while dismissing resistance to the infrastructure and the companies building it. The US president was deaf to voter frustration over [electricity prices, water demand and disruption](https://abcnews.com/Politics/texas-democrats-data-centers-winning-issue/story?id=137684995). Democrats could campaign against those costs falling on residents while the gains went to tech billionaires and their companies, which donated money to Mr Trump’s favoured causes.

Mr Musk’s SpaceX, a combined space, satellite-connectivity and AI group, [warned](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026042639/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm) in its June 2026 prospectus that new regulations could derail its growth plans. He spent [$200m](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-06/elon-musk-donated-millions-failed-republican-midterm-campaigns/107354322) in Texas for Republicans as Democrats took aim at AI datacentres in a state where there was rising voter discontent over the industry’s rapid expansion. Mr Musk could not buy this election. Democrats won a US senate seat, the state governorship and[wiped out every other statewide Republican office](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/11/04/gina-hinojosa-texas-democrat-governor/?utm_campaign=trib-social&amp;utm_content=1793839740&amp;utm_medium=social&amp;utm_source=twitter), taking the Texas house with a comfortable margin. Across the US, [the blue wave](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/the-election-day-revolt-that-threatens-americas-ai-boom-03a53b52?mod=ai_trendingnow_article_pos1) saw wins for Democrats who have been critical of datacentre construction and pledged to slow or pause their rollout.

Democrats say they intend to make AI companies answerable for the environmental and societal costs of their infrastructure buildup as well as the harm their products cause. The new Congress is likely to be [much more ready](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/democrats-planning-flood-ai-bills-when-they-take-control-congress-2026-11-06/) to shield voters from rising prices caused by datacentre expansion, ensure independent safety testing for advanced AI and clarify liability for possible harms. Democrats’ willingness to take public fears about AI seriously is welcome. People need confidence that elected governments can protect their livelihoods and communities.

Whatever happens to tech stocks, the genie of AI won’t be put back into its lamp. This year alone the technology was given as the reason [120,000 Americans lost their jobs](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/goldman-says-ai-layoffs-starting-130936875.html). Politicians ought to strengthen workers’ bargaining power and ensure that productivity gains are fairly shared. Just letting the markets decide will only deepen the economic insecurity and political distrust that is corroding democracy.

## This is just the beginning…

For two years, Americans have witnessed a largely unchecked assault on the foundations of US democracy. Authoritarian leadership has enriched itself through corruption, spread lies and launched relentless attacks on press freedom, voting rights, due process and many fundamental civil rights.

Last night, America’s embattled democracy came roaring back to life: from Texas to Ohio to South Carolina, Americans surged to the polls to fight for a different path forward. The process worked, the people spoke, the candidates – and the president – accepted the results. Today, we’re on the cusp of a new chapter for America, where the people have claimed their right to hold power to account, and millions are looking at politics with a new and unfamiliar feeling of hope.

The past two years have delivered a chilling lesson in just how fragile our fundamental freedoms are, when precious few powerful institutions are willing to stand up for the truth and defend our basic rights and freedoms. Major law firms and universities capitulated to a bullying president’s threats and intimidation. Some corporate news outlets bowed to his pressure, too.

In the face of these attacks, the independent press stood out as one of the only forces for accountability, dedicated to uncovering the truth – and making sure the public understood what was at stake in this election.

At its best, a truly free press can speak directly to the public, shedding light on hidden schemes and revealing the whole picture of our flawed world. Yet these are challenging times for press freedom: Trump’s crackdown on the press shows no signs of letting up; consolidated ownership means fewer options and less diversity of perspective; and the advent of AI has threatened the entire business model of journalism, resulting in extreme staff layoffs that have gutted newsrooms.

We will need a free press in 2027 as much as we relied on one in 2025 and 2026. The Guardian is fortunate to have no billionaire owner, no corporate bosses who put profit over mission. We’re accountable to the facts and to our readers. And the Guardian is fortunate to have our readers who are willing to support – and commit month after month – to help sustain our independence.

**From reporting on the ground to fending off legal risks to keeping our reporters safe, journalism is incredibly expensive to do well. To help fund our work in 2027, we are looking to raise $2,000,000 by the end of the year. If you can contribute any amount, please do so. If you can contribute monthly, even better – I need to know we have the resources to report on the final throes of Trump’s presidency in what may be a tumultuous year ahead.**

Betsy Reed

Editor, Guardian US
