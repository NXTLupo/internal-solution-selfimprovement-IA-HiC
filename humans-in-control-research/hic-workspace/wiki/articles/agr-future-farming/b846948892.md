# Photos | Robots, drones and packed fields at Dutch Arable Farming Day

Source: [agr_future_farming](https://www.futurefarming.com/uncategorized/photos-robots-drones-and-packed-fields-at-dutch-arable-farming-day/)

The organisers of Arable Farming Day, held on 28 May in the Dutch town of Lelystad, Flevoland, look back very positively on the event. More than 1,000 visitors attended a wide range of demonstrations, field tours, presentations and exhibitor stands.
On a sunny day, visitors watched a variety of field demonstrations with great interest. They saw mechanical and laser weeding robots, hoeing machines, cover crop incorporation techniques and drones spreading fertiliser. Exhibitors were pleased with the quality of the audience, as visitors arrived with clear objectives and targeted questions. There was also extensive interaction between visitors, Wageningen University & Research (WUR) researchers and other speakers during fourteen well-attended “Ask Anything” sessions. The annual event takes place at the WUR Open Field Crops site in Lelystad and is organised by Boerderij van de Toekomst, ACRRES, the National Field Lab for Precision Farming (NPPL), Boerderij magazine and TREKKER magazine.
Plenty to see
6 machines were demonstrated for incorporating and reducing well-developed cover crops. For weed control, visitors could see three weeding robots: the TOR and Cropr laser weeders, and the Farming GT camera-guided hoeing machine supplied by Kooiman. Cropr originally developed its machine for weed control on chicory ridges, using hoes on the ridge sides, and is now adapting the technology for crops such as carrots, spinach, onions and lilies.
7 machines demonstrated mechanical weed control. Farmers showed considerable interest in the technology, critically assessing weed control performance and discussing the machines’ operation with suppliers.
Field demonstrations highlighted precision weed control through intelligent, highly accurate camera-guided in-row hoeing machines, either tractor-mounted or autonomous, a robotic gripper weeder, and the 9-metre-wide Lemken precision tine harrow working in sugar beet. The harrow features tine pressure adjustment from 100 grams to 5 kilograms via small hydraulic cylinders, allowing adaptation to varying soil conditions.
Precision in-row hoe demonstrations
2 arable farmers from Zeeuws-Vlaanderen were impressed by the tine harrow’s performance, noting that no beet plants were damaged. However, they admitted they would still find it nerve-racking to use it in their own sugar beet crops. As the saying goes when using a tine harrow: keep moving and do not look back. Precision in-row hoes on display included the Ullmana from AgriBiosolutions, the Invera from Einböck and the Samo in-row hoe.
Also attracting attention was Botato, a chatbot and research prototype of an AI assistant linked to Farmmaps. The system can answer potato growers’ questions about issues such as crop diseases and potential yields based on crop data.
Drone demonstrations
2 DJI Agras T50 agricultural drones were demonstrated. They can be used for spreading fertiliser and granules, sowing cover crops and catch crops, and applying foliar fertilisers and liquid fertilisers. Drones are becoming increasingly popular because they avoid crop damage, soil compaction and the spread of diseases. Sowing cover crops into standing wheat and maize is particularly attractive because it allows soil to remain undisturbed for up to 18 months.
However, there are limitations. Not everyone is permitted to operate these drones. The pilot, company and drone must all hold the required certifications. In the Netherlands, crop protection products may not be applied by drone, and flights are restricted in certain areas. Operating large agricultural drones is specialist work generally carried out by contractors. Contractors typically charge between €40 and €60 per hectare, depending on application rates, area and conditions, with capacities of around 40 hectares per day for spraying or 10 tonnes per day for spreading.
