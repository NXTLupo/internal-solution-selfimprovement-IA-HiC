# The New LinkedIn Content Playbook: AI, Collaboration, Creator Marketplace, and Out-of-Network Reach

Source: [social_media_examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/the-new-linkedin-content-playbook-ai-collaboration-creator-marketplace-and-out-of-network-reach/)

Curious which new LinkedIn features can help your brand reach entirely new audiences? Wondering how to stand out on LinkedIn when AI-generated content is flooding every feed?
In this article, you'll discover how collaborative posts and the creator marketplace work, how a new analytics metric reveals whether your content is breaking beyond your existing network, and how to use AI on LinkedIn without getting flagged.
#1: How LinkedIn Is Handling AI-Generated Content and What It Means for Marketers
LinkedIn has doubled down on reducing what the platform calls AI slop, low-quality content that appears to be generated by AI and lacks a clear perspective. Now, content flagged this way is less likely to be distributed beyond a person's immediate network, which directly undermines the goal most marketers have on the platform: reaching new people.
The irony isn't lost on anyone. LinkedIn was among the first platforms to build AI features directly into the compose box, encouraging users to enhance posts with AI. Now the platform is penalizing content that leans too heavily on those same tools. AJ Wilcox acknowledges the contradiction but sees the shift as a positive one. If every post on the platform reads like AI-generated filler, nobody stays. The feed becomes unusable, and LinkedIn loses its value as a professional network.
The distinction LinkedIn is drawing isn't between AI-assisted and human-written content. It's between content that communicates something of value and content that doesn't. AJ explains that sharing original experiences and genuine insights, especially insights so current that AI models haven't already absorbed them, is what separates useful content from slop. The goal isn't to avoid AI entirely. It's to be the brain behind what gets published.
Michael Stelzner describes his own approach to this balance. After training a Claude project on his writing voice, he initially used it to draft LinkedIn posts from scratch. Over time, he noticed the tells that both humans and algorithms can detect: formulaic phrasing, predictable structures, and a lack of genuine perspective. He shifted to writing entirely in his own voice first, then using AI as a consultant to identify weak spots and sharpen his hooks. The result is content that sounds like him rather than like it could have come from anyone.
The enforcement mechanism isn't purely algorithmic, though. LinkedIn's system flags content, but human behavior reinforces the signal. LinkedIn visibility has always been driven by engagement. Low-quality organic posts have always gotten low-quality reach; the AI crackdown simply formalizes what was already happening.
On the paid side, the dynamic is different. LinkedIn doesn't restrict the reach of ads based on whether AI created them. As long as an advertiser pays and stays within platform guidelines, the ad gets distribution. But AJ's clients see a clear pattern: ads that feel AI-generated or low-quality get less user engagement, which defeats the purpose of running them.
Pro Tip: LinkedIn has also introduced Brand Kit inside Campaign Manager, where advertisers can upload brand assets, including colors, fonts, and brand voice, to keep AI-generated ad copy and creative aligned with their guidelines. The platform now offers AI-powered image generation for ads as well, making it easier to produce ad creative within the LinkedIn ecosystem without external tools.
#2: LinkedIn Collaborative Posts and the Shift Toward Trust-Based Reach
In a landscape where anyone can spin up a professional-looking website and post five LinkedIn updates a day with AI, trust becomes the differentiator. Aligning a brand with people who already have credibility with an audience is one of the most effective ways to break through, and collaborative posts formalize that approach on the platform.
LinkedIn's new collaborative posts feature allows multiple profiles and company pages to publish and share a single post together. This isn't the same as tagging someone in a post, which can happen without their permission. Collaborative posts require explicit opt-in from each party, and every participant must approve the post before it goes live. When the post is live, all collaborators are visibly credited at the top.
AJ believes this feature will become table stakes. Company page posts on LinkedIn get minimal organic reach, as do reshares of those posts. Collaborative posts offer a way around both problems. Instead of asking employees to reshare a corporate announcement, a company can invite an employee to co-author a post that carries their name and reaches their network.
The opportunity extends beyond internal teams. Brands that find partners with tangentially related products or services, not direct competitors, can collaborate on content. Both company pages and individual thought leaders within those companies can appear on the post, extending reach across multiple audiences. And soon, pages and personal profiles will be able to share posts together, allowing a company to tag specific departments or individuals behind a product update.
How to Start Using Collaborative Posts on LinkedIn
The first step is identifying the people who already care about what the brand does and inviting them to collaborate on a shared topic. AJ recommends starting with people already in the brand's orbit: top fans, vocal commenters as well as current and past customers.

#3: Optimizing Profiles for LinkedIn's AI-Powered People Search
LinkedIn has expanded its AI-powered people search to all US users, removing the premium-only restriction.
The search now uses natural language, so users can describe who they're looking for by intent rather than plugging in exact keywords. Verification badges appear in search results acting as an added trust signal, and AI-generated profile summaries explain why a person is relevant, highlighting shared connections and experiences.

#4: The Creator Marketplace and How Thought Leader Ads Amplify Reach
LinkedIn has launched a creator marketplace within Campaign Manager where brands can find, evaluate, and partner with creators based on their expertise, audience, and content performance.
The marketplace is designed to make it easier for brands to launch thought leader ads, which are LinkedIn ad units that promote an individual's organic post to a targeted audience with paid dollars behind it. Thought leader ads are the cheapest and most effective ad format on LinkedIn, generating high reach and engagement at low cost.
The creator marketplace solves a common bottleneck: marketers who can't convince their CEO to write posts or record videos now have access to third-party voices who are already talking about the brand. The marketplace surfaces creators who have mentioned a brand in their posts, making it easy to identify organic advocates and request permission to sponsor their content.
When a brand sponsors a creator's post as a thought leader ad, the brand controls the audience targeting. Because the post already has social proof from its organic run, the sponsored version is more effective than a cold ad.
How Creators Can Attract Brand Partnerships Through LinkedIn
For creators looking to get noticed by brands through the marketplace, the path is straightforward: mention the brand in a post. That's enough to surface in the marketplace when the brand's marketing team checks.
