# YouTube on TV: What Marketers Need to Know

Source: [social_media_examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/youtube-on-tv-what-marketers-need-to-know/)

Wondering what YouTube's aggressive push into living rooms means for your channel strategy? Want to learn how to make sure your YouTube content looks great on a 65-inch screen?
In this article, you'll discover how to optimize your YouTube channel for TV viewers, create content that works on connected TVs, and use new YouTube features like unique reach metrics, Ask YouTube AI search, and buy-with-Google-Pay commerce.
Why YouTube on TV Changes Marketing
YouTube has made its intentions clear: TV is the priority. The platform has said repeatedly that connected TVs are the end goal, and every recent update points in that direction. For many channels, TV has already become the number one traffic source.
The shift matters for marketers because TV viewers are in a fundamentally different mindset than mobile viewers.
Someone watching on their phone during a train commute has low expectations for production quality. But someone who just finished a Netflix documentary and flipped over to YouTube is unconsciously comparing the two experiences. The brain carries over the production standard it just absorbed, even though the viewer knows they're now watching an individual marketers rather than a studio production.
Liron Segev explains that quality on TV doesn't mean perfection, though. Daily vloggers have already trained audiences to accept shaky camera work and imperfect production and as AI makes everything look polished, that authenticity may matter even more than technical precision. The key distinction is being mindful of where the audience is and meeting them there.
#1: Optimize Your YouTube Channel Banner and Thumbnails for TV
The biggest mistake Liron sees is marketers who never watch their own channel on a TV. Most check their content on a computer or phone before publishing, but few actually sit on their couch, at viewing distance from the screen, and experience their channel the way a TV viewer would. That perspective check is free and reveals problems that don't show up on smaller screens.
The first low-hanging fruit is the channel banner. Liron says this is free real estate that most marketers haven't given a second thought.
On TV, the banner displays additional space at the top and bottom that doesn't appear on phones or computers. YouTube provides dimension guidelines when uploading a banner, showing exactly where the image will crop on each device. marketers should ensure their banner works across all three formats, and can even place additional branding or promotional messaging in the TV-only areas.
Thumbnails are the second priority. YouTube recently expanded the thumbnail file size limit from 2 megabytes to 50, enabling 4K resolution uploads. But a higher-resolution version of a cluttered thumbnail is still a bad thumbnail. The fundamental rule hasn't changed: a thumbnail's only job is to stop someone from scrolling. Clean design, simple colors, and minimal text work on every screen size.
YouTube has also introduced an AI-powered feature that automatically upscales videos uploaded below 1080p to higher resolution. Brands can disable this if they prefer to control their own quality.
The third piece of low-hanging fruit is delivering on the promise made in the title and thumbnail. Whatever the title promises, the video should deliver immediately. Liron emphasizes starting with that delivery in mind, then expanding into deeper content afterward. This approach also matters for AI discovery, where the first 100 words of a video's transcript carry outsized weight.
#2: Create Longer, Episodic YouTube Content for the Couch Audience
TV viewers lean toward longer content. 10 minutes, once the stretch goal for many marketers, is now a starting point for TV-optimized videos. The data supports this shift, and the format aligns with how people consume content on their couches: laid back, drink in one hand, phone in the other, looking for something to settle into.
Liron recommends leaning into what he calls the “infotainment” approach. The content should be informative, but it also needs to keep the audience engaged over a longer runtime. Traditional YouTube prioritized tight editing: get in, deliver value, get out. TV viewing rewards a more relaxed pace where viewers feel like they're hanging out with the marketers rather than being lectured to.

Think Episodic, Not One-Off Videos
The content strategy should shift from standalone how-to videos toward episodic formats. People subscribe because they want to know what happens next, not just because they needed a three-step fix for their Wi-Fi. A quick tutorial doesn't need to be 45 minutes long, but content designed for TV should have a story arc that pulls viewers through and brings them back.
Re-hooks, brief periodic recaps designed to re-engage distracted viewers, matter more in the TV environment. Viewers watching on TV are often simultaneously on their phones, so marketers should build these re-engagement moments into longer content without being condescending. Liron suggests framing these naturally: “We've just covered part A; let's get into part B now.”
#3: Understand YouTube's New Unique Reach Metric
YouTube is rolling out a new analytics metric called Unique Reach. Unique Reach estimates the total number of people watching a video, including co-viewers on shared TV screens who aren't logged in separately. It goes beyond the existing unique viewers metric, which counts only individual logged-in accounts, to approximate how many people are actually in front of the screen at the same time.
The metric draws on signals like time of day (evening viewing likely means more household members present), the device type (TV versus mobile), demographics, and video genre. It's modeled on how traditional TV advertising measurement works through systems like Nielsen ratings, which are themselves estimates rather than exact counts.
Liron sees the metric as a useful indicator rather than a definitive number. For marketers with baked-in sponsor segments, the higher unique reach number can strengthen the value proposition when negotiating deals. It looks better in reports, makes budget approval easier, and supports the case for ongoing partnerships.

One creative application of this shift is scripting content that invites group participation. Cooking channels already do this well, saying things like “make sure your kids are helping you” or “get your team around for this.” This shifts the traditional YouTube one-to-one address toward a communal experience that's more natural for TV viewing.

#4: Prepare for Ask YouTube and AI-Powered Search on TV
YouTube is expanding Ask YouTube, its Gemini-powered AI chatbot, to smart TVs, gaming consoles, and streaming devices. Ask YouTube lets viewers use their TV remote's microphone button to ask questions, get AI-generated answers, and search within videos, all without leaving the YouTube app or picking up their phone.
Jerry Potter draws a parallel to what AI overviews have done to blog traffic: if viewers can get answers without watching the video, some view counts could drop. Liron agrees the parallel is valid but reframes the concern. Marketers don't ultimately care about views; they care about results. If a brand becomes the trusted answer source that AI cites, and that leads to phone calls and customers, the outcome is more valuable than raw view counts that don't convert.
