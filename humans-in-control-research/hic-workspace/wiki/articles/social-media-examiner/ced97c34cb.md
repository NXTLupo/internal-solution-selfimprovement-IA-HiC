# New Ways to Expand Reach and Engagement on Instagram

Source: [social_media_examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/new-ways-to-expand-reach-and-engagement-on-instagram/)

Wondering how to get more eyes on your Instagram carousels and posts without creating extra content? Curious which of Instagram's latest features can help small businesses reach new audiences?
In this article, you'll discover how to use Instagram's newest updates, including the TV app, AI-powered story effects, audio swapping, and adding music to carousels for Reels feed distribution, to expand your reach and engagement.
#1: Instagram's TV App Expansion and What It Means for Marketers
Instagram's TV app has expanded to Samsung TVs, joining Amazon Fire TV and Google TV. That means the app is now available on more smart TVs than not. The setup process is simple: download the app, scan a QR code with a phone, and an Instagram account syncs to the big screen within seconds.
The TV experience is organized around personalized interest categories, similar to Netflix. Instagram expert Elizabeth Marberry describes seeing a “For You” section at the top, followed by categories based on her recent activity, including back-to-school content, fitness, and humor. The layout draws on the same recommendation-based algorithm that drives Instagram's mobile experience, but surfaces it in a lean-back, browse-by-category format.
The categories are more specific than broad labels. Elizabeth noticed one of hers was “inclusive fitness,” not just “fitness” or “fitness for women over 40.” The platform categorizes content with surprising granularity based on consumption patterns. Elizabeth advises against forcing a brand into a particular category or stressing about which one the algorithm assigns. What matters more is being crystal clear on what an account wants to become known for. When all of a brand's content consistently reinforces a single focus, the algorithm handles categorization on its own.
Instagram stories are also viewable on TV. Elizabeth reports the experience was surprisingly engaging, even for someone accustomed to consuming stories on a phone. The platform also includes a “wide watch on widescreen” option, surfacing horizontal videos that had gone viral on Instagram and displaying them in a full-screen TV format.
Jerry notes that YouTube has already laid the groundwork for short-form and creator content on TV screens, and Instagram appears to be following that playbook. Meta is unlikely to abandon this direction, given how much YouTube has already normalized the behavior.
Elizabeth advises that marketers should treat Instagram on TV as a “watch, not a pivot.” There's no need to rebuild a content strategy around this update. The fundamentals still apply: strong messaging, a clear customer journey, and content that hooks viewers in the first few seconds. These elements translate to any screen. New features won't fix weak marketing.
An effective Instagram strategy starts with a foundational question: what does a brand want to become known for? Elizabeth explains that Instagram is now an interest-driven platform, and both the algorithm and the AI crawling account content need clear signals to categorize and recommend it. If a content strategy is scattered, posting about unrelated topics from week to week, neither the algorithm nor a new viewer can quickly understand what the account is about. Strategically positioning all content to reinforce one brand story is the most important thing a marketer can control.
That said, brands already producing horizontal or widescreen content could experiment with uploading it to Instagram. While browsing the TV app, Elizabeth noticed a videographer's horizontal video that had earned over 40,000 likes just days after it was posted. Trial Reels, which allow creators to test content with non-followers before committing to a full publish, offer a low-risk way to experiment with this format. The opportunity is niche-dependent; professional videographers and storytelling-heavy brands are better positioned than most to take advantage of widescreen on Instagram.
On mobile, horizontal video still doesn't display well in the standard Reels feed. It appears with black bars on top and bottom, and unlike YouTube and TikTok, Instagram doesn't currently allow users to rotate their phone for a full-screen horizontal view. Elizabeth recommends filling that unused screen space by adding text hooks above the video and engagement questions below it, so the content still takes up the full vertical frame. Jerry notes that Instagram's Edits app is also getting a desktop version and the ability to export longer videos, further signaling a move beyond vertical-only, mobile-only content.
Neither Instagram nor TikTok is available on Apple TV yet. Both apps are available on Amazon Fire TV, Google TV, and Samsung devices, but their absence from Apple TV means a significant segment of users can't access them through their preferred streaming device. Once Instagram arrives on Apple TV, that may signal a stronger commitment to the format.
Pro Tip: Reels are currently capped at 20 minutes for content to appear in the Reels feed. Regular posts can be up to 60 minutes. For anyone testing long-form content on Instagram, keeping videos under 20 minutes ensures maximum distribution via the Reels surface. Instagram will notify creators when a video's length will limit its reach, so the app itself provides a safeguard before publishing.
#2: Using AI-Powered Stories Effects and Keeping Stories Authentic
Instagram has introduced more than 30 new AI-powered effects for stories. These effects can transform photos by interpreting scene elements like lighting, composition, and subjects. Users can also create custom AI effects by entering their own prompts directly in the stories composer. The effects are accessed by tapping the three-star icon on the right side of the story creation screen.

These AI effects are also available on Reels, not just stories. Elizabeth describes using one that transformed a still image to look as though she was underwater, then adding text overlay on top.
From a marketing standpoint, Elizabeth sees these effects as useful for pattern interrupts rather than a daily strategy. During a product launch, for example, an AI-transformed image could stand out in a story feed where followers are accustomed to seeing standard selfies and talking-head clips. The novelty captures attention, and that moment of disruption can be paired with a strategic call to action.
Jerry raises an important concern: stories have traditionally been the most raw, authentic surface on Instagram. Adding AI effects risks eroding the very quality that makes stories effective for building trust. Elizabeth agrees. In her experience coaching clients, stories work best for brands that show up unpolished, share real moments, and speak directly to their audience.
Elizabeth shared an example: she posted a story about working from a library study room to hide from her kids during summer, and received a flood of messages from followers who related to the situation. That kind of human, off-the-cuff content drives more engagement than any effect.
For story frequency and quality, Elizabeth recommends five to six raw, off-the-cuff stories over two or three highly polished ones. Reducing friction around content creation leads to more consistency, and consistency deepens trust. Being “less precious” about each individual piece of content prevents the exhaustion that comes from overanalyzing every post.
Stories also function as a brand's warmest audience. The people watching stories are the hottest prospects, so the emphasis should be on fostering community through discussion, polls, and behind-the-scenes content rather than chasing effects.

#3: Swapping Audio on Published Posts and Carousels
Instagram now allows users to swap out the audio on already-published posts and carousels while preserving all existing engagement, including likes, comments, and shares. There's no need to delete and re-upload content.
The most straightforward use case is when a licensed song gets removed. Instagram pays for music licensing, and when contracts expire, the audio disappears from affected posts. Elizabeth has experienced this firsthand: videos that had performed well suddenly lost their sound months after publishing. Previously, the only options were to leave the post silent or take it down. Now, the audio can be replaced without losing any accumulated engagement.
A second use case is brand-safety related. If an artist becomes associated with a major controversy, brands may want to distance their content from that music. The swap feature allows this without sacrificing the post's performance history.
