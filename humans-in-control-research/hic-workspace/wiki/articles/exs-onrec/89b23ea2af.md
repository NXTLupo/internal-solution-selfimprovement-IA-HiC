# How Recruitment Agencies Know It's Time to Outsource Finance

Source: [exs_onrec](https://www.onrec.com/news/news-archive/how-recruitment-agencies-know-its-time-to-outsource-finance)

Placements climb, contractor headcount grows, invoices multiply across states and pay cycles, and the part-time bookkeeper who was fine at 12 consultants becomes a bottleneck at 30. Owners notice late because the pipeline still looks healthy. By the time margin leakage, late tax filings, or a botched contractor payroll shows up, the damage is already priced into the next quarter.
Outsourcing the finance function is one of the cleaner ways to get ahead of that curve, and the timing matters more than the decision itself. What follows is a staged view of how the transition tends to play out for a recruitment business, starting with the first warning signs and ending at steady-state operation.
The Early Signs Show Up in the Back Office First
The first phase is recognition, and it rarely arrives as a crisis. It shows up as friction. Timesheets take too long to reconcile, and contractor payments slip by a day or two. Management accounts land on the 20th of the month instead of the 10th, and when they do, nobody trusts the gross margin figure by desk or by client.
For a recruitment business, these small delays compound fast. Contractors talk to each other. A late payment on Friday becomes a resignation on Monday, and a client is suddenly asking why their developer didn't show up. The signals worth watching in this phase cluster around four areas:
- 
		Cash visibility. You can't say, within a few thousand dollars, what the bank balance will be in three weeks. Weekly contractor runs against 30 to 60 day client terms make the working capital gap hard to eyeball. 
- 
		Margin clarity. Gross profit per consultant, per desk, or per client isn't reported in a way anyone acts on. Decisions get made on billings, not margin. 
- 
		Compliance drag. Payroll tax deposits, state filings, and worker classification reviews for 1099 versus W-2 status start slipping down the priority list because whoever handles them is also chasing invoices. 
- 
		Owner time. The founder or MD is still approving supplier payments and signing off payroll by hand, which is a decent proxy for how immature the finance function actually is. 
Building the Case Before You Pick a Partner
The second phase is scoping, and it's where most agencies undersell themselves. The instinct is to outsource the bookkeeping and call it done. That solves the cheapest problem. The more useful question is which parts of the finance stack should stay in-house, which should sit with a partner, and what the reporting cadence needs to look like once the business is running at the next size up.
A smaller agency stepping up from a founder-run book to a professionalized one needs different things than a mid-market agency preparing for a controller layer. The first needs reliable management accounts, clean contractor payroll, and someone who understands temp margin. The second needs FP&A, scenario modeling for new desks or geographies, and a controller-level review a bookkeeper can't provide.
Providers like Strategic CFO tend to build the engagement in tiers for exactly this reason, so support scales with the stage of the business rather than being sold as a flat package.
The Transition Is Where Most Agencies Lose Momentum
The third phase is the handover, and it's the part nobody enjoys. Data has to move. The chart of accounts often needs rebuilding so margin actually reports by desk and by contract type.
Historic reconciliations get cleaned up. Approval workflows shift from "ask the MD" to a documented policy with thresholds.
Expect three months of noise. Contractor pay runs are the one thing you can't get wrong. Everything else can tolerate a bumpy week, but a missed contractor payment during transition will cost trust that takes a year to rebuild. Agencies that handle this phase well tend to do three things:
- 
		Run parallel for one cycle. The outgoing bookkeeper and the incoming provider both close the same month. Discrepancies get resolved before the internal team steps back. 
- 
		Freeze the chart of accounts mid-handover. Restructuring the reporting model is worth doing, but not while migrating balances. Do the move first, then redesign. 
- 
		Name a single internal owner. Usually the operations director, or head of finance if one exists. Outsourced doesn't mean unattended, and the partner needs one person who can answer questions in an hour, not a day. 
Steady State Looks Different Than Owners Expect
Once the transition settles, the finance function stops being a monthly scramble and starts being a source of decisions. Management accounts arrive on a predictable date. Contractor margin by client is visible.
Cash forecasts extend 13 weeks out with real confidence behind them. The MD stops approving small supplier invoices one by one and starts reviewing desk P&Ls.
The other shift is technology. AI and automation are moving through back-office functions fast, and outsourced providers tend to adopt those tools sooner than an in-house team of two. An outsourced partner absorbs that cost across a client base rather than passing a licensing bill to each agency.
