# Moving From Operator to Steward

Source: [exs_directors_and_boards](https://www.directorsandboards.com/articles/governance/moving-from-operator-to-steward/)

Many accomplished executives assume the transition from the C-suite to the boardroom will be a natural one. After all, they’ve spent decades making decisions, solving problems and leading organizations. But according to Rebecca Martinez O’Mara, director and chair of the nom/gov committee of Kadant and director of Enviri, effective board service requires a fundamentally different mindset. Success is no longer measured by the decisions directors make themselves, but by the quality of the questions they ask, the perspective they bring and their ability to influence management without stepping into management’s role.
The shift, she says, is less about learning governance mechanics than learning to think differently. “I would say the biggest mindset shift for me was moving from being an operator who was trying to run a company to becoming more of a steward of the business,” says Martinez O’Mara. “That requires a completely different approach.”
Executives spend their careers solving problems directly. “As an operator, whether you’ve run a factory or a billion-dollar company, you’ve spent decades trying to fix things. You’re hands-on, sleeves rolled up, trying to improve operations, drive growth, increase revenue or solve problems.”
“As a director, it’s a much more patient game.”
Martinez O’Mara describes the difference with a phrase that has become central to how she approaches governance. “What I’ve learned is that I’m trying to shift the air.” The expression captures a subtle but important distinction between management and oversight. “What I mean by that is there are situations where I might ask for information to better understand the strategy. That question may not produce an immediate change during the meeting itself. But then I’ll notice another director follows up on the issue or management returns with additional analysis at a later meeting.”
Rather than directing action, directors influence “how the company thinks without trying to manage the business directly. I’ve done it in a way that keeps the long-term interests of shareholders and the company at the forefront,” says Martinez O’Mara.
That philosophy also shapes how Martinez O’Mara prepares for meetings. Like many first-time directors, she initially assumed success depended on mastering the board materials. “Coming from an executive role, I initially thought I simply needed to study the board materials harder. That turns out not to be the answer.”
New directors often find themselves learning an entirely new language. “When you’re new to a board, much of the material is unfamiliar,” says Martinez O’Mara. “You’ve never seen the formats before. You may be in a new industry. There are different acronyms, different reporting structures and different ways of presenting information. You can’t expect yourself to understand every detail immediately.”
Instead, she believes the most valuable preparation happens between meetings. “What I’ve learned is that the real work happens outside the boardroom.”
For Martinez O’Mara, that means continually expanding her understanding of topics that influence governance, even when they fall outside her immediate responsibilities. Recently, that meant spending hours exploring executive compensation — not simply how compensation programs are structured, but how they influence executive behavior.
“What I wanted to understand were the second- and third-order effects of different incentive structures,” says Martinez O’Mara. “For example, what happens when 60% of executive compensation is equity? How does that change behavior? How does it influence decision-making?”
For Martinez O’Mara, becoming an effective director isn’t about accumulating expertise in a single discipline; it’s about continually broadening the perspective directors bring into the boardroom. That broader perspective also helps prepare directors for aspects of board service that many executives never anticipate.
“I think one of the biggest surprises is just how much responsibility comes with the role,” says Martinez O’Mara. “When you’re an executive, you’re responsible for your own area of the business. As a director, you’re responsible for the entire enterprise. That’s a different level of accountability.”
Committee work, she adds, often proves far more demanding than new directors expect. “The board meetings are important, of course, but much of the detailed work happens within the committees. Audit committees, compensation committees, nominating and governance committees — they all require significant preparation, and each one has its own body of knowledge that directors need to develop.”
Those demands can also change dramatically depending on what’s happening at the company. “Serving on an audit committee during a major corporate transaction is a good example. It’s no longer just about attending the regular committee meetings. There are special meetings, additional materials to review, discussions with advisors, and significant time spent understanding the transaction, the associated risks, the financial implications, the required disclosures, and, ultimately, whether the board is making decisions that protect and create long-term shareholder value.”
Even seemingly routine matters can become unexpectedly complicated. “When I first joined a public company board, I didn’t fully appreciate how receiving stock grants would affect my own financial planning,” says Martinez O’Mara. “You’re making decisions about holding periods, taxes, diversification and insider-trading restrictions. Those become very real issues.”
She also points to practical governance requirements that rarely receive much attention until directors encounter them firsthand, such as understanding blackout periods and reporting requirements. None of those responsibilities are particularly visible to shareholders. Yet collectively they represent the broader governance discipline that distinguishes experienced directors from executives who are still approaching the boardroom as operators.
Ultimately, Martinez O’Mara believes the transition to board service is less about giving up leadership than redefining it. Directors create value not by making operational decisions themselves, but by asking thoughtful questions, expanding their own understanding and helping management see issues from a broader strategic perspective. The executives who become exceptional directors, she suggests, are the ones who recognize that influence — not control — is the board’s greatest source of value.
