# Executive Session, Episode 49: Jeffery Perry, Suzan Morno-Wade and Amy Shenkan

Source: [exs_directors_and_boards](https://www.directorsandboards.com/podcasts/executive-session-episode-49-jeffery-perry-suzan-morno-wade-and-amy-shenkan/)

In this episode of Executive Session, the official podcast of Directors & Boards, we speak with Jeffery Perry, director of Fortune Brands Innovations, Equitable and MasterBrand Inc., about right-sizing the board. Our second interview features Suzan Morno-Wade, director and compensation committee chair of Latham Pool Products Inc., on the relationship between the board, the CEO and the CHRO. And in our final interview, we talk to Amy Shenkan, director of EverCommerce and RingCentral, about the quarterly board meeting.
Don’t miss an episode! Follow the Executive Session podcast on YouTube, Spotify, iHeartRadio, Apple Podcasts, or anywhere else you get your podcasts.
Guests
Jeffery Perry
Jeffery Perry is director of Fortune Brands Innovations, Equitable and MasterBrand Inc. He is founder and CEO of Lead Mandates LLC and advisor of Conquer AI. Previously, he was global client service partner of EY, vice president of A.T. Kearney and principal of CSC Index. He began his career as an associate of Booz Allen Hamilton. He is chair of the board of trustees of Babson College, chair of the National Association of Corporate Directors Chicago Chapter and external advisor of UChicago Leadership & Society Initiative. Perry is a member of the council of board chairs of the Association of Governing Boards of Universities and Colleges.
Suzan Morno-Wade
Suzan Morno-Wade is director and compensation and leadership development committee chair of Veritiv and director and compensation committee chair of Latham Pool Group Inc. She is also founder and strategic advisor of Ascendio Management Group. She previously served as executive vice president and chief human resources officer of Xerox Holdings Corporation.
Amy Shenkan
Amy Shenkan is lead independent director of EverCommerce and director of RingCentral. She is also director of Pickles Auctions, vice chair of the board of Report Local and senior advisor of Altamont Capital Partners. The global advisory board member of Harvard Business School has previously served as a director of Zuora, Ritchie Bros., vRide Inc. and e.l.f. Cosmetics. She was president and chief operating officer of Common Sense Media, senior digital transformation expert of McKinsey & Company, interim executive and business advisor of AGS Advisors, senior VP of business development, investor relations and human resources of Travelocity and VP of strategic planning and online financial services for Wells Fargo.

Bill Hayes: Hello, and welcome to Executive Session, the official podcast of Directors and Boards. I’m Bill Hayes, editor-in-chief of Directors and Boards. In this episode of Executive Session, we’ll also explore the quarterly board meeting with Amy Shenkan, and right-sizing the board with Jeffery Perry.
Boards often talk about who sits in the room, but is that really the point? Today, we’re asking a tougher question — not how many seats you have, but what capabilities you actually need. My first guest, Jeffery Perry, director of Fortune Brands Innovations, Equitable, and MasterBrand Inc., argues that oversized, expertise-heavy boards can quietly become slower, more bureaucratic, and less equipped for what’s coming, from AI to cyber threats to geopolitical shocks. So what does a board built for five years from now actually look like, and how do you know when yours has crossed the line from healthy diversity of thought to dead weight?
Jeffery, many boards spend considerable time discussing board composition, but you argue that composition is ultimately more important than size. What are the most common mistakes boards make when they focus on filling seats instead of building the capabilities the company will need five years from now?
Jeffery Perry: Yeah, one of the things I see really fundamentally gets back to the fact that it’s important to have a skills matrix, which most boards benefit from using. With that as a backdrop, one of the mistakes I’ve seen related to just filling seats comes down to really fundamentally replacing board seats on a like-for-like basis. Say, for example, someone leaves the board who has a certain profile, and the desire is to fill that same profile going forward. While that may be okay, that may not be the skill that is actually needed going forward. So one mistake is just filling like-for-like, as opposed to taking a step back and thinking about what’s actually needed at that particular point in time.
Another mistake people make is making board decisions based on urgency. Let’s say, for example, there is an activist challenge or some other urgent situation where a board seat needs to be filled very quickly. Often there’s just a focus on responding to a certain challenge or request, as opposed to proactively thinking about what skills are needed in the future in terms of filling that seat going forward.
Another mistake that’s often made is what I call the resume trap — that’s when a seat is filled based on the prestige of a person coming onto the board, or the notoriety of someone coming onto the board, without necessarily thinking about the skills that are needed going forward. Now, that person may have the skills, but sometimes it gets trumped by the prestige or the status of that particular director.
And the final mistake I see is what I would call the relationship trap. Now, we know that a lot of board seats are filled through networks, and that’s fine — only certain board seats are filled through executive search. But when board seats are filled primarily from a relationship perspective, it’s easy to overlook what the actual skill sets are that are needed, and instead just think more about the relationships being fulfilled in terms of filling those seats.
So those are the four areas I see as common mistakes, but they all get exposed when you overlook the fundamental skills matrix, which I think creates significant value from a board governance perspective.
Bill Hayes: You suggest that many boards continue to over-index on traditional expertise while under-indexing on disruptive technologies and emerging risks, as AI, cybersecurity, and geopolitical uncertainty reshape the business landscape. What experiences or perspectives do you believe boards most urgently need to add today?
Jeffery Perry: Well, it’s hard to generalize — it depends on the industry, it depends on the board, and it depends on the circumstance. But in general, I would say it is important for most companies to have digital competency on their boards — certainly, from an AI perspective, that’s really important — as well as skills focused on disruption and change, and being able to bring that skill set onto the board. Director candidates who are able to demonstrate that add tremendous value to boards.
I do think, though, there is a watch-out from an overall nomination process perspective. If you bring someone onto a board who has specific expertise, which is needed — that’s fine, and that’s valuable. But it’s also really important for those directors to function as full directors, not just directors who are limited to that area of expertise. That has a benefit for the full board, but it also serves the individual director well, because you would not want someone to be limited in terms of what they can contribute while everyone else participates as a full director.
So what I mean by a full director is: in addition to their area of expertise, are they also asking the broader questions? Are they also participating in other discussions outside of their domain of expertise, so that they are recognized and participating as full board directors? So while areas such as digital, AI, and disruption are really important in terms of bringing those skills onto the board, it’s also important for those candidates to function as full directors, just like all other directors.
One other thing I would add is that this highlights, for all directors, the importance of board education, because no one is an expert in everything. But the most successful directors are able to learn about areas that may be outside their expertise, so that they can participate in broader discussions and function as full governance members across a wide range of topics.
Bill Hayes: You’ve said that oversized boards can become burdened by bureaucracy and slow decision-making. How can directors tell when a board has crossed the line from healthy diversity of thought to a structure that is actually hindering effective governance?
Jeffery Perry: Yeah, there are a few warning signs to pay attention to. One is if you see that meetings are running long but without deeper insight. You can have long board meetings with discussions that go on and on, but if those extended discussions aren’t unearthing additional insights, that’s a sign that the board may be too large, or the bureaucracy may be out of control. You may also see decisions get diluted or delayed because you simply have too many voices in the room. You do want constructive conflict, debate, and dialogue, but you want it to be a phase — you don’t want it to be a lifestyle. You want to have periods in your board discussions where you have an exploration phase, but then also a decision phase and an execution phase. You don’t want to live in the discussion phase for an extended period of time, because that keeps you from being able to actually make decisions.
