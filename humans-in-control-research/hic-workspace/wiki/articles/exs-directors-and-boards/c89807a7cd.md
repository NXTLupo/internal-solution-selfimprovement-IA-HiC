# Executive Session, Episode 50: Helmuth Ludwig, Calvin Butler and Nina Henderson

Source: [exs_directors_and_boards](https://www.directorsandboards.com/podcasts/executive-session-episode-50-helmuth-ludwig-calvin-butler-and-nina-henderson/)

In this episode of Executive Session, the official podcast of Directors & Boards, we speak with Helmuth Ludwig, chair of Humanetics Group and director of Myers Industries and Hitachi, about cybersecurity and AI; Calvin Butler, president and CEO of Exelon, about customer affordability, and Nina Henderson, director of International Workplace Group, about the unintended consequences of board decision-making.
Don’t miss an episode! Follow the Executive Session podcast on YouTube, Spotify, iHeartRadio, Apple Podcasts, or anywhere else you get your podcasts.
Guests
Helmuth Ludwig
Helmuth Ludwig is chair of the board of Humanetics Group, director and audit committee member of Hitachi, and director of Myers Industries. He is Professor of Practice for Strategy and Entrepreneurship at Southern Methodist University and senior advisor of Bridgepoint. Previously, he was chair of the boards of CIRCOR International Inc. and Commonwealth Center for Advanced Manufacturing. During a distinguished career at Siemens, he served in several leadership roles, including chief information officer and executive VP, digital enterprise realization.
Calvin Butler
Calvin Butler is president and CEO of Exelon. Butler oversees Exelon’s six local electric and natural gas companies – Atlantic City Electric, BGE, ComEd, Delmarva Power, PECO and Pepco. He served as CEO of Baltimore Gas & Electric from 2014 to 2021, and was senior vice president of corporate affairs for ComEd. Previously, he was senior VP, external affairs, at RR Donnelley, as well as president of the RR Donnelley Foundation. From 1996 to 1999, he was director of government affairs for Central Illinois Light Co. (a CILCORP subsidiary). He is the festival chair of Light City Baltimore.
Nina Henderson
Nina Henderson is a director of International Workplace Group PLC. She has also served as a director of Hikma Pharmaceuticals PLC, CNO Financial Group, Walter Energy Inc., Del Monte Foods Company, The Equitable Companies, AXA Financial, Pactiv Corporation, Royal Dutch Shell PLC and Hunt Corporation. She is a former corporate vice president of Bestfoods and former president of Bestfoods Grocery. Henderson was also the managing partner of Henderson Advisory, a consulting practice providing marketing perspective and business evaluation to investment management firms on consumer markets. She is vice chair of the Board of Trustees of Drexel University and a director of the VNS Health, VNS Health Choice, St. Christopher’s Children’s Hospital and the Foreign Policy Association. She is also a member of the Philadelphia Orchestra Kimmel Center and serves as commissioner of the Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery and as the president of the Kent Land Trust Foundation.

In this episode of Executive Session, we’ll also explore cybersecurity and AI with Helmuth Ludwig and the unintended consequences of board decision-making with Nina Henderson. Cyber risk doesn’t sit still long enough for a board to master it once and move on. The threat evolves faster than most governance structures were built to handle, and AI is only accelerating that pace. Helmuth Ludwig brings a rare vantage point on this challenge. He’s chair of Humanetics Group, a director at Myers Industries, and a director and audit committee chair of Hitachi, giving him a front-row seat to how different boards are dealing with AI oversight. He joins us to unpack where boards commonly stumble on cybersecurity, why it demands a different lens than other enterprise risks, and how committee structure, board composition, and director education need to evolve to keep pace. Helmuth, from a board’s perspective, what does effective cybersecurity oversight look like at the board level today, and where do boards most often get it wrong?
Helmuth Ludwig: Cybersecurity traditionally has been a little bit in the technology corner. I mean, look at it today — it touches really, literally every part of the corporation. It touches operations, finance, legal, and can have an enormous impact on your reputation. And so effective and efficient cybersecurity, number one, needs to start with transparency. Do you know where your company stands? Do you have the right processes in place, and do you have indicators that, number one, describe where you’re standing, absolute or relative to other companies, but also whether you’re getting continuously better? You have to assume you’re probably never 100 percent secure, that you cannot be breached. You have to be ready for the time when actually a breach happens, meaning when an unfriendly actor is able to enter your system. And then the key question is: have you trained how to act? Is your company ready to confront the situation? And also, is it able to recover? So the key point here is not only being ready to avoid as much as possible a potential cybersecurity threat, but also to have the resilience to recover adequately. And I think that the failure sometimes is really thinking, okay, well, we have some technology guys that will take care of it. We have to recognize it’s really literally a business topic, and because of this, relevant for all board members, and all board members should be able to ask the right questions to management regarding the resilience.
Bill Hayes: How should boards think about cyber risk differently from other enterprise risks, especially given the speed of change and the impact AI has on the threat landscape?
Helmuth Ludwig: Maybe let’s start with a similarity. When we look at enterprise risk, we always look at impact and probability, and that simple approach, of course, also is relevant for cybersecurity. You want to make sure you understand which type of assets are most important — where do you have the most critical IP in your company. But where the difference is, is the speed of change, which you mentioned already in your question. Let’s just dial the clock back two years. From detecting a so-called zero-day vulnerability — meaning where somebody encountered and found an issue in software which allows an actor to take advantage of it — to having this vulnerability exploited, meaning a bad actor uses it and really enters your system, there was a time difference of 53 days. Today, it’s hours, and I would expect in the future it will be minutes and maybe less. We saw this in April when Anthropic was coming out with a new offering, Mythos, and they had to recognize, shortly before the actual release date, that besides being a very powerful generative AI tool, it also was very powerful in identifying potential threats at companies — opening up companies’ vulnerabilities. And so they had to start to not make it generally available, but making it available only to very selected software companies that tested their software first, in the frame of a project called Glasswing, where they made sure that the threats that could be identified in the software, they could identify and cover them. This was in April. It took more than two months until a reduced Mythos actually became available. So that already shows the enhanced exposure and enhanced risk. Now the question is, what can companies do about it? When I was CIO at Siemens, we introduced a system, or process, called Zero Trust. If somebody is more interested in this, NIST has a very good publication about it, and NSA just came out this year with a very detailed description of the approach. But in simple terms, what it means is you never trust somebody — meaning somebody walks into your door, you first check identity, and second, you are very clear on which type of information, in this case, an individual is allowed to access. So your salesperson in the UK might have access to the CRM system in the UK, but not to your global ERP system, or not to the CRM information in, let’s say, North America. Now, what Zero Trust means, and this is where it really becomes very interesting, is that by identifying certain areas that only certain individuals are allowed to access, you make sure that you don’t get so-called lateral movements. What does that mean? Some of you might remember there’s a global retail chain that was attacked where somebody came in through the air-conditioning system. That makes a lot of sense — you want to have your air-conditioning being maintained, and being maintained from another area, so somebody is able to access your air-conditioning system. But they were not only able to access your air-conditioning system, they could move laterally into your cash register and access credit card information. And this is something you want to avoid. You want to avoid somebody being able to move from left to right, from maybe something which is not a very critical asset, to highly sensitive information. And I would recommend any board member get acquainted with what Zero Trust really means and what most modern cybersecurity tools allow companies to install to protect their critical assets.
