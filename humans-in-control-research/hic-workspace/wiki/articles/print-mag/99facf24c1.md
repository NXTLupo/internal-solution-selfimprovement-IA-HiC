# ONBOX Crowdsourced 100 Letters Hand-Painted at a Pop-Up for Bar Supernova’s Crafted Identity

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/branding-identity-design/bar-supernova-onbox-brand-identity/)

The newest bar to open in Vancouver is doing things a little bit differently— even in how they’ve gone about their brand identity. Located in the city’s Chinatown neighborhood, Bar Supernova, founded by bartenders Max Curzon-Price and Andrew Kong alongside chef-partner Shaun Couzelis, has been built around a waste-conscious philosophy, in which they transform food byproducts from other bars, restaurants, and cafes into new, shelf-stable ingredients. In doing so, they’ve “shaped their menu around creativity as much as sustainability.” The goal for Bar Supernova’s branding, then, was to develop a look that reflected that same level of ingenuity. They turned to the team over at the design studio ONBOX for the task, and, boy, did they deliver.
“When we kicked off, Max and Andrew described all these experimental, evocative things they wanted Supernova to feel like,” says Chris Rodgers, a Creative Strategist at ONBOX. “We kept coming back to how we could build that same spirit of experimentation into the brand. The idea that the process itself could carry meaning and become part of the story became the seed for something bigger: a wordmark with no single author.” That’s when they came up with the idea: asking guests at a Bar Supernova pop-up event to each paint a single letter that would then come together to create a modular wordmark. Over 100 guests participated, producing over 100 unique letters that now interchange in how the Bar Supernova name is written throughout the brand.
ONBOX then carried the quality of this innovative, expressive, and crafted wordmark into the rest of the system they built out, using the same irregular shapes, texture, and organic movement in the typography, color, real cut-paper forms, tactility, and handcrafted materials. This system extends across Bar Supernova’s menus, coasters, signage, and a new website, all balanced with white space and clear typography to keep all of that raw energy from tipping into chaos.
“This is exactly the direction Andrew and I wanted for Supernova. We just didn’t know what it looked like yet,” says Curzon-Price. “ONBOX helped us hone that ethos and vision through the brand work. For twenty-four years we’ve been dreaming about bringing our own bar into existence. They did more than give us a beautiful brand. They helped us articulate who we are and why we’re doing it.”
We take a deeper dive into ONBOX’s unique process for this project with co-founder and director Adam Hartley below!
Can you shed more light on the unique process you developed, and how the idea of crowdsourcing the calligraphy came to you?
It actually came out of Discovery with Max and Andrew. We were talking about art, curiosity, experimentation, and authenticity, and it got us wondering if there was some kind of experiment we could set up that would generate elements of the brand itself, something imperfect, human, and a little out of our control, almost like raw ingredients we could then shape.
We wrote an internal brief, got the whole team in a room for a brainstorm, and landed on a lot of ideas—everything from brush strokes as texture to guests literally hand-painting letters. The hand-painted letters won out because it tied the whole thing back to the people the bar is actually for: the guests.
What was the environment like at the pop-up? Can you set the scene?
It was one of a few pop-ups Max and Andrew ran to test the menu and their style of hospitality before opening. Dimly lit, loud, genuinely bustling. Guests came through a thick curtain at the door, and the second they were in, Max would yell, “Friends at the door!” and the whole room, guests and staff, would yell back, “Hi, friends!” About two seconds later, they’d hit our table: brushes, ink, and, “It’s time to paint your letter!” as our opening line.
The night got so busy, the reservations filled up completely, so we started moving from the door to guests’ actual tables. It stopped feeling like an activation, and started feeling like part of the service itself. At one point, we were even taking orders and reservations alongside the staff.
What was the curation process like?
We didn’t curate for “best,” we curated for authentic. If a letter looked more like a “u” than a “v,” we worked around it rather than fixing it; the whole point was staying true to the original handwriting. We were drawn to anything that showed real evidence of someone’s hand: rough brush strokes, inkblots, inconsistencies. We also had to think about variety. If too many letters overlapped in style, we’d catalogue and place them carefully so the finished mark still felt rich throughout. Honestly, we barely left anything out. We leaned into the chaos of what came back, and built the system to work with it, not around it.
Why was this the right approach for Bar Supernova specifically?
The brief wasn’t “make it pretty;” that was table stakes. They wanted something that felt more like art than branding—experimental, philosophical, a little imperfect, entirely human. So the process became less about building a rigid system, and more about trusting emotion and intuition. We tried to design almost as if we were guests ourselves, responding to the room rather than a brief on paper.
The real challenge was balancing that spontaneity with intention. The brand needed to feel alive and imperfect, but Supernova itself is actually incredibly thoughtful in everything it does. That tension ended up being one of the most rewarding parts of the project.
How did you build out the rest of the identity around the wordmark?
Once we had the letters, we studied what made them work—the irregular shapes, the organic movement, the texture and warmth—and translated those same qualities into the rest of the system. Left unchecked, that kind of expressiveness can tip into visual chaos, so we introduced structure through typography, composition, and a lot of white space to let those raw elements breathe without overwhelming everything. We leaned hard into real, tactile materials too, such as actual cut paper forms, handcrafted compositions, and imagery that felt genuinely human, using Matisse and Joan Miró as touchstones for balancing playfulness with sophistication. The read we were going for across the whole identity was “anti-speakeasy,” approachable, but extremely premium when it comes to craft. Poetic, but not precious.
What aspect of this project are you proudest of?
The collaboration. Everyone on the team touched some part of this brand, and it turned into a genuine passion project for all of us. Beyond that, seeing how much it has resonated with Max and Andrew has been the real reward. This has been their dream for years, and getting to help bring it to life turned into a real friendship, not just a client relationship.
