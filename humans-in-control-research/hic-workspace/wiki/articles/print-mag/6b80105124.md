# The Daily Heller: Irish Skies Are Smiling

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/daily-heller/the-daily-heller-irish-skies-are-smiling/)

Come Fly With Me: Aviation, Tourism and Irish Design, now on view at the National Print Museum in Dublin, examines Irish cultural history through examples of aviation- and tourism-related graphic design. It focuses on the 1940s through the 1960s, a period that marked the golden age of commercial aviation expansion, fueled by the mobility of U.S. consumers in the immediate post-WW2 period.
The exhibition, curated by Dr. Linda King, comprises travel posters, original sketches, brochures, tickets, baggage labels, in-flight publications and newsreels borrowed from a variety of Irish cultural institutions including The National Library of Ireland, The National Irish Visual Arts Library, The Irish Film Institute and the Little Museum of Dublin.
Come Fly With Me uses flight as a central thread to explore how design and print helped construct Irish national identity, support Irish modernization, fuel economic expansion and position Ireland politically on an international stage. It is an exploration of how Ireland saw itself, how it was seen by others and the legacy of those exchanges.
In all of this, a U.S. “gaze” is keenly felt: Ireland received tourism advice from the U.S. in the 1950s as it was a receipt of Marshall Aid funding. Ireland was horrifically poor at the time, and this advice encouraged Irish tourism agents to target U.S. tourists. There was a particular focus on strengthening links between Ireland and its diaspora in New York and Boston, which led to the establishment of Aer Lingus’ transatlantic route in 1958. Below, Dr. King tells us more.
What inspired you to curate this exhibition?
There were a couple of factors. Firstly, I completed my Ph.D. in 2007 on the advertising strategies of Aer Lingus in the 1950s and early ’60s (which I have continued to build on over the years) and long wanted to curate an exhibition on this material, as I thought it would have appeal for both the general public and the design community.
Secondly, 2026 marks the 90-year anniversary of the establishment of Aer Lingus as the Irish national airline, or “flag carrier.” This was a seismic achievement considering (partial) Irish independence from Britain had only been achieved in 1922. Aer Lingus was established by the Minister of Industry and Commerce, Seán Lemass, who said it was the “real instrument of liberation for this country,” connecting Ireland both physically and ideologically with the rest of Europe and the U.S. The foundation of a national airline was literally announcing Irish sovereignty on the world stage; it was a highly charged political act. I am most interested in design when it is political.
Thirdly, aviation is a huge Irish success story. Aer Lingus preceded the foundation of Alitalia (1945) and SAS (1946), for example, and has outlasted many industry leaders, including TWA, Pan Am and Sabena. Ireland has proven to be very good at airlines: we also have Ryanair, which was established in the 1980s, based on the model of Southwest Airlines, and which completely democratised European travel. Duty Free was invented at Shannon Airport in 1947. I think we have little other choice to be good at aviation as we are an island!
Finally, in 2025, I was invited by the NPM to curate an exhibition of theater posters from The Abbey and Peacock Theatres (the Irish National Theatre). This was hugely popular with both the public and design communities and I loved working with the team at the NPM. So I was actively looking for an opportunity to work with them again when I pitched the Come Fly With Me exhibition idea. I was also fortunate to receive some Research Ireland funding (the national research and innovation agency). So I guess a few disparate threads came together quite neatly.
How does travel fit into Irish design culture? Who were the leaders?
Travel and tourism are hugely significant in the story of Irish design, and creating travel ephemera had a seismic impact on Irish design development. The exhibition places particular emphasis on the arrival of a number of Dutch graphic designers to Ireland in the 1950s to specifically work on advertising for Aer Lingus. They were poached from KLM, the Dutch national airline, to fill a void in Irish design expertise. Most of them remained in Ireland and their descendants can now be found across Irish film, art and design professions.
KLM at the time was streets ahead of other European airlines in terms of having coherent and systematic advertising and publicity strategies. Aer Lingus, through its advertising agency, Sun, therefore targeted the very best design expertise available at the time.
The Dutch designers who arrived in Ireland from 1951 onwards included Guus Melai, Jan de Fouw and Willem van Velzen, who are all featured in the exhibition. With others who also came to work in Ireland, they had a huge impact on Irish design development, which had not grown organically as it had in the U.S. or Continental Europe. Ireland had been a colony of Britain, and centuries of systematic and targeted cultural erosion resulted in it having a limited industrial base by the time the Irish Free State (the 26 counties now comprising the Republic of Ireland) was established in 1922. In this environment, design professionalization and education opportunities were very limited, and farming was the main indigenous industry.
The Dutch designers were trained by Bauhaus graduates and produced really high-quality work. They became highly sought after and were commissioned by other influential Irish companies, including Bord Fáilte (the tourism board), John Hinde (the postcard manufacturer), RTÉ (the state radio and television broadcaster) and Guinness. Their work regularly featured in international design journals, including Britain’s Modern Publicity and Germany’s Gebrauchsgraphik. Some also worked as adjunct faculty in Dublin art and design schools, including IADT (the Institute of Art, Design and Technology, where I work), so they also had an impact on Irish design education. The exhibition sheds particular light on the work of Jan de Fouw, as he served on the Curatorial Board of the NPM for over 20 years, and it received a significant donation of his work from his family.
What does this show mean in the continuum of Irish graphic design?
I’ve been very taken aback (pleasantly!) by the huge support from the public and design community to this exhibition. There are few opportunities to show and celebrate Irish graphic design within Irish cultural institutions, and writing on Irish graphic design is limited. Thankfully The National Print Museum is dedicated to raising awareness of the importance of printing and print culture, and thankfully graphic design comes under this remit. Of course, people can find examples of Irish graphic design online, but that’s very different to a curated show that has a specific perspective and a desire to generate public discussion as to the “value” of design more broadly (culturally as well as economically). So I’d like to think the show provides an important marker in contributing to the history of Irish graphic design and inspiring other similar initiatives.
