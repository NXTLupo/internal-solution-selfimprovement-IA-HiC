# They Found the Identity for Calder Gardens

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/branding-identity-design/they-found-the-identity-for-calder-gardens/)

Who said Less is More? With Karlssonwilker, More Is More.
At this weird, scary moment in design history, when AI promises to do everything instantaneously (“just upload your product link and you’ll get a complete branding program in five minutes”), Karlssonwilker dares to put forth its new-old, slow way of working. A process that leads to momentous, unique solutions.
I had the pleasure of speaking with partners Jan Wilker and Vera Yuan about the extraordinary way the firm has been practicing design for the past 26 years, as exemplified by their collaboration with Calder Gardens, the Philadelphia cultural space set in lush gardens designed by Piet Oudolf. The centerpiece is a Herzog & de Meuron-designed building where permanent and rotating installations of Alexander Calder’s artworks are displayed and screenings, lectures, and performances are held.
“It’s a place for contemplation and reflection, to personally experience and interpret nature, architecture, and art,” explains Yuan, “a destination honoring a single artist with a lot to see and explore.”
Photographs by Iwan Baan © Calder Gardens. Artwork by Alexander Calder © 2026 Calder Foundation, New York / Artists Rights Society (ARS), New York
A Long and Winding Road
In the spring of 2023, the Karlssonwilker partners met the client, Alexander S.C. “Sandy” Rower, Alexander Calder’s grandson. The Calder Gardens board already had the art, the plans for the landscape and the building, and the funding. But it did not have a visual identity. With proposals from several other design firms in hand, Rower made it clear that he wanted to work with designers who would deliver something unexpected and non-commercial, something with Karlssonwilker’s “Calder-like, in-your-face disruptive attitude.”
Jan Wilker describes the resulting collaboration as a journey in which the client and designers meandered through more than 60 exploratory sketches, “looking and seeing without a specific goal other than to better understand the client.”
That’s not how most graphic designers operate. If they’re super-famous and confident, they show one thing. Many present three options, “one that’s way out there—as a much-awarded designer told me—one safe but still good, and one right in the middle.” Everyone agrees that it’s unwise to show anything they don’t want the client to buy.
That’s not the Karlssonwilker way. They show the weeds as well as the flowers. But those flowers must be damn good ones. “When the client is smarter than you, wants something unprecedented, and tells you that this is the most important project of your lifetime, the expectations are extremely high,” says Hjalti Karlsson.
Every so often a project appears where everything aligns, allowing the focus to be on the creative endeavor itself. An enchanting creative path is in front of us and slowly, step by step, we see a bit more clearly.
Jan Wilker
Nearly two years—not five minutes or five months—after that introductory meeting, it was all there: the logotype, signage and wayfinding, website, applications including shopping bag, social media icon, coffee cups, style guide. Those kinds of deliverables are pretty typical, but the way they came about was anything but. The project was complex and challenging, Yuan emphasizes, “with many voices to be listened to and absorbed.”
The documentation of the process, illustrated and with commentary, is the basis of Karlssonwilker’s 144-page, 2025 self-published book, Finding an Identity for Calder Gardens, sold in the museum shop and design-related bookstores—and soon to be commercially published and more widely available.
Finding an Identity?
The word ‘finding’ piqued my curiosity. Not ‘creating’ or ‘developing’? Was it there all along? I asked. Didn’t you create it from scratch?
Wilker has a lot to say about that. “Spending a lot of time hand-in-hand with the client is what gives results,” he says. “People are bored with things they’ve already seen. We humans need things that amuse us, surprise us, make us sit up. Taking this kind of adventure into the unknown, discovering new things, and weeding the path as we go, we are bound to stumble across the right thing. We try not to be fast and efficient. There are so many entry points. We want to expand the experience, not contract it. The quality time you spend with the client, that’s the thing. The discussions should be flowing. That way, we have co-ownership with the client.” It’s true, he admits, that not many organizations want or need that kind of process. But a glance at the Karlssonwilker website shows that the right ones do manage find each other.
In the first presentation, Rower was shown 20 approaches on 156 slides: type on blocks, type in perspective, on a diagonal, “All quite unsuccessful.” Then came big, fat letters. Letters that form a concrete bench. And more. And more. After a visit to Calder’s home and studio in Roxbury, Connecticut, 10 reworked and 11 new approaches were presented—including a hot dog cart selling Philly cheesesteaks and entry tickets to the gardens.
The designers were feeling, as Yuan puts it, “increasing pressure to deliver an identity that would resonate with the essence of Calder and with Sandy Rower.”
Aha! The 3D Illusion
Then they experimented with a type treatment in which the words ‘Calder’ and ‘Gardens’ were connected via diagonal rules. Getting somewhere. “The direction only made sense when we removed the lines connecting the words and filled the negative space,” Wilker comments. They tried out various sans-serif typefaces, settling on Söhne, described on the Kilm Type Foundry site as “the memory of Akzidenz-Grotesk framed through the reality of Helvetica.”
At a board meeting, it was presented on posters, T-shirts, and signs. “The first time Sandy saw it, there was an immediate connection,” Yuan recalls. “It has an energy that captures the energy of Calder’s work. The wordmark is created by the negative space, which was something Sandy often referred to as we delved into Calder’s work.” She notes that Rower’s most telling comment was, “It gives the illusion of push and pull between the two words. Which is in the front, which is in the back? It depends how you look at it. The relationship changes as you move around.” And it’s not green, like many garden logos. “No, it’s black and white because much of Calder’s work is black.”
What if your client doesn’t have that kind of time?
That was a question I had to ask. There are more immediate situations that call for a new identity or a brand refresh: we’re opening in six weeks, sales are down, we have a new acquisition, our product line is changing, whatever. “The process can be compressed,” Wilker insists. “Just follow a path in the forest of ideas. As long as there’s the human connection, it can be a shorter path with fewer stops.”
And it works. Yuan enumerates some of the projects they’re working on now: an innovative auction technology company; UNMAS United Nations Mine Action Service; the Atelier Calder artists’ residency in France, an international airport in India. Lots of travel. Not bad for a five-person studio based in Ridgewood, Queens, New York, where on summer days ice cream is still served to friends and neighbors. Becoming more global in scope, the partners choose to remain small, approachable, and work closely with clients, with no account executives or layers of management in between. Looking at it that way, less becomes much more.
