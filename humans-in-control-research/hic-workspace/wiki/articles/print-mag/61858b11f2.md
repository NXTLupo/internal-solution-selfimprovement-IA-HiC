# What Matters to Jayna Zweiman

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/what-matters/what-matters-to-jayna-zweiman/)

Jayna Zweiman is an architecturally trained artist and designer exploring cultural intimacy and soft power through fiber art. She is the founder of Welcome Blanket and co-founder of the Pussyhat Project, believing joy and crafting are our most powerful tools for connection.
What is the thing you like doing most in the world?
I love cloudy early mornings on Venice Beach with my family and coffee. I love to laugh so hard I cry. The moment a stranger ceases being a stranger. Twirling, which I am almost able to do again.
I love to create, and I love to create with others. And not just professionally. I love being my kid’s mother. It is the most creative, joyful, stretching, relentless, and value-sorting experience I’ve ever had, and I get to do it with my husband.
What is the first memory you have of being creative?
I don’t remember a time when I wasn’t creating, but there is a clear before and after moment: when I held my father’s Nikon SLR (finally!) for the first time. It was probably the summer before I turned five. I was in our backyard. I held the camera, wondering what magic was inside. I felt its weight and looked through the viewfinder. I realized I could make something new that was my point of view. It was that connection between shared reality and my constructed dream in a frame. I now had the potential to communicate my interior world to those around me.
What is your biggest regret?
My biggest regrets are thinking there is more time with people I love and not sending the letters I write.
I am both a masterful ruminator and a recovering perfectionist, a spectacular combination that has left room for a lifelong discipline of self-compassion. Luckily, I mess up and fail with frequency, so I have so many opportunities to grow.
I chose to metabolize my regrets and failures into future wisdom. It leaves so much more room for joy and rest.
How have you gotten over heartbreak?
Of the romantic variety? Feeling it to my core. So many sad songs. Dancing it out. Talking it out. I have very patient friends. Growing from it.
Of the friendship variety? That is its own kind of heartbreak. I own my part, feel it in my gut, and learn from it.
My heart broke when my body broke. I felt alone and isolated because how I interacted with the world dramatically changed. I took my own freedom and health for granted. I had to open myself up to recognize that in my loneliness, I wasn’t alone. I recognized the incredible community nodes of local yarn stores and felt that belonging. I literally had to make a global project to get over myself!
My heartbreak for our country is a bit different. A relationship between two people is very different from a relationship to a culture and system. We collectively make it and we can collectively change it; I try to transfer my outrage into joyous collaborative action- to connect. Unlike the end of a relationship with a person, this is a structure and a culture. It requires relentless optimism.
What makes you cry?
The extremes of cruelty, kindness, beauty, and vulnerability.
When my son says something profound or designs full worlds to inhabit.
Every time I hear Tracy Chapman sing “Fast Car.”
How long does the pride and joy of accomplishing something last for you?
The pride of accomplishment is usually a few minutes and, tops, a week. I find more joy in the actual doing of things. That said, I know when there are finish line moments or official recognition, they help me continue to do the work I love.
What lasts significantly longer, and acts as fuel, is doing work that aligns with crafting the world I want to see — and doing so with others. Any time someone shares their Pussyhat or Welcome Blanket story, I feel more in the world and connected with another soul.
When I see people connect with one another through my projects, my heart soars. It makes me feel like the projects are doing what they are designed to do, and I feel like an excellent designer for a day.
I’ve designed structures for that to happen again and again. I think that’s why I love working on Welcome Blanket almost a decade later.
Do you believe in an afterlife, and if so, what does that look like to you?
I don’t know what comes next. I live in this life as if it is the only chance I have to leave what’s around me better than how I found it. I hope there is an afterlife because there are people I would love to reunite with. And hopefully, there would also be chocolate, salty air, and endless art supplies.
What do you hate most about yourself?
My body is incredibly slow to heal. I had a life-altering head and neck injury in 2013 (and then another in 2022). I am still rebuilding myself.
I am frustrated by the gap between what I think I should be able to do and what I am able to do. I wrestle with a slow learning of self-acceptance, but in raising my child, I am also learning to raise myself.
What do you love most about yourself?
I deeply care and love. I can find joy almost anywhere. I’m a good friend.
I figured out how to turn my isolation and pain into belonging for others beyond myself.
What is your absolute favorite meal?
A long lingering meal with friends and family. Usually, it’s one where there are at least two spills. If there is something grilled, like a branzino at Bacetti, or a gigantic stew with a serious ladle, and a chocolate dessert to stick around for, even better.
