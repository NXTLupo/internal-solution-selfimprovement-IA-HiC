# Egon Van Herreweghe Reveals the Artistry of Storefront Awnings in His Show ‘The Menu’

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/design-news/egon-van-herreweghe-the-menu/)

Consider the storefront awning. Perhaps you never really have before, because, well, why would you? Storefront awnings are the sort of mundane structures that populate our day-to-day lives, serving a clear utility function and don’t necessarily elicit much more thought. And yet, multidisciplinary artist Egon Van Herreweghe has given them a whole lot of thought, recently opening a show of storefront awning sculptures at Footnote Gallery in Ghent, Belgium entitled The Menu.
The show composition is simple: a white room with four sculptures of storefront awning reproductions installed on the walls. Each sculpture represents a different type of restaurant from a different culture, envisioned with the archetypical visual conventions of each place. The four places Van Herreweghe decided to depict in the show are Greece, China, Italy, and France, since each of these countries have distinct, highly recognizable visual languages that Van Herreweghe wanted to explore.
In his artist statement he writes: “My work engages with the visual and material culture of commerce. Working across sculpture, photography, and installation, I appropriate the forms, materials and display strategies of retail environments, treating them as structures through which value and meaning are produced. Through processes of reproduction, fabrication and displacement, familiar objects become subtly estranged from their original function. Positioned between replica and original, commodity and artwork, they expose the conventions that govern perception and authenticity. Rather than offering representations of everyday life, the work reflects on the infrastructures through which images and objects circulate, acquire significance and become desirable.”
In this way, Van Herreweghe reveals the art in our world that’s hidden in plain sight. By re-contextualizing the commonplace signage of storefront awnings in a gallery space, suddenly these structures become something entirely different, meaningful, and worth our attention. Van Herreweghe unpacks The Menu below.
What is it about storefront awnings that you find so captivating? What about them compelled you to reproduce them in this way for The Menu?
What interests me about awnings is that they sit between architecture, advertising and decoration, and they tell you something about a place before you have even entered it. They project into the street and try to attract you, but they also create an expectation of what you will find behind them.
I see them as postcards. A postcard doesn’t give you the actual place; it gives you an image of the place that is meant to make you want to go there. I think awnings can work in a similar way. They use a very limited visual language, but somehow we immediately understand what kind of experience they are promising.
By presenting them in a gallery, I wanted to highlight this sculptural and visual quality.
What sort of commentary are you making with this collection of storefront canopies?
I’m interested in how easily we construct ideas of other places and cultures from very small visual cues. We don’t necessarily need to travel to imagine somewhere else. A certain color combination, a typeface, an ornament, or a particular shape can already produce an idea of Greece, China, Italy, or Paris.
So I was deliberately looking for clichés. Not because I think these clichés are accurate representations of those cultures, but because they are so recognizable. They show how culture can be condensed into a few signs and then turned into something that can be consumed.
How did you decide on these four specific storefront canopies? Why was it important to you to depict four different food cultures in the show?
I started by looking for the awnings that were most deeply ingrained in my own mind. I was quite sure about the bistro because of its particular form, and the Greek awning interested me because of its inverted scalloped edge. The gelateria felt like a very standard type of awning, and there was also something nice about having an ice cream awning in the summer.
I really like the way the Chinese awnings I was looking at often put much more useful information right on the front: a phone number, “take out,” “free delivery,” opening hours, and so on. With the other awnings, you might mainly get the name of the restaurant, whereas these are much more explicit about their communicative and commercial function.
So the four are not meant to represent four cultures in any comprehensive way. They are four very recognizable visual clichés that I happened to have in my own visual memory, selected partly because of what they communicate and partly because of the differences in their shapes and visual languages.
How do the canopies function individually as standalone pieces, and how do they then work as a collection?
Individually, each awning works almost like a small promise. It gives you enough information to construct a place in your head, even though you have never been there. Each one has its own vocabulary and its own particular version of “elsewhere.”
Together, they start to look more like the kind of restaurant street you find in many cities. You can move from one cultural image to another, and the differences between them become more apparent. At the same time, you start to notice how similar the mechanism is: each one uses a few familiar signs to create an expectation.
I also like the fact that, once they are on the gallery wall, they no longer really function as awnings. They are suspended somewhere between an architectural object and an image. Their practical function has disappeared, and what remains is their capacity to signify.
What was your research process like for designing each canopy to make sure each was accurate and authentic?
I wouldn’t say that I was trying to make them authentically representative of those cultures. In fact, the opposite is probably closer to what I was interested in. I was looking for recognizable and believable clichés.
A lot of the research was done through photographs. I looked at many examples and tried to identify the visual elements that kept returning—particular shapes, colors, lettering, decorative details and, in some cases, the kind of information that was placed on the awning.
In that sense, the photographs became almost like source material for the sculptures. The awnings are a kind of proto-photography: they are already images that have been designed to make you imagine something. They are like postcards trying to make you long for a place before you have experienced it firsthand.
What was your process like for designing and fabricating the canopies? What materials did you use?
The process was simple in principle, but slow in practice. I started with some research and used photographs as references. I made a selection from these and then made watercolors of the four that made the final selection. I used these to decide on the proportions and dimensions of each awning.
A welder made the metal frames based on my instructions. I then sourced the fabrics, after which a seamstress constructed the awnings. Finally, my partner painted the selected texts onto the fabric using textile paint.
It’s quite a long process because some of the paints didn’t cover the darker fabrics well enough and needed several layers. With the sewing, there was no existing pattern to work from, so every mistake could mean having to start over.
I find it interesting that the final objects look quite simple. They have a very familiar, almost disposable appearance, but there is actually a lot of time and labor hidden in them.
What sort of experience do you hope viewers of The Menu have when they attend the show?
Ideally, there is a moment where the awnings feel familiar, but also slightly strange because they have been taken out of the environment where they normally make sense. I also hope the exhibition makes people think about how much information we project onto very simple images. We see a certain color or shape and immediately construct a place, a culture, or an expectation around it.
And perhaps the title “The Menu” is the simplest way of describing the experience I want to create. There is something about looking at these objects that is similar to looking at a menu: you are presented with possibilities, you imagine an experience, and desire starts before consumption. In this case, the things being offered aren’t dishes, but images of elsewhere.
