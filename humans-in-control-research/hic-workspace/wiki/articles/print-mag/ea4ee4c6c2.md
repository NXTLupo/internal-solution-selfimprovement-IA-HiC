# The Historic ALEX Theatre Restores Its Cinematic Roots for ‘The Odyssey’

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/design-news/the-historic-alex-theater-restores-its-cinematic-roots-for-the-odyssey/)

The way we commune to watch movies has drastically changed over the last decade. The streaming industry has flipped the media viewing experience on its head, promoting solitary watching on small, personal screens from the privacy of our homes, versus the collective, community-based gathering to watch that used to bring us together. But while this general shift is undeniable, as an all-things-retro-and-analog-loving “old soul” who lives in Los Angeles, I’m noticing a promising move back to the movie-going glory days. The recent release of Christopher Nolan’s summer blockbuster hit The Odyssey is cold hard proof of that.
The Odyssey is hitting whopping numbers at the box office just three weeks into its release, already reaching a worldwide gross of over $900 million and just under $400 million domestically. And this is all happening at—that’s right—movie theaters. These numbers show that the modern movie watcher still has a thirst for the grandeur of going to the cinema; cinemas like Glendale, CA’s veritable movie palace, The ALEX.
I’ve been driving past The ALEX since I first moved to LA a decade ago, but had never interacted with the theater beyond ogling its glorious marquee from the street, out of which a large neon obelisk sprouts upward into the city sky. This is because up until The Odyssey release this past July, The ALEX hadn’t shown a first-run feature in 35 years—its last one prior was Terminator 2 in 1991. Originally built in 1925, The ALEX thrived throughout the 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, and 80s, but despite its unmissable gravitas and splendor, it fell on hard times in the 90s due to mismanagement and deferred maintenance.
“Old buildings like this, theaters in particular, there’s so many things that need fixed all the time, and without the funding and an operator that’s committed to the maintenance and upgrades, theaters fall into disrepair a lot, more so than other types of properties. That’s what happened here,” Artistic Director of The ALEX, Miles Williams, tells me as we chat at the onsite bar, the dulcet sounds of The Odyssey rising and falling from the adjacent theater. “The moment we’re having now is a reclamation of our original identity,” he says, and there’s no better person to lead this revival than him. He has over 35 years working in event management, and has overseen the restoration and management of four historic theaters in the LA area—the Orpheum (Downtown), the United Theater at the Ace Hotel (Downtown), the Saban Theater (Beverly Hills), and now The ALEX. Williams is something of an old theater revival whisperer; when a theater needs saving, Miles Williams is your guy. But when the city of Glendale first reached out to Williams to take on The ALEX, he was hesitant.
When I arrived it was a rescue mission, which is right up my alley.
“I went back and forth as to whether I wanted to take on the project,” he shares. “I had all this success with these other theaters that I gave birth to or was part of, but this one in Glendale… Nobody thinks Glendale’s cool! It’s maybe where you go Christmas shopping at the mall. So to bring the ‘downtown cool,’ which I was from, into this place, I was like ‘Huh, I don’t know if I can do it.’ I didn’t want to do it if it wasn’t going to be successful.” But after negotiating with the city and inspiring them of the possibilities if they committed to the upgrades and upkeep needed to the space and the equipment, they struck a deal. Ultimately, Williams just couldn’t pass it up. “When I arrived it was a rescue mission, which is right up my alley,” he says with a sly grin.
Williams took the helm at The ALEX after the pandemic, originally focusing on live event entertainment but setting his sights on restoring the venue to its cinematic roots. “I had my eyes on this cinema revival over the last few years as real film cinema has become very important, and there’s a whole community that supports it.” He tells me when he first took over, a common sentiment he heard from the public was, “I used to go to movies there when I was a kid.” He realized that this history was powerful, and that The ALEX holds many memories for people throughout LA County. “I thought, this is the real identity of this building.” After the first wave of renovations Williams oversaw, it was time to get The ALEX back to showing movies. “That’s its natural state,” he says.
Williams had the idea to use The ALEX’s 100th anniversary celebration last year as the perfect moment to reintroduce it as a movie theater. He produced a series to re-open the door to that “cinema identity” called, “A Century of Cinema,” where they showed over 40 films from the 1920s through the present day. But these were all shown in digital, and Williams knew that, sooner or later, they’d have to get to film projection—to really give the cinephiles of LA what they want, and show movies the way they’re meant to be seen. He began putting together budgets and plans, which involved a new sound system, new acoustic treatments, and, of course, a new booth for the film projectors.
Williams reached out to Taylor Umphenour, a leading IMAX 70mm film projectionist, about getting The ALEX up to 70mm-film snuff. Umphenour said the next big movie release was going to be The Odyssey; “I think we could maybe barely get it done in time,” he told Williams. Taylor wrangled contacts of his at Universal to see if they’d give The ALEX a print of the film to show on 70mm, and they agreed to it. It was go time.
The ALEX’s Grecian architecture makes it the perfect venue for The Odyssey, and, conversely, makes The Odyssey the perfect film to usher The ALEX back into showing first-run features. “The fullness of time had come!” says Williams on the serendipity of these stars aligning. “It was a perfect film for us to do as our ‘coming out’ again.” But the timing was going to be tough to get the projection booth done in time. “It was very last minute because Universal gave us the green light five weeks before the opening,” says Williams. “It worked out, barely; we all kind of lost our minds a little. It was 12 of us, working 80 hours a week for three weeks. No days off. It was that kind of a project.”
I was really young when I first realized that being in the same space with a bunch of people who are watching something that they love and I love connects us in a way that nothing else can.
Of course, Williams had to incorporate something unique to make The ALEX’s booth extra special. “I had the idea of putting picture windows in—I don’t think anyone else has that,” he says proudly. “Most of these rooms are way up at the top of the theater, but for this, the idea came to us to put it downstairs. I only had to lose two rows of seats—27 seats—that’s not that bad. And I put the picture windows in because that’s part of the attraction. The cinephiles of LA, they want real film, and you can walk in and there it is, real film—they can see it in a way they never get to see it.” Decisions like this are all part of Williams’ efforts to elevate the patron experience at The ALEX. “That’s always what I’ve been about,” he says. “I was really young when I first realized that being in the same space with a bunch of people who are watching something that they love and I love connects us in a way that nothing else can. The joy and mission of my life has been to provide that for audiences.”
With The Odyssey’s success at The ALEX as a springboard, Williams is eager to keep pushing the theater to greater and greater heights. This includes exclusively showing movies on film, even 35mm. “We’re definitely working towards being always on film, because that’s what the community wants, and we value that. We want to be a part of that community,” he says. “My perspective on keeping The ALEX alive and vibrant and ready to continue another hundred years is offering something that people want to be here for. The Odyssey has broken us in because we’re seeing the coolest people, the coolest crowds from all over. People from Silver Lake are here! The cool people showed up, and that’s a great thing! Gathering spaces matter, and this kind of place is exactly that, and community does grow from it. I want to nurture that, too.”
The Odyssey might be the main attraction putting people in The ALEX’s seats, but its the overall movie-going experience these patrons are having once there that will keep them coming back. “Obviously Nolan and his gang get all of the credit for the success of this movie and the fact that we’ve sold so many tickets and that we’re full, but I think when people come here, there’s an elevated experience,” says Williams. “Not only because of the architecture and my incredible staff that’s so welcoming and professional, but it’s the history in the walls. It’s palpable, even if it’s not something we can see or hear. And I look forward to providing that experience for the next generation as we look at our 101st year. Let’s just keep going!”
