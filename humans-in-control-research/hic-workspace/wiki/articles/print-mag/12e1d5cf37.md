# The Daily Heller: It Was Great Working With You, Steven Brower

Source: [print_mag](https://www.printmag.com/daily-heller/the-daily-heller-it-was-great-working-with-you-steven-brower/)

Last Friday, I decided on the spur of the moment that The Daily Heller post was going to be a reprise of an earlier published notice on Steven Brower’s last book, Roy Kuhlman: The Reluctant Modernist. I wanted to make certain it did not become yet another remainder. So, after it ran, I was expecting to hear something from Steve about the surprise posting. Instead I was shocked and saddened to learn that he died on the day following the post. He was 74 years old.
Brower was an extraordinary builder of graphic design, comics and pop culture history. I happily worked with him on two of his books, writing the introductions for his Breathless Homicidal Slime Mutants, which chronicles mass-market paperback cover art, and the Kuhlman monograph, based on the designer’s family-held archive. Brower was creative director of PRINT when it was a printed magazine, giving it a fresh vibrant new design, and he contributed writing to the current iteration of this website. He also edited illustration for The Nation and designed many book jackets. In fact, I was surprised by how many interests we shared, from caricature and cartoon to typography history and editorial art direction. It is hard for me to find where we did not overlap.
He was director of the low-residency MFA program at Marywood University, where he avidly taught graphic design and illustration. His greatest strength was as editor and curator of American cultural highlights. He authored and co-authored several legacy books, including Satchmo: The Wonderful World and Art of Louis Armstrong and Woody Guthrie Artworks (with Nora Guthrie). His favored topic was the history and practice of comics, with titles that included Fatal Femme Fatales: Women in Crime Golden Age Comics and Astounding, Mysterious, Weird and True: The Pulp Art of Comic Book Artists, among a dozen anthologies. With Seymour Chwast, he recently authored The Mighty Elvis: A Graphic Biography, and Charlie: Charles Chaplin, The Funniest Man in the World. I knew him for around three decades, and as a testament to his modesty, I had no idea how deep his bibliography was.
Talking with Seymour Chwast last night about the sad news, Brower’s many accomplishments and his decided humility, I was surprised to learn that Seymour did not know that Milton Glaser was Brower’s first cousin once removed. It wasn’t a secret, but it wasn’t pinned to his chest like some kind of award; Brower was not boastful, but he was purposeful. He filled his working life with the themes that mattered the most to him—forgotten comics artists, discarded biographical artifacts and expanding the reach of history into realms that were deemed ephemeral and disposable. The academic and professional worlds should be grateful for these notable contributions.
Fortunately, the PRINT Book Club was lucky enough to have him on a recent segment (which can be seen here). We have a fine record of his words and works, but his person will be inconsolably missed.
