# 2026 Hiring Trends Report: AI, Ghost Jobs, & Getting Hired

Source: [resume_genius_blog](https://resumegenius.com/blog/job-hunting/hiring-trends-report-2026)

Hiring in 2026 is more automated, less transparent, and increasingly difficult for job seekers to navigate.
AI now shapes everything from job descriptions and resume screening to interviews, while ghost jobs, ghosting candidates, and misleading job listings continue to make it difficult to tell whether an open position actually means there’s an active hiring process.
To understand what these changes mean for candidates, Resume Genius surveyed 1,500 U.S. hiring managers for our 2026 Hiring Trends Report. The findings reveal how employers are using AI, why qualified applicants get passed over, which behaviors raise red flags, and what still gives candidates an advantage in a rapidly changing job market.
- 87% of hiring managers say their company has implemented AI in at least one part of the recruitment process, up from 82% in 2025.
- 58% now use AI to screen resumes and applications, compared with 35% in 2025.
- 90% believe companies intentionally post job listings they don’t plan to fill immediately.
- 64% admit their company has misrepresented some aspect of a role or the hiring process to candidates.
- 53% have noticed an increase in applicants over age 50, with only 48% saying that age never affects a candidate’s hireability.
- 79% say a candidate’s enthusiasm and positive attitude make them more hirable, showing that human qualities still carry significant weight in an increasingly automated process.
AI in the 2026 hiring process
AI use has become standard recruiting practice, and it is expanding most quickly in high-stakes stages such as resume screening. However, employers have been slower to embrace transparency, even as they raise concerns about candidate AI use.
AI adoption is accelerating, especially in resume screening
AI use in hiring is now nearly universal: 87% of hiring managers say their company has implemented it in at least one part of recruitment, up from 82% in 2025. The biggest jump in AI usage was in resume screening, which rose from 35% to 58% — the largest increase among the directly comparable use cases.
- The share of hiring managers using AI to write job descriptions has also increased substantially, from 31% in 2025 to 46% in 2026.
- By contrast, AI-driven candidate outreach and communication stayed flat at roughly 27%, suggesting that adoption is concentrating more heavily in evaluation than relationship building.
The AI generation gap is wide and consistent
The generational divide is clearest in how deeply AI is embedded in hiring decisions. Resume screening is the top use case across every age group, but adoption ranges from 68% of Gen Z hiring managers to 45% of Baby Boomers.
- The gap is starkest in AI-assisted video interviews, used by 40% of Gen Z hiring managers and 25% of Millennials, compared with 12% of Gen X and 10% of Baby Boomers.
- Older hiring managers are also far more likely to opt out entirely: 20% of Baby Boomers and 17% of Gen X use no AI in recruitment, compared with 7% of Millennials and 3% of Gen Z.
- Millennials lead the use of AI for skills matching and job-description writing, at 51% for each.
Candidates are using AI, too — and hiring managers have noticed
Candidate AI use is now routine from the employer’s perspective: 81% of hiring managers have encountered it during recruitment, most often in resumes and cover letters (58%). At the same time, 82% are concerned about candidates using AI in their applications.
- Deepfake use remains less common but is no longer marginal: 17% of hiring managers report encountering it during a video interview.
- A detection gap may be hiding in the data. One-third of Baby Boomers (33%) and 24% of Gen X say they have never noticed candidate AI use, compared with 11% of Millennials — raising the question of whether older managers encounter less AI or simply detect it less often.
The disclosure gap
Adoption has outpaced transparency: only 35% of hiring managers say their company always discloses when AI is used to evaluate candidates, while 20% say AI is used without any disclosure.
- Disclosure also varies by age: 43% of Gen Z hiring managers say their company always discloses AI use, followed by 38% of Millennials and 33% of both Gen X and Baby Boomers.
- A further 19% say their company does not use AI in interviews or candidate evaluation in the first place.
Ghost jobs and process honesty
Trust problems extend beyond AI. Nine in 10 hiring managers accept that companies post roles they do not plan to fill immediately, nearly two-thirds admit their company has misrepresented part of a role or hiring process, and reported reasons for ghosting candidates have grown since 2024.
Ghost jobs are an open secret
Ghost jobs are widely understood as a deliberate pipeline-building tool. Ninety percent of hiring managers did not reject the premise that companies intentionally post roles they do not plan to fill immediately, and 47% identify building a pool of candidates for future openings as the leading motive.
- Another 34% say ghost jobs are used to signal growth or stability to investors, clients, or competitors.
- Nearly a third (31%) point to internal HR policies requiring a role to be advertised externally before an internal candidate is appointed.
Talent-first hiring is going mainstream
More than half of hiring managers (52%) say their company is already using or seriously considering talent-first hiring, where people are recruited before a specific role is defined. More than a quarter (28%) have used the approach and plan to continue.
- Millennial hiring managers are driving adoption: 39% say their company actively uses talent-first hiring, compared with 21% of Gen X.
- The traditional application funnel is not the only route into a role: 39% have hired someone who made contact through an unconventional channel, such as a LinkedIn message or walk-in.
Employers mislead candidates, and younger hiring managers admit it more readily
Nearly two-thirds of hiring managers (64%) admit their company has misrepresented some aspect of a role or the hiring process. The most common examples are understating how difficult a role or team environment is (32%) and giving candidates an inaccurate hiring timeline (29%).
- Younger hiring managers are much more likely to acknowledge the behavior. More than half of Gen Z hiring managers (51%) admit their company has understated a role’s difficulty, compared with 16% of Baby Boomers; 52% of Baby Boomers say their company has done nothing dishonest, versus 20% of Gen Z.
- In 2024, 65% of hiring managers admitted exaggerating a job’s benefits or career opportunities at least once.
Candidate ghosting is becoming more widespread
Candidate ghosting appears to be driven less by individual applicants than by ineffective or overloaded hiring processes. Indecision and excessive applicant volume are tied as the leading reasons: 86% of hiring managers report ghosting candidates for each, up from 81% and 79%, respectively in 2024.
- Salary expectations beyond the available budget have led 82% of hiring managers to ghost a candidate, up from 76% in 2024.
- Communication problems are accompanied by poor hiring outcomes: 83% report a hiring regret from the past year, most commonly choosing someone who looked like a good candidate on paper but underperformed in the role (45%).
Age, overqualification, and generational dynamics
Hiring managers are seeing more candidates over 50, but a slight majority still say age affects hireability at some point. Similar assumptions shape how employers evaluate overqualified candidates, while perceptions of Gen Z remain consistently negative across every generation.
Older applicants are increasing, and age still quietly matters
More than half of hiring managers (53%) have noticed an increase in applicants over age 50 during the past year. Yet only 48% say age never affects hireability; the remaining 52% believe it becomes a factor at some point in a candidate’s career.
- Age most commonly becomes a factor later in a career: 20% identify age 60 or above as the point when it matters, while 16% place the threshold between ages 50 and 59.
Overqualified candidates are passed over for predictable reasons
Budget and retention concerns dominate how employers assess overqualified candidates. Nearly half of hiring managers (47%) worry their salary expectations will exceed the role’s budget, while 43% fear they will leave when a better opportunity appears.
- More than a third (36%) have passed on an overqualified candidate because they expected the person to be difficult to manage or unwilling to take direction.
- Only 20% say they evaluate overqualified applicants in exactly the same way as other candidates.
Every generation agrees: Gen Z is the most challenging to work with
Nearly half of hiring managers (49%) name Gen Z the most challenging generation to work with, up from 45% in 2024. The finding is unusually consistent: Gen Z ranks first among hiring managers from every generation, including Gen Z itself.
- Gen X and Baby Boomer hiring managers are the harshest critics, with 52% of each group choosing Gen Z, followed by 49% of Gen Z hiring managers and 44% of Millennials.
- Millennials rank a distant second overall at 20%, although Millennial hiring managers are especially critical of their own cohort (25%). Gen X is the least criticized generation, selected by just 7% of respondents.
Evaluation criteria, red flags, and degree value
When deciding whom to hire, managers consistently favor attitude and fit over credentials alone. That preference also shapes how they interpret employment gaps, AI-generated applications, and the value of advanced degrees.
Attitude beats qualifications
