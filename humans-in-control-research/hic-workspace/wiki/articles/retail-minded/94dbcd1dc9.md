# A Small Retailer’s Practical Guide to Getting Started with Digital Signage

Source: [retail_minded](https://retailminded.com/a-small-retailers-practical-guide-to-getting-started-with-digital-signage/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=a-small-retailers-practical-guide-to-getting-started-with-digital-signage)

A Small Retailer’s Practical Guide to Getting Started with Digital Signage
Digital signage used to be the preserve of big chains with big budgets. That has changed. The software runs in the cloud, the screens are ordinary commercial displays, and an independent store can now run the same kind of setup a national retailer uses, at a scale that fits one location. The US market alone is projected to grow from $7.44 billion in 2025 to $12.97 billion by 2033, according to Grand View Research, and a growing share of that is smaller businesses getting in.
For an independent, the appeal is straightforward. A single screen in the window or above the counter replaces the endless cycle of printing, laminating and taping up new signs, and it can be changed in seconds from a laptop. The question for most small retailers is not whether it works. It is how to start without overspending or overcomplicating it.
Start with one screen and one job
The mistake that wastes money is buying screens before deciding what they are for. Start with a single screen and one clear purpose. A window display that pulls passersby in is a good first job, because it works on people who have not yet decided to come inside. So is a screen above the counter that promotes an add-on while a customer is already paying.
Pick the one spot where a message would change a customer’s behavior, and put a screen there. One screen doing a defined job beats five screens showing wallpaper, and it lets you learn what works before spending more.
The setup is simpler than it looks
A digital signage setup has three parts: a commercial-grade screen, a small media player that drives it, and the software that creates and manages the content. The screen is the part most people overthink. A commercial display is built to run all day without failing, which a home television is not, but beyond that the choice is about size and brightness for the spot it will sit in.
The part that does the real work is the software. The team at Mandoe Media, which builds a digital signage platform for exactly this kind of business, told us “the screen is the part people overthink. The software is what decides whether a small shop keeps its content fresh, because drag-and-drop editing and remote updates are what let a one-person business change the screen without being stuck behind the counter to do it.” Content can be built, scheduled to change through the day, and updated from anywhere, so the store is never tied to one location to keep the screen current. Modern tools include template libraries and design assets, which means a small retailer without a designer can still produce something that looks professional.
Content is what makes it pay
A screen is only as good as what plays on it. The independents who get a return keep the content specific and current: today’s special, this week’s promotion, the product that pairs with the one a customer is looking at. The ones who do not treat the screen like a billboard, load it with a logo, and leave it running for a month.
Keep it about the customer’s next decision. Schedule it around the trading day, so a breakfast message becomes a lunch message on its own. Change it often enough that regulars keep noticing. None of that takes long once the content is built, because the whole point of cloud software is that a change made once appears on the screen straight away.
Judging whether it is working
The advantage a small retailer has is that results are easy to see. Put a promotion on the window screen and watch whether more people come in. Push an add-on above the counter and watch whether the average sale moves. Because the content changes in seconds, a small store can test a message, see the effect, and adjust, in a way a print run never allowed.
That feedback loop is what turns a screen from an expense into a tool. Start with one, give it a job, keep the content sharp, and let the results tell you where the next one should go. Digital signage rewards the retailers who treat it as part of how they sell, and it no longer asks for the budget of a chain to do it.
The wider market backs the small-store case. Mordor Intelligence notes that cloud software is the fastest-growing part of retail digital signage, which is exactly the part that made screens affordable for independents in the first place. The hardware was never really the barrier. What kept small retailers out was the cost and complexity of managing content, and that is the piece the cloud has now made cheap and simple. An owner-operator with a laptop and one screen can run the kind of setup that used to need an agency, which is why the technology has stopped being a big-chain privilege and started showing up in independent windows. For an independent deciding where to spend a limited budget, that shift is the whole opportunity.
