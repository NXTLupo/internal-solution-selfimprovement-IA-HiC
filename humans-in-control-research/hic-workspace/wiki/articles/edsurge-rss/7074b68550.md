# Translating Dignity, Not Just Language

Source: [EdSurge (RSS)](https://edsurge.com/news/translating-dignity-not-just-language)

Translating Dignity, Not Just Language
Automated platforms can translate messages in seconds, but as a school principal, I've learned that true belonging requires a human connection technology can't replicate.
By Monika Vereb
July 29, 2026
Credit: Point Normal / Unsplash
One evening, while supporting a family after hours, I asked a mother whether she felt more comfortable speaking with me in Spanish or English. My bilingual front office staff had already left for the evening, and I knew this family would likely need direct support navigating a series of complex digital enrollment forms and communication systems before they left.
Although I retained conversational Spanish from years of academic coursework and lived experience, my own speech in the language remains slow and hesitant. I wanted to ensure they felt entirely supported by the school from day one. Yet, before the mother could fully respond, her older son quickly interrupted to clarify that his mother speaks English.
I immediately sensed from his distinct defensiveness that my question had impacted him in a way I had not intended. In his expression, I recognized a familiar, painful look: the exhaustion that comes from feeling judged, categorized, or misunderstood before you have even had the opportunity to introduce yourself.
Later, when we sat down together to work through the school division’s digital portal, I could not let the interaction pass without gently asking about his reaction. The student explained that he was deeply frustrated by the constant assumption that his mother did not speak or understand English. I validated his feelings and clarified that I had asked which language his mother preferred for navigating dense administrative jargon, not which language she was capable of speaking.
Then I shared something with him that I rarely say out loud: As a second-generation Chicana American, I sometimes carry a persistent internal guilt about not feeling “Latina enough” because I did not grow up speaking Spanish with absolute fluency. That tension has followed me throughout my life. Long before I became a principal, it deeply shaped how I understood the concept of belonging: who feels seen, who feels understood, and who feels as though they must constantly defend or explain themselves.
Years later, those exact same questions have resurfaced in my work with multilingual students and families navigating school systems that are not always designed with their lived experiences in mind.
Now serving as a Latina principal in a predominantly Spanish-speaking school community, I find myself translating far more than literal language. Every day, I bridge the gap between human families and the digital frameworks of modern education. I help families navigate educational jargon, intricate online platforms, and digital communication that can easily feel overwhelming without direct, personalized support. Increasingly, that work means helping families slow down and process information that often arrives on their smartphones much faster than genuine, trusting relationships can be built.
I have come to realize that the true promise of educational technology is not simply faster communication, but communication that helps families feel confident, capable, and connected to their children’s school.
Technology Drives Alienation
As school districts rapidly adopt automated communication platforms, mass text alerts, and AI-driven translation tools, we face a critical intersection. Technology is often championed as the ultimate equalizer for equity and access. Yet when implemented without a human-centered lens, these digital tools risk automating the very sense of alienation that multilingual families already experience.
The fundamental question we must ask ourselves as educational leaders is simple yet profound: How do we ensure that people feel genuinely understood, rather than simply informed? For me, this question has become less about whether schools should embrace technology and more about how leaders intentionally shape technology to strengthen relationships instead of replacing them.
Clarity is Key
Recently, I participated in an illuminating listening session with our multilingual families focused specifically on communication, digital access, and school engagement. What struck me most was that families were not asking the school to communicate more frequently. Instead, they were asking the school to communicate more clearly. Parents spoke candidly about wanting critical information delivered in accessible formats, practical support navigating digital opportunities, and flexible ways to participate when demanding work schedules, lack of transportation, or unfamiliar digital systems created barriers.
That listening session fundamentally shifted my own thinking as a school leader. I realized that successful technology implementation cannot be measured solely by how efficiently information is delivered, but by whether families feel empowered to act on that information and engage confidently with their school community.

Many of the solutions raised during our conversation directly involved technology. One parent shared how she used the translated messaging feature through our school’s communication platform to ask specific questions about an advanced academic program — an opportunity she had previously not felt comfortable pursuing due to the language barrier. That interaction was a beautiful reminder of technology’s genuine potential to expand access, democratize information, and open doors that were previously closed.
However, beneath every parent’s comment in that session was a deeper, more urgent request. Families did not just want error-free translated messages; they wanted communication that felt fundamentally empathetic and understandable. They wanted real people they could trust to help them co-navigate increasingly complex, digitized systems. The technology opened the door, but relationships gave families the confidence to walk through it.
The Promise of AI
The deeper I listen to multilingual families, the more I realize that truly accessible communication requires far more than literal, word-for-word translation. Translation software often strips away cultural nuances, regional dialects, idioms, and emotional tone. Many of the families I serve also communicate through rich oral traditions and lived experiences that cannot always be captured through written text alone. This is where I see tremendous potential for emerging educational technologies and AI-supported communication tools. Forward-thinking schools have a unique opportunity to move beyond static, text-based translation and toward AI-generated audio and video communication.
Imagine a system where a parent receives a short, personalized video in their native dialect explaining a school policy, or an interactive audio guide that walks them through a complex enrollment form. These tools could remove barriers not only by translating words, but by preserving voice, emotion, and cultural context. In doing so, technology becomes less transactional and more relational.
As Christopher Emdin powerfully reminds us through reality pedagogy, authentic relationships must always come before trust. Technology should be used to help us translate deep human understanding, not simply literal language. The teenage student who fiercely protected his mother from my question wasn’t just reacting to an inquiry about language preferences; he was defending her dignity against an educational system that too often feels cold, rigid, and transactional. His reaction was not ultimately about whether his mother spoke English. It was about her identity, her absolute right to belonging, and the profound exhaustion that comes from constantly feeling misunderstood by authority figures.
As schools continue to aggressively adopt new technologies, automated platforms, and digital communication tools to streamline their daily operations, we must remember that the ultimate goal of education is not simply the efficient dissemination of data.
Culturally responsive leadership in the digital age requires us to practice vulnerability, intentionally build human checkpoints around our software, and foster deep connection long before our technology can ever do its job effectively.
As educational leaders, our responsibility is not simply to implement new technology but to ensure that every digital innovation reflects the values of empathy, dignity, and belonging. Technology can expand access, but authentic understanding, mutual trust, and true belonging will always be built through the irreplaceable power of human connection.
