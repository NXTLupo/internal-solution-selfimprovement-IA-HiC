# Fandom is becoming too big for brands to ignore

Source: [inside_retail_asia](https://insideretail.asia/2026/08/24/fandom-is-becoming-too-big-for-brands-to-ignore/)

On any given night, I’m curled up in bed with my phone in one hand and a book I only heard about three days ago in the other, ugly-crying over a final chapter where a great war sacrifices the lives of many for a greater world. How did I get here? Hours of scrolling recommendations, each one leading me to this moment. As a marketer, I’m acutely aware that I was influenced. Fandom got me, and if you look around, it’s everywhere. Defined as an active community of fans who share a deep passion    
assion, fandoms drive culture and commerce, but are largely underutilised by brands.
There’s a formula that marketers have leaned on for decades: find the audience, buy the attention, shift the product. What we’re less good at is finding audiences before they become obvious. Communities already mobilised around something they love, spending freely, and waiting for brands to show up in a way that feels made for them.
This is not a small opportunity. Global research commissioned by Amazon Ads and Twitch Ads across 12 countries found that 70 per cent of fans consider their fandom and its community part of their everyday life, while 64 per cent say fandom is a defining part of their identity. The study covered markets including Japan, South Korea, the US, UK, Australia, Brazil and Mexico. Fans are consumers, too: 64 per cent use digital services to discover and purchase products related to their interests.
Here’s the stat that should stop any brand strategist mid-scroll: 61 per cent of fans enjoy seeing brands sponsor or create content related to their fandoms, while 63 per cent believe any brand can participate as long as it makes the effort to understand the community. But authenticity matters: 54 per cent say they can tell when brands advertising within their fandom are being authentic.
The gaming fandom opportunity
Take gaming. A 2025 global study of more than 24,000 active players across 21 countries and six continents found that the average gamer is 41 years old, demolishing the teenage stereotype that still informs many media plans. The audience is also almost evenly split by gender, at 51 per cent male and 48 per cent female. The study included major Asian markets such as China, India, Japan and South Korea. This audience is enormous, engaged and spending, yet many non-gaming brands still treat it as someone else’s territory.
Non-endemic brands – those with no direct link to gaming – have become a major part of the esports sponsorship ecosystem. McKinsey research found that non-endemic companies accounted for 60 per cent of esports partnerships, illustrating how far gaming has moved beyond technology and gaming brands. McDonald’s brought Pokémon TCG to Happy Meals. Lacoste partnered with Minecraft. Elf Cosmetics built its own world in Roblox. These brands identified where a passionate, high-spending audience already lived and showed up authentically within it.
The brands that haven’t made this move yet aren’t absent from gaming because the opportunity isn’t there. They’re absent because they haven’t looked.
BookTok brings all the fans to the yard
The same dynamic is playing out in the literary world, with numbers just as hard to ignore. In 2025, more than 50 million books recommended by TikTok’s #BookTok community were sold across key European markets, generating €800 million in revenue, according to analysis from NielsenIQ BookData and Media Control. More than a third of 16- to 39-year-olds surveyed said they discover new books through BookTok, while digital platforms are having a noticeable influence on purchasing behaviour, with around one in three young readers buying books they have previously seen or had recommended online.
The phenomenon extends well beyond one market. NielsenIQ BookData found that BookTok-inspired genre fiction was lifting sales across numerous countries in 2025, with romantasy titles from authors including Rebecca Yarros and Sarah J Maas appearing among bestsellers internationally. In the US, meanwhile, Circana estimated that sales associated with BookTok jumped 20 per cent in 2024 to around 60 million units.
This fandom doesn’t stay contained to books. Skyscanner named “Bookbound” – trips inspired by literature – one of the defining travel trends of 2026, with hotel bookings using its “library” filter up 70 per cent globally year-on-year. More than half of travellers surveyed globally had booked or would consider a trip inspired by literature.
The story spills into fashion, beauty, food and film. Every category a brand would want to be in.
Brands capitalising on the opportunity
American fashion house Coach saw this and moved. In late 2025, the brand partnered with Sunnie Reads, the Gen Z book club from Reese Witherspoon’s company Hello Sunshine, and created The Book Nook, an immersive experience featuring book swaps, reading spaces and custom leather bookmarks. The brand followed this with a book charm collection developed with Penguin Random House, fronted by actors Elle Fanning and Storm Reid, and athlete Paige Bueckers. Attendees and creators generated organic content that amplified the campaign far beyond paid reach. A luxury brand, earning cultural credibility within a community rather than purchasing it.
The brands missing out
Meanwhile, beauty brands have largely left book creators out of their influencer programmes. The organic content already exists: character makeup tutorials, book cover-inspired looks, dark academia aesthetics racking up millions of views. The community made it without being asked. The sponsored version remains unrealised.
Brands look at fandom communities and see niche. What they’re actually looking at is depth. A mainstream audience of passive scrollers and a fandom of 10 million deeply invested people are not the same media buy. The fandom audience already trusts, already spends, already creates content and is actively looking for brands that get them.
Still, the hesitation is fair. Fandom communities have fierce authenticity standards, and international research suggests fans are highly attuned to whether brands genuinely understand the spaces they enter. More than half can tell whether a brand engaging with their fandom is being authentic, while 63 per cent say brands are welcome to participate provided they make the effort to understand the community. But the answer to a sophisticated audience is a sophisticated approach. The brands that get it right show up with a genuine understanding of the culture and let the community take it from there.
Fandom moments form on TikTok, Twitch, Discord and Reddit well before they reach the mainstream. Brands need to build the connection where an audience’s culture is forming, as opposed to where it has already peaked. Every brand has a fandom somewhere. The question is whether brands will show up before someone else does.
Yasemin Akpinar is a senior account manager at media agency Hatched.
