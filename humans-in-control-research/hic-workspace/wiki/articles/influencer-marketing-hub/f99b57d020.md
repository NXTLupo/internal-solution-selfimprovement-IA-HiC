# How Yoto Turned Parent Advocacy Into a $127M Kids’ Audio Brand

Source: [influencer_marketing_hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/how-yoto-turned-parent-advocacy-kids-audio-brand/)

A Yoto Player is unusually easy to explain without saying much. A child chooses a physical card, places it in the player, and starts a story, song, or activity without asking an adult to unlock a screen.
That simple interaction has become one of Yoto’s most effective marketing assets. Parents can immediately see the product working, children can operate it themselves, and creators can place it inside familiar routines such as bedtime, travel, and independent play.
By 2024, Yoto was generating £95 million, approximately $127 million, in annual revenue. But its growth came from more than selling screen-free hardware. Yoto turned family use into recommendation, recommendation into customer acquisition, and each new customer into an audience for more cards, programming, and partnerships.
The result is a business in which parent advocacy brings families into the product ecosystem and a constantly expanding content catalogue gives them reasons to remain there.

How Yoto Built a Screen-Free Audio Player Around Child Independence
Yoto co-founders Ben Drury and Filip Denker met while working at digital music company 7digital. Both became parents around the same time and began questioning why children’s access to audio increasingly depended on a screen, microphone, or adult-controlled device.
Their answer was a connected audio player inspired by Montessori principles. Children would choose what to hear by handling physical cards, while parents retained control over the content available. The product had no camera, microphone, or advertising.
Early prototypes were assembled from plywood, Raspberry Pis, laser-cut parts, and hand-soldered electronics. Drury and Denker tested them with their own children, then with other families. A small Kickstarter campaign in 2017 showed that the idea had demand beyond their immediate circle, including early interest from the United States.
That initial proposition has remained remarkably consistent: parents buy reassurance, while children receive autonomy.
How Parent Creators Demonstrate Yoto Through Everyday Family Routines
The second-generation Yoto Player arrived in early 2020, followed by a US launch that June. The timing placed it inside homes when parents urgently needed ways to occupy children without adding more screen time.
But Yoto’s creator appeal extends beyond the broad screen-free promise. Parent videos show the Player solving specific moments: keeping a toddler occupied in a café, giving children something to do during a car journey, making bedtime calmer, or buying parents another 20 minutes of rest in the morning.
One creator reduced the entire value proposition to a household routine. The video moved from screen-free mornings to independent play, showing the outcome instead of reciting specifications.

How Customer Feedback and Word of Mouth Fueled Yoto’s Early Growth
Yoto involved customers before it had a formal global creator operation. The company shipped an imperfect early product, communicated openly about its limitations, and used family feedback to improve later versions.
A Facebook group started by an early customer grew to roughly 70,000 members, with hundreds of related subgroups emerging around cards, age recommendations, Make Your Own content, and regional communities. Drury has said around 60% of customers arrive through word of mouth.
The Make Your Own Card feature deepened that participation. Parents could add recordings from grandparents, family playlists, home-language stories, or audio unavailable in the official catalogue. Customers were no longer confined to consuming Yoto’s library. They could adapt the system to their family.

How Yoto Built Its Influencer Marketing and Social Media Strategy
As Yoto expanded, it began turning the parent advocacy already surrounding the product into a more deliberate operating model.
Influencer marketing sits close to Communications and Advocacy under Sophie Mawby, which reflects the role creators play for the brand. Their job extends beyond generating clicks. They help families understand the category, assess the product, and picture where it would fit into their lives.
Yoto has gradually built that capability across markets. Faisal A.’s global influencer remit focuses on developing creator partnerships internationally, while Geri Somers has recruited influencer, affiliate, and social talent for North America.
The company has also looked for regional teams and agency relationships with established creator networks in markets such as Australia and New Zealand.
Organic social performs a different but connected role. Instead of treating customer questions as support requests that disappear into an inbox, Yoto turns them into public content.
Senior Social Manager Sophie Timmons said its Tip Tuesday series responds to questions about features, settings, and creative uses with short how-to videos. The series passed one million views, showing how product education can become an ongoing social format rather than a one-time launch campaign.
Paid social sits within Yoto’s acquisition and growth organization, supported by dedicated creative and testing roles. That creates a practical division of labor. Communications and creator partnerships build trust, organic social answers recurring questions, and paid media can scale messages and formats that have already demonstrated their relevance.
Yoto’s influencer strategy is therefore less about assembling a recognizable ambassador roster and more about building layers of recommendation around the customer journey.
What Data Reveals About Yoto’s Creator Strategy
A conventional influencer program usually leaves a recognizable footprint: a defined group of creators, repeated sponsored posts, and a campaign message that remains relatively consistent from one account to the next. Yoto’s footprint looks different.
Across six months of social media activity analyzed through HypeAuditor, the conversation was spread across hundreds of accounts. Only a relatively small share of the mentions were clearly sponsored, while most appeared outside Yoto’s visible paid activity.
HypeAuditor’s classifications are modeled, so they cannot confirm every relationship, but the overall shape is still revealing. Yoto is being discussed by a much wider group than the creators it visibly pays.
The accounts themselves reinforce that interpretation. Almost three quarters were nano or micro creators, family content was the largest category, and individual accounts overwhelmingly outnumbered brand profiles. These are the kinds of voices parents encounter while looking for product advice, activity ideas, travel tips, or help choosing between Yoto and Tonies.
Short-form video dominated because Yoto needs to be seen in use. A Reel can show a child inserting a card, changing stories, carrying the Mini through an airport, or settling down at bedtime. In that format, the creator is simultaneously reviewing the product, teaching the viewer how it works, and proving that a child can use it independently.
The aggregate engagement rate across the activity was modest, but that does not make the content ineffective. Many of these videos are designed to answer a specific purchase question rather than attract mass entertainment. They can influence a decision through demonstration, search visibility, saved recommendations, affiliate clicks, or repeated exposure across several parent accounts.
When a Yoto post does travel widely, the product is usually supporting a larger parenting point. A video from @mum_mode_unhinged opened by arguing that toddlers do not need an iPad, then showed how Yoto helped during cafés, bedtime, early mornings, and independent play.
