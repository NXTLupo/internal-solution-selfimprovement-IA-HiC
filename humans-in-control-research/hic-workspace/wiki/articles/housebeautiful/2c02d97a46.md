# 5 Things Designers Wish Couples Knew BEFORE Starting a Renovation

Source: [housebeautiful](https://www.housebeautiful.com/home-remodeling/renovation/a73466288/couples-renovation-advice/)

Before embarking on a home renovation as a couple, it’s key to align on the big things first: your budget, who has decision-making authority, how the space needs to function, and which design elements are truly non-negotiable. Without those conversations, a remodel can quickly become a time-consuming and frustrating process—and interior designers can end up acting like couples’ therapists.
“Doing a remodel can be a huge challenge for a relationship since it puts clients under a lot of different types of pressure in a short period of time,” interior designer Sierra Troitsky says. “It requires efficient decision-making from both partners, clear communication, and can be quite a time and financial investment. If clients aren't on the same page and communicating well, it can be a really frustrating process for everyone involved.”
Designer Mackenzie Collier has a background in family and child psychology, and she’s noted the importance of “pre-renovation counseling” as a first step. Think of it like premarital counseling, but focused more on a floor plan than a life plan. It helps you identify any blind spots in your design dreams, prior to attaching dollars and cents to them. Below, designers reveal how they act as pre-renovation counselors, plus the things they wish couples knew before the walls start coming down (literally).
Define the Budget and the Decision Maker
Designers are begging couples to decide on two things before starting a remodel: the budget and who gets the final say. If the exact dollars aren’t laid out ahead of time, every upcoming decision becomes a renegotiation, and that’s where conflict arises.
Anita Yokota is both a licensed marriage therapist and an interior designer, and she won’t let design clients tell her the budget is unfinished. “Come back to me when you know the exact number,” Yokota says. “There's always something that happens that you don't plan for. You come in with the exact number, and I will tell you, based on your design, what is possible.”
It’s not just the dollars and cents that matter. Someone needs to be the tiebreaker. When a stalemate happens, someone has to take the lead on the final decision. It’s best to agree on that person at the beginning of the renovation, rather than in the middle of a dispute.
“Who has final say on aesthetics? Who controls the budget?” designer Cathleen Gruver says. “Those conversations feel awkward, but skipping them is exactly how a project stalls at week three when one partner is horrified by the countertop sample the other approved, or decides they don't think spending money on a certain item is worth it.”
Decide on a point person first. Even if couples have equal investment in the process, one person should approve final purchases and be in charge of communication.
Function Comes Before Style
Decorative choices like art, paint, and decor can all be reversed without much cost. But the room’s layout, flow of traffic, and storage needs can be pretty expensive to change your mind on once it’s built. That’s why designer Priyanka Korat says before a single finish is selected, couples should focus on the function of each renovated room. What do you want the space to be used for? Couples who renovate for different reasons can run into trouble.
“One wants the house to impress people, the other just wants it to function better for daily life,” Korat says. “Neither is wrong, but the design ends up pulling in two directions if it's not named early. Usually just asking each of them separately, without their partner in the room, what they actually want the finished house to feel like, gets it out in the open fast.”
Let the Designer Help Mediate
Remember that you hired a professional designer for a reason. You don’t need to have solved every dilemma before you start working together. You’re not in this alone, and the professional’s eye can spot a path through the problem.
“What you don’t need to nail down is the solution to every problem,” designer Lauren Sventrup says. “Actually, I’d rather you didn’t. Bring me the problem, not the solution. Give me the opportunity as a designer to come up with something you may never have considered on your own. That’s part of why you hired me.”
Your design team can also help you understand the core of your renovation wishes. Rather than getting attached to a specific aesthetic, look at style beyond the label. Mike Shively and Lucas Goldbach of En Masse Architecture and Design focus on finding the actual themes in their client’s design inspiration.
“We once worked with homeowners who described their dream home as ‘French,’ but after reviewing their inspiration images, it became clear they were really drawn to specific architectural elements, including tall windows, arches, and French doors, rather than a purely French architectural style,” the duo said. “That discovery ultimately led us toward a home that better reflected what they truly loved.”
Too Much Compromise Gets Bland Fast
If everyone gives up too many of their desires, it can lead to a watered-down design that neither partner is happy with. Per Regina and Robin Reaves of R&R Interior Design 365, never compromise for compromise’s sake. Both personalities deserve to have a vibrant place in the home, even if they’re at opposite ends of the spectrum.
“One may gravitate toward the clean lines and restraint of modern or contemporary design, while the other is drawn to the warmth, ornamentation, and familiarity of traditional interiors,” the couple says. “Rather than asking one style to win out over the other, we look for the common ground between them. When approached thoughtfully, contrasting aesthetics can be layered together in a way that feels effortless rather than divided.”
This could look like balancing furniture with modern silhouettes with traditional decor. And though it might be hard to hear, sometimes it’s best to just let one partner win and save yourself the headache. “If you really care about the sofa fabric and your partner doesn’t, you should get the sofa fabric you love,” Sventrup says. “And there will probably be something else your partner feels strongly about that you don’t. They should get to have exactly what they want there.”
Your History Will Come Into Play
As a couple, your renovation decisions will not be made in a vacuum. Disagreements you didn’t even know you had will come to the surface.
“Anytime you work with a couple, the remodel is going to be an extension of their existing relationship,” Collier says. “If they have a power imbalance, trust issues, or lack of integrity, that will come out during the remodel.”
That’s part of why Korat puts so much stake in a thorough questionnaire at the beginning. Chances are, you’re about to hear your partner’s real thoughts on a home for the first time out loud.
For longtime couples, any history in your relationship can change the way you receive feedback during the renovation process. That’s another reason to embrace your designer’s counsel. “When it comes from your partner, you’re hearing it with 10 or 20 years of history and emotion attached,” Sventrup says. “When it comes from me, you can hear it as a new idea or an objective point of view.”
