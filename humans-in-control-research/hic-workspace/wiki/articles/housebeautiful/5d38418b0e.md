# The Clever “60-30-10 Color Rule” Designers Use to Make a Room Look Instantly Pulled Together

Source: [housebeautiful](https://www.housebeautiful.com/design-inspiration/a73512287/designer-60-30-10-color-rule/)

Pictured Above: This North Carolina home, designed by Charlotte Lucas, balances a prominently pink palette with earthy brown and green hues.
A color palette is one of the most influential elements in defining a home’s overall aesthetic, but choosing the right colors for your space can feel overwhelming. Play it too safe, and the room may fall flat; go too bold, and your space can start to feel visually cluttered. That’s where one designer’s most reliable decorating principles come in. The “60-30-10 color rule” is a simple approach that helps create cohesive, well-balanced color schemes.
At its foundation, the 60-30-10 color rule is all about proportion and harmony. The guideline suggests using one dominant color for 60 percent of the room, a secondary color for 30 percent, and an accent shade for the remaining 10 percent. Following this balance helps ensure a space feels layered and intentional without becoming overpowering—or so subtle that the colors go unnoticed.
“In interior design, there aren't any hard and fast rules,” says designer Amanda Wyatt. “But the formula gives people a jumping-off point to feel more confident when they're designing on their own.”
If you’ve spent hours saving inspiration images without knowing how to recreate the same effect at home, you’re certainly not the only one. From choosing a palette to deciding where each shade should appear throughout the room, here’s how to use the 60-30-10 color rule to create a polished, pulled-together space.
How to Use the 60-30-10 Color Rule
More often than not, your primary color (the 60 percent) will be on your walls. No matter how big or small your space, those four walls take up the most square footage. Your furniture and larger decor accents, like artwork, will account for 30 percent, and any soft goods, like pillows and throws, act as your 10 percent. Of course, these percentages can shift and swap—it's a customizable formula for a reason—but this is the most useful starting point.
Examples of the 60-30-10 Color Rule
Yellow, Blue, and Brown
Use the 60-30-10 color rule to balance a color palette you already love, like this yellow-and-blue sitting room designed by Annie Kern.
- 60: The floral motif in the wallpaper works with the yellow sofa and window’s stained glass treatments to make yellow the primary hue in this sitting room.
- 30: The two pale blue armchairs cement the cool tone as the secondary hue.
- 10: The flooring, stained glass, and light fixture make brown a subtle but grounding accent color.
Green, Yellow, and Pink
The 60-30-10 color rule also works for color palettes that incorporate patterns and multicolor prints. The primary bedroom seen above, designed by Isabel Ladd Interiors, is from House Beautiful's 2023 Whole Home.
- 60: While the patterns can make it difficult to identify a primary hue, the green ceiling and trim cement the hue as the focal point of the color palette.
- 30: There are splashes of yellow all over the room, showing up in the lighting, bench, and drapery.
- 10: Pink is the accent color in this bedroom. The bold throw pillow coordinates nicely with the wallpaper's florals, tying everything together.
White, Brown, and Black
Minimalists can utilize the 60-30-10 color rule too! Just look at this graphic dining room from designer Ali Budd.
- 60: Crisp white walls and ceiling make white the primary hue.
- 30: Brown shows up as the secondary shade in the natural wood tones of the dining furniture and the flooring.
- 10: The black credenza and serving bowl tie the room together as the accent shade.
How to Use the Rule in Monochromatic Rooms
With a monochromatic color palette, think of the different tones of color as individual pieces of the puzzle. Take the above room, designed by Charlotte Lucas, as an example. Pale pink is the 60-percent base layer, while the deeper shade is the 30. To round out the room, a deep, almost burgundy hue acts as the 10 percent. A helpful hint is to work from lightest to darkest (or vice versa). Designate the lightest or darkest shade as your primary color and work the formula from there.
When to Break the 60-30-10 Color Rule
If you find that it isn't working, you can break the 60-30-10 color rule whenever you'd like. While it's called a rule, this formula is merely a guideline for balance in a colorful room. It's fine if you have 40 percent of one hue and only 20 percent of the accent. Two-color palettes are a great example of when the color rulebook can be thrown out.
