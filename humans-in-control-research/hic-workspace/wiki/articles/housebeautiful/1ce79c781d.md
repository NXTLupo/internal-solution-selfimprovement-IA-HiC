# The Most Valuable Antiques to Hunt for Across the U.S., According to Expert Curators

Source: [housebeautiful](https://www.housebeautiful.com/design-inspiration/a73485427/best-antiques-us-regions/)

Part of the thrill of antiquing is never knowing exactly what you’ll find. The pieces tucked inside a Savannah antiques shop can look completely different from those filling a Detroit warehouse or lining the shelves of a roadside stop along Route 66. That’s because every corner of the country has its own design language, influenced by the region’s history, industries, traditions, and generations of craftspeople.
In the South, graceful crystal and passed-down silver reflect a long tradition of hospitality and entertaining. Travel west, and the finds shift to hand-tooled leather, turquoise jewelry, and pottery rooted in Native American artistry and frontier culture. Across the Midwest, practicality and craftsmanship take center stage, with well-made pieces that balance function and beauty. On the coasts, meanwhile, global influences mingle with local tastes, producing everything from breezy Palm Beach rattan and Hollywood Regency glamour to the refined nautical style associated with New England.
Antiques all have a history, but their place of origin can reveal just as much as the objects themselves. We turned to collectors and design experts from around the country to uncover the antiques their regions are famous for, along with the stories behind them—and why these distinctive pieces are still worth seeking out today.
The Southeast
In the South, antiques are as much about hospitality as they are about history.
“It’s no surprise that Southern antiques are almost synonymous with hosting,” says writer and collector Nicole Letts of Grand Millennial Shop, based in Atlanta, Georgia. “There’s a reason we say ‘Southern hospitality,’ after all.” Translation: you’ll find porcelain and transferware, sterling flatware, hand-embroidered linens, and crystal glassware at nearly every antiques market from Charleston to Savannah. Collectors also delight in the small details that make a table sing, from butter pats and knife rests to delicate salt cellars complete with tiny spoons.
But there’s a new energy shaping Southern collecting. “We’re seeking the voices and craftsmanship that were historically overlooked,” Letts explains. That means Gee’s Bend quilts, folk art, functional pottery, and handmade instruments now share space with silver and china. Regional influences shine through, too: French flair along the Gulf Coast, British formality on the Atlantic, and a dose of Palm Beach playfulness down in Florida. “Who doesn’t love that quirky midcentury look?” Letts says.
Florida’s antiques scene is as varied and colorful as the state itself. “In Palm Beach, you’ll see a lot of 1960s Hollywood Regency-style vintage,” says Virginia Chamlee, a Ponte Vedra resident and author of Big Thrift Energy. “Think lucite, brass, and a little unapologetic glitz.” Head south to Miami and the look shifts to postmodern ‘80s and ‘90s pieces—sleek lines, bold shapes, and plenty of personality. Up north, coastal classics like rattan and bamboo still steal the show. “It’s timeless,” Chamlee adds, “depending on how you style it.”
The Great Plains
In Oklahoma, you won’t find many colonial relics or centuries-old armoires—but that doesn’t mean the region lacks character.
“We’re a relatively young state,” says designer and collector Daniel Barrett Mathis of Oklahoma City. “So our antiques tend to be a little younger, too.” Instead of 18th-century furniture, expect midcentury finds, vintage chinoiserie, and plenty of Western and Native American–inspired pieces.
That mix reflects the area’s unique crossroads of cultures: Mexican pottery, steer horns, and woven baskets share space with Frankoma Pottery, a local favorite among collectors. “Since Route 66 passes through here, you also see a lot of vintage tourist memorabilia,” Mathis adds. Oklahoma’s surprising amount of midcentury architecture also means its flea markets and estate sales are dotted with sleek furniture and sculptural décor from that era. The result? A spirited blend of rustic and refined that feels distinctly, proudly regional.
The Northeast
In the Northeast, antiques tell the story of America’s earliest craftsmanship.
“Traditional Colonial, Shaker, and Federal pieces remain timeless,” says Noel Fahden Briceno, vice president of Global Merchandising at Chairish. Rich woods like cherry, mahogany, and maple still dominate, reflecting what early artisans had on hand. The region’s seafaring past also left its mark in the form of shell-encrusted sailor’s valentines, oil-painted seascapes, and a palette of deep browns, ocean blues, and mossy greens that mirror the coastline.
“The Northeast carries its cultural DNA through its antiques,” Briceno explains. While those roots remain strong, regional styles are more fluid than ever, with buyers nationwide embracing what once felt distinctly New England. Whether it’s a roll-arm sofa or a weathered nautical motif, these pieces prove that classic craftsmanship never really goes out of style.
The Midwest
Heartland antiques may not boast the gilded flair of the coasts or the centuries-old provenance of New England, but what they lack in pedigree, they more than make up for in character.
“The Midwest came later than the East Coast, so most of our antiques date from the mid-1800s onward,” explains Sally Schwartz of Randolph Street Market in Chicago. “It’s a mix of practicality and pride—you can see the beauty in how things were built to last.”
Oak, walnut, and other hearty woods dominate, reflecting both the materials at hand and the no-nonsense sensibility of the area’s early settlers. Victorian and Arts and Crafts furniture—often crafted by German and Scandinavian immigrants—still turns up at estate sales and small-town shops, alongside folk art, hand-stitched quilts, and primitive farm tools that speak to a simpler way of life.
But it’s not all milk paint and tool benches. The Midwest’s industrial past produced some of the most sought-after 20th-century designs, Knoll and Herman Miller among them, and cities like Chicago remain hotbeds for vintage advertising, posters, and print ephemera from their days as national publishing hubs. From farmhouse finds to midcentury icons, the region’s antiques embody an enduring balance of craftsmanship and authenticity.
Southern California
The state’s mix of architectural eras and international influence means there’s no single “SoCal antique style.” Dealers source midcentury treasures from Palm Springs estates, rustic European imports, and coastal finds from Santa Barbara to San Diego.
“Because California living is so connected to the outdoors, antiques made from natural materials (ceramics, weathered woods, patinated metals, natural stone like travertine, woven rattan, even aged concrete) always feel right at home,” says designer Kara Piepmeyer of Studio Kosma.
Southern California’s antique landscape mirrors its layered history: Spanish Revival homes call for hand-forged ironwork and terra-cotta accents, Mission and Arts and Crafts–era houses favor oak furniture with simple joinery, and the region’s midcentury architecture keeps the demand high for clean-lined teak credenzas, sculptural lighting, and statement lounge chairs. Collectors still flock to estate sales in Palm Springs for pieces by Eames, Baughman, and Florence Knoll, while coastal buyers hunt for weathered European antiques that pair beautifully with the area’s relaxed, sunlit interiors. Even Hollywood’s Golden Age left its mark, with gilt mirrors, Deco vanities, and Regency-style consoles still surfacing at Los Angeles markets.
The modern California collector is also unafraid of contrast. Think sculptural Danish or Italian pieces with strong silhouettes, hand-carved Art Deco chairs reimagined in new fabrics, or a Brutalist dining set that balances strength with artistry. “I’m drawn to robust handcrafted forms that feel solid and soulful—the kind of honest beauty you can actually live with,” says Piepmeyer. Whether it’s aged brass, pale oak, or weathered concrete, SoCal antiques are less about perfection and more about patina or the subtle wear that tells a story and makes a space feel lived-in, layered, and entirely one-of-a-kind.
