# The Working Dog on the American Farm: Care, Cost, and What Happens When You Travel

Source: [agr_morning_ag_clips](https://www.morningagclips.com/the-working-dog-on-the-american-farm-care-cost-and-what-happens-when-you-travel/)

BOISE, Idaho — Dogs have been partners to American farmers for centuries. They keep herds of livestock, protect property, warn owners of predators, and repel pests from grain storage. A farm dog’s place is earned daily. Owning a working dog is a responsibility and it has costs that many farmers don’t recognise.
In the U.S., it is estimated that there are 89 million pet dogs. Many reside on farms and properties in rural areas. Australian Shepherds, Border Collies, Great Pyrenees and Blue Heelers are all common breeds found on American farms. These aren’t just any pets. Over the years they have been bred for strength, particular requirements and territorial instincts; they are working animals.
The total expense of feeding a large working dog is from $600 to $1200 a year, depending on breed and diet. Regular vet care costs an additional $300 – $700 per year. Add vaccinations, flea and tick prevention, heartworm treatment, and the dog’s occasional injuries from farm work and the true annual expense of a farm dog is well over $1,500. Its number is surprising to many first-time farm dog owners, who thought the dog would take care of itself the majority of the time.
Structure is important for working dogs, too. Without work, a Border Collie will become a destructive dog in a hurry! These breeds require mental stimulation and exercise. On an active farm that’s usually not a problem. Once a farmer has to go away for a couple days, however — for equipment auctions, family visits, ag conferences.
It is the planning that makes a difference here. High energy working dogs do not do well alone or with an inexperienced resident neighbor. Professional dog boarding facilities specializing in active and working breeds are now in use across the country by many farmers. These facilities offer regular exercise, good nutrition, and enclosure — things a farm dog truly requires to remain calm and healthy when left alone.
The price of boarding differs from region to region. The price for urban and suburban facilities is generally $35-$85 per night. Rural boarding tends to be more affordable and can be $25 to $50 a night. Some even provide discounts for weekly rates, making the overall price cheaper for extended travel. If a farmer has to board for 4-5 times a year, boarding costs can be $400-$600 per year in addition to other dog ownership costs.
There’s a welfare argument as well as the money. Farm dogs are used to lots of room, physical activity and stimulation. If one is kept in a small kennel without adequate exercise for days at a time, they become stressed. A boarding facility that is well-versed in the behavior of working breeds recognizes that it is important to adapt care to the needs of these types of dogs. It’s worth spending more money for.
Some farmers have other solutions to this problem. Temporary caretakers are provided by trusted neighbours, farm workers or family members. This is effective as long as the individual has dog experience and has the stamina to manage an energetic, strong dog. Farms are beginning to develop succession plans which include animal care plans. All working farm animals are now documented with feeding schedules, vet contacts, medication notes and behavioural notes for larger farm operations. Dogs included. It is practical thinking that saves the animal and keeps the farm running smoothly when the main owner is not around.
Farmers are coming to accept new paradigms in American agriculture. Because there is a shortage of workforce, input costs continue to rise and the complexity of operations keeps growing, farmers need all the help they can get. The best tool on a farm is a well-cared-for working dog. Much cheaper than some equipment, no fuel required and can be used with
true loyalty.
This is not just good animal welfare to take that dog’s needs seriously, even when traveling — including when he is put up for the night — It’s good farm management. Farmers who value their working dogs as assets are more likely to retain good dogs, have fewer losses, and make their operation more robust in general.
A well cared-for dog is a good dog. It’s always been that way on American farms.
