# Resonance: A Plague Tale Legacy review – Asobo takes a risk and delivers the best game in the series

Source: [creative_bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/entertainment/gaming/resonance-a-plague-tale-legacy-review-asobo-takes-a-risk-and-delivers-the-best-game-in-the-series)

Our Verdict
Beautiful, creative and constantly surprising, Resonance takes A Plague Tale in new directions and evolves the series wonderfully. One of the year's best games.
For
- Grimly beautiful art direction
- A must play story arc
- Good balance of gameplay ideas
Against
- Theseus combat feels undercooked

Developer Asobo Studio

I was worried about Resonance: A Plague Tale Legacy, mostly because of the sword combat. When Asobo announced that Sophia, the pirate we met in the previous game, Requiem, would be running around with a blade and proper melee combat in her own prequel, I couldn't help thinking, do we really need this? A Plague Tale had carved out a little space for itself with an original blend of stealth, puzzles, rats, Medieval misery and beautifully written characters, and suddenly it looked like we were heading towards a 14th-century God of War. There are enough games where I can smash someone in the face with a sword; A Plague Tale didn't need to be another.
But the combat turns out to be one of the least important things about Resonance, and that's why it works so well. The combat is perfectly decent, Sophia is a nimble fighter, and there are some nice but unrevolutionary ideas around parrying, dodging and using the environment – kicking enemies off bridges and into ancient 'holes' is oddly satisfying. The aim is to always knock enemies off balance, such as kicking a shield carrier or throwing objects to stun enemies, which leaves them open to a final killer blow – you've done this before. A simple upgrade tree gives Sophia things like faster dodges and double kicks, and new hidden weapons offer buffs and debuffs, like a broken spearhead for stabbing through shields but only useful when running.

Sword fights are well implemented but feel basic compared to games that make melee combat the whole point, like Devil May Cry 5 or God of War. The ancient Minoan 'vision resonances' where Sophia lives out Theseus' trials centuries earlier show this most clearly, because these gladiatorial encounters don't really develop the system beyond the same parry-and-counter rhythm; in fact, by the credits Sophia is more skilled with a sword than Theseus.
But the thing I kept thinking about while playing was how recognisably A Plague Tale it all felt, despite the introduction of combat into the mix. The writing, the sense of place, the puzzles, the horrible creeping dread, the way a beautiful landscape will evolve into grimly dark decay – it's all still here and done better than ever before.
Still A Plague Tale at heart
Resonance is both a prequel to A Plague Tale: Innocence and Requiem, with Sophia setting out to uncover the truth about her past on the mysterious Minotaur's island. Sophia is already a very different lead from the series' heroine, Amicia. She's a plunderer, a pirate, confident with a sword, and has enough swagger to make the early sections feel lively and just a little carefree, particularly when she's travelling with sidekick Leni. There's a real sense that the two of them have stumbled into something exciting, and for a while Resonance is happy to let you enjoy that feeling.
But things turn dark – visions, secrets, supernatural – and the game doesn't simply turn the lights off and throw monsters at you, but Sophia's whole attitude changes as she discovers what is happening on the island, what it means, and the performance sells that change particularly well. She starts out excited and curious, then becomes frightened, remorseful and increasingly aware that she's dealing with something she can't simply stab her way out of.
That shift is reflected in the environments too, as the spectacular open spaces, brilliant blue skies, clear water, sandy ruins and enormous ancient statues give way to claustrophobic tombs and catacombs where the walls are crawling with the remnants of the Macula – the game's supernatural blood infestation. There's a lovely late-game scene where the sunrise catches the orange rocks and temple ruins, and everything looks almost impossibly beautiful, which makes the uglier things lurking underneath feel even more horrible.

Exploring is enjoyable; there are grapple hooks, hidden tombs, secret passages and ancient mechanisms, and while none of this is especially revolutionary – and the 14th-century Tomb Raider tag feels apt – Asobo has such a good eye for environments that I rarely minded following the obvious path. I kept wanting to see what was around the next corner, and uncover the next tease of why everything went to literal hell for King Minos centuries before, because looking around these crumbling ruins, something wild went down.
The same goes for the puzzles. Most of them revolve around light, using an ancient golden sphere that Sophia can upgrade as she progresses. It can become a prism for matching coloured light or uncovering secret messages and clues, among other things, and eventually a focused beam with a rather more practical use. I wouldn't call the puzzles difficult – there were two that made me properly stop and think –, but I liked the way the idea runs through the game, particularly because manipulating light feels like a natural extension of what A Plague Tale has always done with torchlight and darkness. The more I play, the more Resonance feels like an evolution of the series' core identity and mechanics.
That's because the combat is never allowed to take over. You still spend plenty of time sneaking, solving puzzles and exploring the medieval world's enormous environments, as Sophia's emotional story unfolds, drawing together multiple strings into a slow-burning revelatory arc, and when the game really wants to frighten you, it drops the combat entirely and embraces the series stealth roots.
The tombs of Minoa are covered in fluorescent dust, and Sophia picks it up as she moves around. The Macula has produced a new sort of worm-like creature that can track her by following that dust, so now your footprints become a track it can follow. You have to think about where you're walking, where you can hide, which areas are safe and how quickly you can get from one piece of cover to another.
Artistically, the new creature feels like an evolution of the rat horde in how it moves and attacks – the creature fears light, will suck the dust from the air, and you can see the glowing particles being pulled away from Sophia as it gets closer, and more so, every particle in the scene is sucked past at speed. Its breathing changes too, becoming deeper and more urgent, and as it breathes in slowly, those dust particles move rhythmically on screen; there were several points where I found myself sitting completely still, waiting for it to move away, just watching the particles ebb, flow and subside.
You can light cauldrons to create safe spaces; moving slowly while crouched leaves no dust behind, and jumping across gaps breaks the trail for a little while, but there's never quite enough security to make you comfortable. You're always watching that dust, listening for the creature and wondering whether you've left too obvious a trail, or questioning whether you can run to safety or keep crawling, creating a novel sense of risk and reward. It's a different mechanic from the rats, obviously, but it produces the same kind of tension.
The horror keeps developing as well, with an impressive late-game sequence involving crusted, desiccated figures that starts out looking like another piece of environmental dressing and then becomes much less harmless as they crack into life and chase you down, and like the earlier games' rats, make too much noise, and you'll be swamped in the mannequin-like monsters.

Resonance doesn't just repeat its best tricks either, as the further Sophia gets into Minoa, the more unpleasant everything becomes, and the Macula's organic tendrils start appearing everywhere, turning what should be grand ancient ruins into something diseased and almost alive. The story pulls it all together through Sophia's connection to an ancient Carrier and Protector, with events from the Minoan era bleeding into her own journey.
I don't want to get too deep into what happens here because much of the pleasure comes from watching the pieces connect, but this is where Resonance earns its place in the series. Sophia isn't simply discovering an ancient mystery and ticking off a list of tombs. The Carrier, the Protector and the Macula become tied to the choices she has to make, and the game's revelations gradually explain why she has been drawn into this particular story.
A particularly effective parallel is how Sophia begins the game thinking she can handle whatever is put in front of her, only to confront something that can't be solved with confidence, a sword, or a clever bit of tomb raiding. That emotional progression is more interesting than watching her become a better fighter, especially since, like Requiem, no one here is safe from a gruesome death, and that pendulum hanging over every plot twist gives Resonance the emotional hook I've loved about this series. By the final third, Resonance is fully back in the territory where A Plague Tale has always been strongest. It's miserable, beautiful, frightening and surprisingly heartfelt, with the different pieces of the game finally pushing in the same direction.
