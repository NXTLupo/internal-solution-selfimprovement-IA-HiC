# Why making the real thing still holds its magic: Isabel + Helen on a decade of putting impossible inventions into motion

Source: [its_nice_that](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/isabel-and-helen-motion-sickness-art-spotlight-100926)

- Words
- Ellis Tree
- —
- Date
- 10 September 2026
- Tags
Why making the real thing still holds its magic: Isabel + Helen on a decade of putting impossible inventions into motion
As their Motion Sickness show opens at The Conran Shop for LDF, we catch up with the pair on their continued fascination with the mechanics of things, to uncover just how they have pulled off so many breathtaking projects – from larger-than-life kinetic sculptures to wearable Power Suits.

Share
When we heard that Isabel + Helen were putting on a show at The Conran Shop to celebrate ten years of the studio this LDF, we were immediately taken back to 2016 when we first interviewed the pair on the early days of their set design work for our emerging talent series Ones to Watch. A whole decade since then, and a whole host of career-defining collaborations, breathtaking builds and awe-inspiring campaigns under their belt, we’ve watched the studio go from strength to strength, all with a perpetual dedication to hands-on craft.
Shining a light on the past decade of their joint practice, the exhibition focuses on one singular theme that has touched all of Isabel + Helen’s work to date: the studio’s unending fascination with the mechanics of making things move. Motion Sickness (which really is the best possible name such a show could have!) is a particularly special glimpse into the inner workings of their obsession with kinetic sculpture. Studio shelves full of models, maquettes and motion tests are being displayed for the first time, revealing the playful spirit of invention that has defined so many of their iconic personal projects and collaborations with brands to date.
A “perfect excuse” to reveal behind the scenes, according to the pair, the display brings all the background stuff to the fore, and, in doing so, makes a case for the magic of making the real thing. From inventive lo-fi tricks to DIY effects for motion, playful products and much more, we talk about why being open to whichever form a project might take has always led to the most joyful results and how they have managed to tread a playful line between art and design in over a decade of work.
It’s Nice That (INT):
 I’d love to jump straight into chatting about Motion Sickness – what a great name for a show of your work! Can you tell us a bit about what’s in store for this LDF display at The Conran Shop celebrating a decade of the studio?
Helen Chesner (HC):
 Our work is all about movement, we never really planned it to be, but it’s just kind of happened quite naturally. Over the past ten years, we’ve collected this big pool of references and a lot of models, machetes and tests of lots of different motion experiments that formed part of our research for different projects over the years – the studio is full of them! We thought that this exhibition at Conran would be a perfect excuse to show all of that to the public and reveal the behind the scenes in a way, as a lot of our work is hands-on and develops through the making.
This process side to the show is a bit of a nod to when we first started out – everything was quite low-fi and had this kind of relatable feel to it because we had no money or very little means to make something super complex or highly engineered. From the very beginning our practice has been about playing with the materials that we’ve had at our disposal and using all these low-fi tricks in order to create quite simple effects or motion in different ways.
Isabel Gibson (IG):
 The show will be a real mix of the old and the new! It’s also so great to be back at Conran. It’s such an iconic space for British Design and we did our first project with them, in collaboration with It’s Nice That round about ten years ago for LDF. So it’s really a full circle moment!
“Our work is all about movement, we never really planned it to be, but it’s just kind of happened quite naturally.”
Helen Chesner
INT:
 Yes! I wanted to ask you how it feels to have reached ten years of the studio? Another part of this full circle moment for us is that you were both one of our Ones to Watch all the way back in 2016, and here we are a decade later interviewing you about ten very successful years of Isabel + Helen!
IG:
 It really is full circle! I guess we just feel really proud and just so lucky to be able to do this as our job. It’s been a nice chance not just to look back at what we’ve achieved and the real kind of mix of projects we’ve worked on, but also just a reminder of the fact we still get to experiment and try new stuff all the time in our practice and that’s really exciting.
HC:
 We’ve always kept everything quite broad and taken opportunities from lots of different mediums or projects that have come our way, which has been great. Now, we’re starting to kind of narrow it down a little bit into what we really enjoy doing or what really excites us but we’ve realised how nice it’s been to have had the chance to spread our roots quite wide over the last decade. I think that’s going to inform everything that comes next.
IG:
 We have been reminiscing on all the different projects that we’ve worked on. We’ve constructed everything from really large-scale installations at Coachella right down to developing small products of our own like our egg holder that spins.
HC:
 The fact that we get to dip our hands into these different pools is really nice.

INT:
 I love that spinning egg holder!
IG:
 We’ve had the pleasure of working with such a mix of brands and clients over this time too. We’ve created a spinning shoe sculpture for Converse but then also created a series of maypole weavings for Burberry that included 16 dancers! I think we’ve always been open to whichever form a project might take, whether it’s a film or a book, an installation or a toy.
HC:
 I think over the past ten years, we’ve learned not to take ourselves too seriously as well and not be too worried about where something’s going to take you or not too cautious to just say yes to things, not knowing what the outcome might be.
INT:
 That’s a really nice little nugget from ten years of work. It’s very true. You have such a diverse portfolio of work from this past decade and like you said it’s been so multidisciplinary from products to wearable pieces like your Power Suits to huge installations. The show at Conran distils all of this into the theme of motion and a continued fascination with the mechanics of things that has touched all of your work to date in whichever direction it’s taken you in. What do you think has continued to propel this fascination over the years?
HC:
 I think we have both always been really interested in how things work and the mechanics of things, which perhaps comes from the fact that we are both quite logical, practical people. I think that’s what first drew us to work together at university too. This ongoing curiosity of how we can make something really unexpected keeps pushing us forward.
IG:
 We also really love the idea that these sculptures we make act like a form of visual therapy or a form of meditation in some way, and perhaps as a result everything’s got that kind of hypnotic feeling about it for us and others.
HC:
 There’s also so much that comes out of working as a duo. I think that our collaboration itself plays such a big part and builds on so many of our mutual interests. We were really inspired by the same artists at university – there’s three that stuck out to us that I think have propelled our work over the years. Daniel Eatock was a big inspiration for us when we started, all his work was about how your process drives the outcome or in some cases is the outcome and that idea has really fed into our work.
John Wood and Paul Harrison with their really deadpan performance art where they use really low-fi tricks in camera to create these performances, using the human body as kind of a vehicle for movement or a vehicle for transformation inspired us back then too. Then 2014 was the first time we saw Julio Le Parc’s work who’s currently on at the Tate actually and we were amazed by his really hypnotic kinetic sculptures – the way that they move and create really interesting optical effects has been a huge inspiration for us over the years.
INT:
 I can really see that parallel with Daniel Eatock and what you’re saying about your work being as much about the process as it is about the outcome. There is so much motion involved in the work’s making, not just in how the final thing moves and behaves.
IG:
 That’s why it’s been so nice developing the In Orbit painting series as well because it produces something static at the end, but we’re always thinking about how we can create as much of a feeling of movement as possible in that final piece to indicate how it’s been made.
INT:
 What was the process of going back through years of work like to curate the show? Are there any projects that really stand out to you when you look back – what pieces have you most enjoyed bringing to life?
HC:
 I think there’s a lot of personal work that definitely stands out, but there’s also been some really amazing collaborators that have pushed our work beyond what we could have imagined over the years. Working with Craig Green has been a huge inspiration and influence for us in particular. He’s been an incredible creative collaborator and he has really pushed us to experiment and create new worlds.
We’ve worked with him for five or six seasons now, with his collections as our starting point. There are so many rich visual references that feed into his work, which we can take and create something really big and sculptural and immersive from.
