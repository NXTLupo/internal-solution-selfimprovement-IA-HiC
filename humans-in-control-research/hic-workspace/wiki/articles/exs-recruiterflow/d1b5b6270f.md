# How to Audit a Dormant Recruiting Database in 6 Steps

Source: [exs_recruiterflow](https://recruiterflow.com/blog/recruiting-database-audit/)

How to Audit a Dormant Recruiting Database in 6 Steps
Most recruiting firms treat their old database records as clutter to clean up.
A more useful way to look at them is as a revenue asset you have already paid to build.
Every candidate you have sourced, screened, submitted, or placed carries context your recruiters do not have to rebuild from scratch. The same is true of past clients, hiring managers, and contacts who have gone quiet.
A recruiting database audit helps you identify which of those dormant records still have commercial value, update the ones worth keeping, and prioritize the candidates and clients most likely to generate placements.
That matters because 71% of placements come from candidates who were already in the CRM before the role opened, according to Recruiterflow’s benchmark of more than 2,100 recruiting firms in The Economics of Recruiting.
This guide gives you a six-step dormant database audit you can use to:
- Identify high-value dormant candidates
- Find lapsed clients worth re-engaging
- Spot job-change and promotion signals
- Prioritize records with the highest placement potential
- Estimate the revenue sitting inside your existing CRM
- Build a repeatable re-engagement queue
If you want the broader strategy for bringing those records back into active recruiting and business development, read our complete guide to database resurrection in recruiting.
What Is a Recruiting Database Audit?
A recruiting database audit is a structured review of dormant candidate and client records in your ATS and CRM to identify which ones still have placement or business-development potential.
That includes people such as:
- Candidates you previously submitted
- Candidates who reached interview stages
- Past placements
- Silver-medalist candidates
- Candidates sourced but never placed
- Clients you previously worked with
- Hiring managers who have gone quiet
- Contacts who may have changed companies or roles
A database audit is different from a standard data-hygiene exercise.
Data hygiene asks: What information is inaccurate, incomplete, duplicated, or safe to remove?
A database audit asks: Which records are still commercially valuable, and who should we re-engage first?
You need both.
But only one directly turns old CRM records back into pipeline.
If your goal is specifically to resurface candidates for live searches, that falls under talent rediscovery. A database audit goes one level broader because it looks at both the candidate side and the client side of your CRM.
The 6-Step Dormant Recruiting Database Audit
Work through these six steps in order.
Each stage should narrow a large, cold database into a smaller group of candidates and clients your team can realistically act on.
1. Size the Dormant Part of Your Database
Start by understanding how much of your recruiting database has actually gone cold.
Choose a dormancy threshold. For many firms, a useful starting point is 12 months without meaningful activity.
Then calculate candidates and clients separately.
For candidates, look for records with no:
- Email or call activity
- Candidate status change
- Submission
- Interview
- Placement
- Recruiter note
- Meaningful engagement
For client and contact records, look for no:
- Outreach or replies
- New jobs
- Meetings
- Deals
- Placement activity
- Business-development engagement
You are trying to answer a simple question:
What percentage of the database is sitting untouched?
For example, a firm with 100,000 candidate records may discover that 60,000 have had no meaningful activity in more than a year.
That does not mean 60,000 records should be deleted.
It means you have 60,000 records that need to be classified.
If your database structure itself needs work, our guide to building and maintaining a recruitment database covers the fundamentals.
2. Segment Records by Placement Potential
Do not treat every dormant record equally.
A candidate you submitted six months ago is more valuable than someone imported five years ago who never replied.
The same applies to clients.
Start by identifying records that previously came closest to producing revenue.
High-value dormant candidates
Prioritize candidates who were:
- Previously placed
- Submitted to a client
- Interviewed
- Shortlisted
- Finalists or silver medalists
- Actively engaged with your firm previously
These candidates already have relationship history and often have recruiter notes, salary information, preferences, feedback, and interview context stored in the CRM.
That makes them far easier to evaluate than a completely new candidate.
High-value dormant clients
Prioritize clients who:
- Previously made a placement with you
- Gave you multiple jobs
- Reached late stages in a deal
- Worked with your firm repeatedly before going quiet
- Had a key contact leave the organization
A dormant record can look cold according to the timestamp while still being extremely warm commercially.
This is the same principle behind database resurrection: historical relationship context changes the economics of re-engagement.
3. Check Whether the Records Are Still Reachable
Once you have identified the high-value segments, check whether you can actually contact them.
Review:
- Email address
- Phone number
- LinkedIn profile
- Current company
- Current title
Then group records into three buckets.
Reachable
The contact information appears current and the person can be approached immediately.
These records should move directly into your re-engagement queue.
Stale but Valuable
The record has strong historical value, but the email, phone number, title, or company appears outdated.
These records are worth enriching before outreach.
No Viable Contact Path
There is no reliable way to contact the person and not enough commercial value to justify enrichment.
These should be deprioritized or handled according to your firm’s retention policies.
The goal is not to make every record perfect.
It is to spend enrichment time where improving the record could realistically produce revenue.
4. Look for Job Changes, Promotions, and Other Change Signals
This is often the highest-value part of the audit.
People continue moving even when their CRM record stays still.
Since you last contacted someone, they may have:
- Changed employers
- Been promoted
- Become a hiring manager
- Moved into an executive role
- Joined a growing company
- Left a client and created an opening at another account
These changes create natural reasons to reconnect.
When a Candidate Changes Jobs
A job change gives you new information about:
- Their current experience
- Career trajectory
- Industry exposure
- Seniority
- Future marketability
It can also create a reason for a recruiter to reopen the relationship.
When a Client Contact Changes Jobs
This can be even more commercially valuable.
Someone who previously hired through your firm may have joined a new company where they now control hiring decisions.
Instead of being a dormant contact, they become a warm business-development opportunity.
Recruiterflow’s Job Change Alert Agent is designed around exactly this workflow: identifying when candidates and contacts move roles so recruiters can update their CRM and act on the change.
Recruiterflow also lets teams filter candidates and contacts based on when job changes were detected or accepted, making it possible to build dynamic re-engagement segments around recent movement.
5. Audit Data Completeness on the Records That Matter
This is where many database-cleanup projects go wrong.
Teams try to clean every field across every record.
That can turn an audit into a months-long data project with no immediate commercial return.
Instead, focus on the records identified as high value in the previous steps.
For candidates, verify the fields that affect whether they can be matched to a live search:
- Current title
- Current company
- Core skills
- Seniority
- Location
- Salary or compensation expectations
- Industry
- Candidate status
- Contact information
- Consent or lawful basis for contact
For client contacts, focus on:
- Current company
- Current role
- Decision-making seniority
- Previous relationship with your firm
- Last placement or engagement
- Contact information
- Industry or market
- Account owner
Think of this as revenue-weighted data hygiene.
Do not fix everything.
Fix the records most likely to pay.
This is also where recruitment automation becomes useful. Automating CRM updates and enrichment reduces the amount of manual cleanup recruiters need to do before the database becomes searchable and useful again.
6. Score and Prioritize Records for Reactivation
By this point, you should have a much smaller pool.
Now rank those records according to how likely they are to produce an outcome.
A simple scoring model can use three dimensions.
Reachability
Can you reliably contact them?
Relevance
Are they relevant to current jobs, clients, markets, or business-development priorities?
Historical Intent
How close did the relationship previously come to producing revenue?
A candidate who interviewed twice for a similar role and recently changed jobs should rank far above someone added via an old CSV import.
Likewise, a hiring manager who previously made three placements with you and just became VP of Talent at a new company should rank above a prospect your team cold-emailed once.
You can turn the output into segments such as:
- Tier 1: Act now
- Tier 2: Enrich and contact
- Tier 3: Nurture
- Tier 4: Archive or review later
The result of the audit should not be a cleaner database.
It should be a ranked re-engagement queue.
What a Recruiting Database Audit Should Surface
When the audit is finished, you should have a shortlist rather than another spreadsheet.
That shortlist might include:
- Past candidates suited to current open jobs
- Silver medalists worth resurfacing
- Previously placed candidates whose careers have progressed
- Former clients worth reopening
- Past hiring managers who moved to new companies
- Candidates or contacts
