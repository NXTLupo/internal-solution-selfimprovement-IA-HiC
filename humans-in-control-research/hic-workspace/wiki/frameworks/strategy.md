# CX Strategy Framework Specification

## Canonical Framework

**Rumelt's Kernel (Good Strategy Bad Strategy)** + **Playing to Win (Lafley/Martin)** + **OKRs (Doerr/Grove)**

This specification governs the generation, review, and validation of all CX strategy deliverables. Rumelt's Kernel provides the structural test: every strategy must contain a diagnosis, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. Playing to Win provides the choice cascade that connects aspirations to capabilities. OKRs provide the measurement discipline that translates strategic intent into accountable outcomes.

Strategy is not aspiration. It is not a list of goals. It is not a vision statement. Strategy is a coherent set of choices about where to play and how to win, grounded in an honest diagnosis of the current situation. Any deliverable that calls itself a strategy but lacks these elements is, per Rumelt, "bad strategy" and must be rejected.

---

## Rumelt's Kernel Test

Every strategy document produced under this framework MUST pass the Kernel Test. The three elements are non-negotiable:

### 1. Diagnosis of the Central Challenge

The diagnosis defines the nature of the challenge being faced. It simplifies the overwhelming complexity of reality by identifying the critical aspects of the situation. A good diagnosis does not merely list problems -- it identifies the structure of the problem, the causal mechanisms at work, and the leverage points where intervention will have the greatest effect.

The diagnosis must:
- Name the central challenge explicitly (one to three sentences)
- Distinguish symptoms from root causes
- Identify the 2-3 most critical factors that create or perpetuate the challenge
- Be grounded in evidence (quantitative data, customer research, competitive analysis)
- Resist the temptation to diagnose the problem as "we need more of everything"

Example of a weak diagnosis: "Our customer satisfaction scores are declining."
Example of a strong diagnosis: "Our customer satisfaction decline is structurally driven by channel fragmentation -- customers who interact across more than two channels experience a 40% higher effort score because context is lost at every channel transition, and our siloed technology architecture makes context sharing architecturally impossible without fundamental re-platforming."

### 2. Guiding Policy

The guiding policy is an overall approach chosen to cope with or overcome the challenge identified in the diagnosis. It is not a goal or a vision -- it is a decision about how to address the diagnosed problem. The guiding policy creates advantage by focusing energy and resources, exploiting leverage points, and making clear what the organization will and will not do.

The guiding policy must:
- Directly address the diagnosed challenge (not a different, more comfortable problem)
- Create focus by ruling out alternatives (a policy that includes everything is not a policy)
- Be specific enough to guide action but general enough to allow adaptation
- Articulate the source of advantage (why this approach will work where others might not)
- Be expressible in one to two sentences

Example of a weak guiding policy: "We will become customer-centric."
Example of a strong guiding policy: "We will eliminate context loss at channel transitions by building a unified interaction layer that maintains conversation state across all channels, prioritizing the three highest-volume transition paths first, and accepting temporary degradation in channel-specific feature depth as a trade-off."

### 3. Coherent Actions

Coherent actions are coordinated, mutually reinforcing steps that carry out the guiding policy. The word "coherent" is critical -- individual actions must work together, not merely coexist. Actions that contradict each other or compete for the same resources are a sign of absent strategy.

The coherent actions must:
- Flow logically from the guiding policy
- Be sequenced (what comes first and why)
- Reinforce each other (action A makes action B more effective)
- Include resource commitments (not just intentions)
- Define decision rights (who is authorized to act)
- Specify a timeline with milestones

## Rumelt's Four Hallmarks of BAD Strategy

Every generated strategy must be explicitly tested against these four failure modes. If any hallmark is detected, the strategy must be revised before approval.

### 1. Fluff

A restatement of the obvious combined with abstract language that creates the illusion of high-level thinking. Fluff uses buzzwords and jargon to mask the absence of actual strategic content.

Detection signals: sentences that contain "leverage," "synergy," "transform," "paradigm," "best-in-class," or "world-class" without specific, measurable meaning. Paragraphs that could be copied into any company's strategy without modification. Language that sounds impressive but conveys no information about what specifically will be done differently.

Test: Take any sentence from the strategy and ask "Could a competitor write the exact same sentence?" If yes, it is fluff.

### 2. Failure to Face the Challenge

The strategy does not define the challenge being addressed. Without a clear diagnosis, there is no basis for evaluating whether the proposed actions make sense. This hallmark often manifests as a strategy that jumps directly to goals and actions without articulating what problem those actions are solving.

Detection signals: the document begins with vision/mission statements and moves directly to initiatives. No section explicitly names the central challenge. When asked "what is the #1 problem this strategy addresses?", the answer is not obvious from reading the document.

Test: Can you state the central challenge in one sentence after reading the strategy? If not, the strategy fails to face the challenge.

### 3. Mistaking Goals for Strategy

Stating desired outcomes without articulating how they will be achieved. "We will grow revenue by 20%" is a goal, not a strategy. A strategy explains the mechanism by which the goal will be achieved -- the diagnosis of what creates the growth opportunity, the guiding policy for capturing it, and the coherent actions that execute the policy.

Detection signals: the document is structured as a list of targets (revenue, NPS, market share) without a connecting logic explaining how the organization will move from current state to target state. The word "strategy" is used interchangeably with "goal" or "objective."

Test: Remove all numeric targets from the document. Does any strategic content remain? If the document is empty without its targets, it contains goals but no strategy.

### 4. Bad Strategic Objectives

Strategic objectives that fail to address the critical challenge or that are impractical. This includes objectives that are mutually contradictory, objectives that are merely restated goals in different language, and "blue sky" objectives that ignore real-world constraints.

Detection signals: objectives that cannot all be pursued simultaneously given available resources. Objectives that, if achieved, would not actually resolve the diagnosed challenge. Objectives that assume away the hard trade-offs.

Test: Are the objectives achievable with current resources? If not, does the strategy address the resource gap? Do the objectives collectively address the diagnosed challenge? If not, the objectives are disconnected from the strategy.

## Required Sections (15)

### Section 1: Executive Summary (SCR Format)

Use the Situation-Complication-Resolution structure:
- **Situation:** The factual context (market position, customer base, competitive landscape)
- **Complication:** The challenge or tension that demands strategic response
- **Resolution:** The strategic approach being proposed (the guiding policy in summary form)

Maximum one page. Must be self-contained -- an executive who reads only this section should understand the strategic direction, the rationale, and the key commitments.

### Section 2: Vision and Mission

**Vision:** The aspirational future state the organization is working toward (3-5 year horizon). Must be specific enough to guide decisions. "Be the best" is not a vision. "Be the employer of choice for CX professionals in financial services, measured by industry-leading employee NPS and talent retention" is directional.

**Mission:** The organization's purpose and scope. Defines what the organization does, for whom, and what makes it distinctive. Must pass the "and therefore we won't" test -- if the mission does not exclude anything, it is too broad to be useful.

### Section 3: Current State Assessment (The Diagnosis)

This is Rumelt's diagnosis. The most important section of the strategy. Must include:

- **Quantitative assessment:** Current performance on key CX metrics (NPS, CSAT, CES, First Contact Resolution, Customer Lifetime Value, churn rate) with trend lines
- **Competitive position:** Where the organization stands relative to competitors and best-in-class benchmarks
- **Capability assessment:** Organizational strengths and weaknesses across people, process, technology, and data
- **Voice of customer synthesis:** Key themes from customer research, complaint analysis, and behavioral data
- **Root cause analysis:** Not just what is underperforming, but why -- the structural, systemic, and cultural factors driving current performance
- **The central challenge statement:** One to three sentences naming the core strategic problem

### Section 4: Target State

The desired future state expressed in concrete, measurable terms. Must cover: customer experience outcomes (what customers will experience differently), organizational capabilities (what the organization must be able to do), technology architecture (what platforms and systems will be in place), and operating model (how work will be organized and governed).

The target state must be achievable within the strategy's time horizon. Aspirational targets that require capabilities the organization cannot plausibly develop are not strategy -- they are wishful thinking.

### Section 5: Strategic Pillars (3-5, MECE)

Define 3 to 5 strategic pillars that organize the strategy's initiatives. Pillars must be:
- **Mutually Exclusive:** No initiative should logically fit under two pillars
- **Collectively Exhaustive:** All strategic activity should fit under one of the pillars
- **Outcome-Oriented:** Named for the outcome they achieve, not the activity they entail

For each pillar:
- **Name:** Outcome-oriented label
- **Strategic Intent:** What this pillar aims to achieve and why
- **Scope:** What falls within this pillar (and what does not)
- **Key Initiatives:** The 3-5 major initiatives within this pillar
- **Success Measures:** How you will know this pillar is succeeding
- **Investment Required:** Order-of-magnitude resource commitment

### Section 6: Initiatives with Prioritization

For each initiative, provide: Initiative Name, Owning Pillar, Problem Being Solved, Expected Outcome, Priority (using MoSCoW or RICE from the PRD framework), Dependencies, Resource Requirements, Timeline Phase, Risk Assessment.

Apply a 2x2 prioritization matrix: Strategic Impact (low/high) vs. Implementation Feasibility (low/high). Initiatives in the high-impact/high-feasibility quadrant are candidates for quick wins and early phases. High-impact/low-feasibility initiatives require de-risking before commitment.

### Section 7: Governance (Steering Committee, RACI/RAPID)

Define the governance structure:
- **Steering Committee:** Composition, charter, meeting cadence, decision rights, escalation path
- **RACI/RAPID Matrix:** For each major decision type, who Recommends, who Agrees/has veto, who Performs, who provides Input, who Decides
- **Review Cadence:** Monthly operational reviews, quarterly strategic reviews, annual strategy refresh
- **Performance Management:** How strategic KPIs are tracked, reported, and acted upon
- **Exception Process:** How to handle deviations from the strategy (scope changes, budget overruns, market shifts)

### Section 8: Measurement Framework (Perception + Interaction + Outcome)

Structure metrics across three layers:

- **Perception Metrics:** How customers feel (NPS, CSAT, brand perception, trust indices). These are lagging indicators that reflect cumulative experience quality.
- **Interaction Metrics:** How individual interactions perform (CES, First Contact Resolution, Average Handle Time, channel containment rate, task completion rate). These are the operational levers that drive perception.
- **Outcome Metrics:** Business results (Customer Lifetime Value, churn/retention rate, revenue per customer, cost to serve, share of wallet). These are the financial translation of CX performance.

For each metric: Current Baseline, 12-Month Target, 24-Month Target, Measurement Source, Reporting Cadence, Accountable Owner. Define the causal hypotheses linking the three layers (e.g., "reducing CES by 20% is expected to improve NPS by 8 points and reduce churn by 5% within 12 months").

### Section 9: Investment Case (NPV/IRR/Scenarios)

Quantify the financial case for the strategy:
- **Total Investment Required:** Broken down by category (technology, people, process change, external services)
- **Expected Returns:** Revenue impact, cost reduction, risk mitigation value
- **NPV Calculation:** Net present value using the organization's standard discount rate
- **IRR Calculation:** Internal rate of return
- **Payback Period:** When cumulative returns exceed cumulative investment
- **Scenario Analysis:** Minimum three scenarios (Conservative, Base, Optimistic) with different assumptions about adoption rates, cost estimates, and market conditions
- **Sensitivity Analysis:** Which assumptions have the greatest impact on the business case? What would have to be true for the investment to break even?

### Section 10: Implementation Roadmap (Foundation / Build / Transform)

Structure the implementation in three phases:

**Phase 1 -- Foundation (0-6 months):** Establish the prerequisites. Technology platform decisions, team formation, governance establishment, quick wins that build momentum and credibility. Focus on de-risking and creating the conditions for success.

**Phase 2 -- Build (6-18 months):** Execute the core initiatives. Deliver the capabilities defined in the strategic pillars. Iterate based on learning from Phase 1. Manage dependencies across initiatives.

**Phase 3 -- Transform (18-36 months):** Achieve the target state. Optimize based on accumulated data. Extend capabilities to edge cases and secondary use cases. Embed new ways of working into organizational culture.

Each phase must have: Entry Criteria (what must be true to start), Exit Criteria (what must be true to proceed to next phase), Key Deliverables, Resource Plan, Risk Register.

### Section 11: Risk Assessment

Comprehensive risk register covering:
- **Strategic Risks:** Market shifts, competitive disruption, technology obsolescence
- **Execution Risks:** Skill gaps, vendor dependency, integration complexity, organizational change resistance
- **Financial Risks:** Budget overruns, benefit shortfalls, exchange rate exposure (for global programs)
- **Regulatory Risks:** Compliance changes, data protection evolution, industry-specific regulation

For each risk: Description, Category, Probability (1-5), Impact (1-5), Risk Score, Mitigation Strategy, Contingency Plan, Early Warning Indicators, Owner. The top 5 risks must have fully developed response plans with triggers, actions, and owners.

### Section 12: Change Management

Address the human dimension of the strategy:
- **Stakeholder Analysis:** Map stakeholders by influence and impact; identify advocates, resistors, and fence-sitters
- **Impact Assessment:** What changes for each stakeholder group (role changes, process changes, tool changes, reporting changes)
- **Communication Plan:** Key messages per audience, channels, cadence, feedback mechanisms
- **Training Plan:** Skill development required, training modality, timeline, measurement of competency
- **Resistance Management:** Anticipated resistance points, root causes of resistance, mitigation approaches
- **Culture Change:** If the strategy requires behavioral change, define the target behaviors, the reinforcement mechanisms, and the measurement approach

Use the ADKAR model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement) or Kotter's 8-Step model as the organizing framework.

### Section 13: Communication Plan

Distinct from change management communication, this section addresses ongoing strategic communication:
- **Internal Communication:** How the strategy is communicated to the organization (town halls, newsletters, dashboards, team briefings)
- **Stakeholder Updates:** Cadence and format for steering committee updates, board updates, investor updates
- **Customer Communication:** How strategic changes are communicated to customers (where relevant)
- **Narrative:** The "strategy story" in plain language that any employee can retell. Must fit on one page. Must answer: Where are we? Where are we going? Why? How? What does it mean for me?

### Section 14: Quick Wins (90-Day Plan)

Identify 3-7 initiatives that can be delivered within 90 days of strategy approval. Quick wins must:
- Be genuinely valuable (not manufactured wins)
- Be visible to stakeholders
- Build momentum and credibility for the broader strategy
- Require minimal investment relative to their visibility
- Not create technical debt that impedes later phases

For each quick win: Description, Expected Impact, Owner, Timeline, Success Measure, Dependency on Broader Strategy (none -- quick wins must be independently deliverable).

### Section 15: Long-Term Vision (3-5 Year)

Paint the picture of the CX organization 3-5 years out. Address:
- **Customer Experience:** What will the customer experience look like? What will be possible that is not possible today?
- **Operating Model:** How will the CX organization be structured and governed?
- **Technology Landscape:** What will the technology architecture look like?
- **Talent and Culture:** What capabilities will the team have? What will the culture be?
- **Competitive Position:** Where will the organization stand relative to competitors and industry benchmarks?
- **Emerging Technology:** How will AI, automation, and other emerging technologies be integrated?

This section is explicitly aspirational and lower confidence. It provides directional guidance for Phase 3 and beyond, not a detailed plan. It should inspire and align without creating false commitments.

## Anti-Patterns (Generation Must Avoid)

### The Goal List Strategy
A document that lists desired outcomes (grow revenue, improve NPS, reduce costs) without any explanation of how those outcomes will be achieved. This is Rumelt's "mistaking goals for strategy." Detection: the document can be summarized as "we will achieve [metrics] by trying harder." Fix: add diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions.

### The Buzzword Strategy
Relies on jargon and consulting language to create the appearance of strategic thinking. "Leverage our core competencies to drive synergistic value creation across the omnichannel ecosystem." Detection: replace every buzzword with "thing" -- if the sentence still makes as much sense, it is fluff. Fix: rewrite in plain language that a new employee could understand.

### The Everything Strategy
Attempts to address every possible opportunity simultaneously. No trade-offs, no focus, no sequencing. This is the opposite of strategy -- it is the refusal to make choices. Detection: more than 5 strategic pillars, more than 20 initiatives, no explicit "we will NOT do" section. Fix: cut pillars to 3-5, cut initiatives to 3-5 per pillar, add a "Strategic Non-Goals" section.

### The Copycat Strategy
Replicates a competitor's strategy without adapting to the organization's unique context, capabilities, and constraints. Detection: the strategy could be applied to any company in the industry without modification. Fix: ground every element in the organization's specific diagnosis.

### The Consultant Shelf Strategy
A beautifully formatted document that is never operationalized. Contains no governance model, no measurement framework, no quick wins, no change management plan. Detection: the document lacks sections 7, 8, 12, 13, and 14. Fix: a strategy is only as good as its execution plan -- include all operational sections.

### The Conflict-Avoidant Strategy
Avoids naming the hard trade-offs. Promises to simultaneously reduce cost, increase quality, expand scope, and accelerate timelines. Detection: no tension between any objectives, no explicit trade-offs stated. Fix: for every strategic choice, state what is being traded away.

## Quality Gates

A strategy must pass ALL of the following gates before adoption:

| Gate | Criterion | Validation Method |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| QG-S1 | Rumelt's Kernel present: Diagnosis + Guiding Policy + Coherent Actions | Kernel identification test |
| QG-S2 | Diagnosis names the central challenge in 1-3 sentences | Challenge statement extraction |
| QG-S3 | Guiding policy is distinct from goals | Policy-goal differentiation test |
| QG-S4 | Coherent actions are sequenced and mutually reinforcing | Action coherence review |
| QG-S5 | None of Rumelt's 4 bad strategy hallmarks detected | Bad strategy hallmark scan |
| QG-S6 | Strategic pillars are MECE (3-5 maximum) | MECE validation |
| QG-S7 | All 15 sections present and substantive | Section checklist review |
| QG-S8 | Measurement framework spans perception, interaction, and outcome layers | Metrics layer audit |
| QG-S9 | Investment case includes NPV and minimum 3 scenarios | Financial model review |
| QG-S10 | Quick wins identified (3-7 items, deliverable in 90 days) | Quick win feasibility review |
| QG-S11 | Change management plan addresses stakeholders and resistance | Change plan completeness |
| QG-S12 | No anti-patterns detected | Anti-pattern checklist scan |
| QG-S13 | Trade-offs explicitly stated for each strategic choice | Trade-off audit |
| QG-S14 | Strategy is expressible in a one-page narrative | Narrative compression test |

## Generation Instructions

When generating a CX strategy:

1. **Start with the diagnosis.** Do not begin with vision, mission, or goals. Begin with an honest, evidence-based assessment of the current situation. Name the central challenge. If you cannot name it, you do not understand the situation well enough to strategize.

2. **Apply Rumelt's Kernel rigorously.** After drafting, extract the diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent actions. If any element is missing or weak, revise before proceeding. The Kernel is the backbone of the strategy -- everything else hangs from it.

3. **Test for bad strategy.** Run each of Rumelt's four hallmarks as an explicit check. Read every sentence and ask: Is this fluff? Does this face the challenge? Is this a goal masquerading as strategy? Is this objective practical and relevant?

4. **Use the Playing to Win cascade.** Work through Lafley and Martin's five questions: What is our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win? What capabilities must we have? What management systems are required? Each question constrains the next.

5. **Make trade-offs visible.** For every strategic choice, state what is being traded away. If nothing is being traded, the choice is not strategic -- it is obvious, and obvious choices do not need a strategy document.

6. **Quantify the investment case.** Strategy without resource commitment is fantasy. Define what the strategy costs, what it returns, and under what conditions. Include scenarios because single-point forecasts are unreliable.

7. **Plan for execution from the start.** Include governance, measurement, change management, and quick wins. A strategy that cannot be operationalized is academic exercise, not strategic leadership.

8. **Write for action.** Every section should answer "so what?" and "what next?" If a section provides analysis without implications, it is incomplete. If it provides recommendations without evidence, it is ungrounded.

9. **Validate with the one-page test.** If the strategy cannot be summarized on one page without losing its essential logic, it is either too complex (simplify) or lacks a clear throughline (find the Kernel).

10. **Address CX-specific dimensions.** Every CX strategy must address: the customer journey (where are the moments of truth?), the operating model (how is CX work organized?), the technology architecture (what platforms enable the experience?), and the talent model (who delivers the experience and how are they developed?).
