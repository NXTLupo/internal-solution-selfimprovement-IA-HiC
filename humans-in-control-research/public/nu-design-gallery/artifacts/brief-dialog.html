Brief Dialog · Pressure-test

The case for an AI co-op readiness tool survives, but only if it triages advisor attention instead of generating resumes.

Partner · Strategist

The Partner
Senior, advancement
Open
We are proposing the university build an AI tool to get first-years co-op ready. I have signed off on advising software three times in my career and watched two of them become shelfware. Walk me through why this is not the third.
The Strategist
Analyst, this engagement
Evidence
Because the failure first-years describe is not a missing tool, it is a missing signal. On the student boards the same line keeps surfacing: "I applied to 60 co-ops and heard back from 4." Reddit r/NEU They are not under-applying; they are flying blind into a keyword filter. Two more threads on r/cscareerquestions describe identical filtering before a human reads the resume. The pattern is convergent across student-side sources.
The Partner
Senior, advancement
Challenge
Convergent on the student side, sure. But every vendor pitches the same dashboard, and the coordinators ignore it. What does the advisor actually do differently on a Tuesday? If the answer is "log into one more system," this dies at adoption, the way the last donor portal did.
The Strategist
Analyst, this engagement
Evidence
Agreed, which is why the design is a triage queue, not a dashboard. The coordinators told us directly: "I find out a student didn't apply when the cycle's already closed." Coordinator retrospective The tool's job is to surface the stalled students on Friday, in the channel they already use, not to add a login. Northeastern's own first-year readiness roadmap already analyzes resumes against declared majors; the triage layer rides on that, it does not replace the advisor.
The Partner
Senior, advancement
Concession
Fair. A queue that tells a coordinator which twelve students to chase before the deadline is a different product than a dashboard, and it is one I can defend to the provost. I will give you that.
The Strategist
Analyst, this engagement
Pivot
Then let me name the real risk, because it is not adoption. It is trust in the score. If the tool ranks students and a coordinator cannot interrogate why a student is flagged, they revert to gut, and the queue becomes noise. The same distrust shows up wherever opaque scoring meets a professional who is accountable for the outcome. The score has to be explainable at the row level or it will not be used.
The Partner
Senior, advancement
Synthesis
So the recommendation holds, with a condition I can live with: build the triage queue, ride the existing roadmap, and make every flag explainable to the coordinator who acts on it. The test is simple. One cycle, one cohort: does first-cycle placement velocity move for the students the queue surfaced, and can the coordinator say why each one was on it? If yes, we scale. If the score is a black box, we stop.

Weakest claim surfaced

That coordinators will act on an AI-generated ranking they cannot interrogate. Adoption depends on row-level explainability, not model accuracy.

Final stance

Qualified

The recommendation survives, narrowed to a triage queue with explainable flags, tested on one cohort for one cycle before scaling.
